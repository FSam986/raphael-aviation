Nautical Air Miles vs Ground Miles — Headwind

fig_03_01Original redraw

Air distance and ground distance are different things. Nautical air miles (NAM) are the distance flown through the air mass: TAS × time. Nautical ground miles (NGM) are the distance covered over the ground: ground speed × time. With a headwind the air mass is moving back along track, so the aircraft must fly more air miles than ground miles to cover the route. Fuel burn depends on air miles, which is why headwinds cost fuel. Convert with NAM = NGM × TAS ÷ GS.

Nautical Air Miles vs Ground Miles — Tailwind

fig_03_02Original redraw

With a tailwind the air mass carries the aircraft forward, so ground speed exceeds TAS and the aircraft covers the ground distance in fewer air miles: NAM is less than NGM. The same formula applies, NAM = NGM × TAS ÷ GS. Example: 600 NGM at TAS 240 kt with a 40 kt tailwind (GS 280 kt) is 600 × 240 ÷ 280 = 514 NAM. Fuel tables and specific range figures are given in air miles, so always convert the ground distance first.

Point of Safe Return — Deriving the Formula

fig_14_02Original redraw

Where the formula comes from. Let E be the safe endurance, T the time out to the PSR, O the ground speed out and H the ground speed home. The time left for the return is E − T. By definition the distance out equals the distance home, so T × O = (E − T) × H. Multiply out and collect T: T × O + T × H = E × H, hence T = E × H ÷ (O + H). Multiply T by O for the distance. Because O + H is largest when there is no wind component, any wind reduces the distance to the PSR.

PSR on the Navigation Computer

fig_14_03Original redraw

One setting solves it. The formula T = E × H ÷ (O + H) is a simple proportion: T ÷ E = H ÷ (O + H). On the circular slide rule of the navigation computer, set the safe endurance E on the inner scale against (O + H) on the outer scale. Then, opposite H on the outer scale, read T on the inner scale — the time to the PSR. Example: E = 300 min, O = 200 kt, H = 250 kt. Set 300 against 450; against 250 read T = 167 min. Distance to PSR = 167 min at 200 kt = 556 nm.

Balance Arms — Datum, Arm, Moment & CG

fig_mb_2_2Original redraw

Every load has a position as well as a mass. The datum is a fixed reference plane chosen by the manufacturer, from which all positions are measured. The distance from the datum to a load is its balance arm: positive aft of the datum, negative forward. Moment = mass × arm. Add all the masses and all the moments, and the centre of gravity is total moment ÷ total mass. In the example: total mass 1 200 kg, total moment 3 120 000 kg·mm, so the CG is 2 600 mm aft of the datum. It must lie between the forward and aft limits at all times.

Centre-of-Gravity Envelope

fig_mb_2_10Original redraw

The CG envelope shows every permitted combination of mass and CG position. The left edge is the forward limit and the right edge the aft limit; the top is the maximum take-off mass, with the maximum landing and maximum zero-fuel masses below it. The forward limit often moves aft at high mass, because a heavy, nose-heavy aircraft needs more elevator than is available at low speed. Plot the take-off, landing and zero-fuel points: all three must lie inside, because the CG moves as fuel burns. A CG too far forward means heavy controls, higher stall speed and more drag; too far aft means reduced stability and difficult stall recovery.

Repositioning Mass — CG Shift

fig_mb_2_12Original redraw

Moving a load inside the aircraft does not change the total mass, only the total moment. The CG moves in the same direction as the load, by an amount given by a simple ratio: mass moved ÷ total mass = change of CG ÷ distance moved. So change of CG = m × d ÷ M. Example: moving 100 kg aft by 4 m in a 5 000 kg aircraft shifts the CG 100 × 4 ÷ 5 000 = 0.08 m aft. The same ratio, rearranged, tells you how much mass to move to bring an out-of-limits CG back inside.

Adding or Removing Mass — CG Shift

fig_mb_2_15Original redraw

Adding or removing a load changes both the total mass and the total moment. The ratio becomes: mass added (or removed) ÷ new total mass = change of CG ÷ distance from the old CG to the load. Adding mass moves the CG towards the load; removing mass moves it away. Example: adding 200 kg at a point 3 m aft of the CG of a 4 800 kg aircraft gives a new mass of 5 000 kg and moves the CG 200 × 3 ÷ 5 000 = 0.12 m aft. Always use the new total mass and measure the distance from the old CG.

Stabiliser Trim Setting vs CG Position (% MAC)

fig_mb_2_19Original redraw

Large aircraft express the CG as a percentage of the mean aerodynamic chord: % MAC = (CG − leading edge of MAC) ÷ MAC length × 100. The take-off stabiliser trim setting is then read from a graph or table against % MAC. A forward CG (low % MAC) makes the aircraft nose-heavy, so it needs more nose-up trim; an aft CG needs less. Setting the trim for the actual CG gives the correct stick force at rotation — a wrong setting can make the aircraft rotate too early or refuse to rotate. Example: CG 12.0 m, LEMAC 11.0 m, MAC 4.0 m gives 25% MAC.

Declared Distances for Performance

fig_pf_2_4Original redraw

Performance calculations start from the declared distances. The take-off run required must fit within TORA; the take-off distance required (to 35 ft, or 50 ft for small aeroplanes) within TODA, which includes the clearway; and the accelerate-stop distance required within ASDA, which includes the stopway. The landing distance required must fit within LDA. A clearway may not be longer than half the TORA. If no stopway or clearway is declared, TORA, TODA and ASDA are all equal to the runway length.

Forces During the Take-Off Run

fig_pf_2_11Original redraw

Acceleration depends on the excess of thrust over retarding forces. Thrust acts forward. Against it act aerodynamic drag, which grows with the square of speed, and wheel (rolling) friction, which equals the friction coefficient times the load on the wheels — weight minus lift — and so falls as lift builds. Net accelerating force = thrust − drag − friction; acceleration = that force ÷ mass. A heavier aircraft accelerates more slowly and needs a higher lift-off speed, so take-off distance rises roughly with the square of mass. An upslope adds a weight component rearwards; soft or contaminated surfaces raise the friction.

Runway Contamination & Stopping Distance

fig_pf_6_7Original redraw

Stopping distance depends on tyre-to-runway friction. A wet runway (surface soaked, no significant standing water) gives less braking than a dry one, so the regulations add a margin: the landing distance required on a wet runway is 1.15 × the dry figure. A contaminated runway — standing water, slush, snow or ice over more than 25% of the area in use — reduces braking far more and adds drag to the take-off roll. Dynamic hydroplaning starts when the tyre rides up on a film of water: about 9 × √(tyre pressure in psi) knots for a rotating tyre and 7.7 × √P for a non-rotating one. Use reported braking action (good, medium, poor) to pick the correct data.

Wind Effect on Descent Angle & Range

fig_pf_4_14Original redraw

Wind changes the path over the ground, not the path through the air. At a given airspeed the aircraft descends through the air mass at the same angle and the same rate of descent whatever the wind, so the time to descend is unchanged. But the air mass itself is moving: a headwind reduces ground speed, making the descent steeper over the ground and the range shorter; a tailwind makes it shallower and the range longer. For maximum glide range, fly a little faster than best-glide speed into a headwind and a little slower with a tailwind.

Thrust Required & Thrust Available — Minimum & Maximum Speed

fig_pf_5_5Original redraw

Level flight is possible only where thrust available equals or exceeds drag. The drag (thrust required) curve is the sum of induced drag, which falls with speed, and parasite drag, which rises with speed squared; it is lowest at VMD, the minimum-drag speed. The thrust available from a jet is roughly constant with speed. Where the two lines cross are the minimum and maximum level-flight speeds at that weight and altitude (the low-speed limit is often set by the stall first). The gap between the lines is excess thrust, which is available for climb or acceleration; it is greatest near VMD, which is why a jet’s best angle of climb is close to VMD.

CAS, TAS & Mach Number with Altitude

fig_pf_5_10Original redraw

The three speeds drift apart as altitude changes. Climbing at constant CAS, falling air density makes TAS increase, and because the speed of sound falls with temperature, Mach number increases even faster. Above the crossover altitude the climb continues at constant Mach: now TAS decreases (the speed of sound is falling) and CAS decreases faster still. Above the tropopause temperature is constant, so at constant Mach the TAS stays constant. Memory aid for the order of the lines left to right: E-C-T-M (EAS, CAS, TAS, Mach) — whichever is held constant is vertical, those to its right increase in the climb and those to its left decrease.

Balanced Field Length — The Ideal V1

fig_pf_14_1Original redraw

V1 is the decision speed. If an engine fails below V1 the take-off is rejected; at or above V1 it is continued. Choosing a higher V1 means the aircraft is going faster when the stop begins, so the accelerate-stop distance increases; but it has less far to accelerate on the remaining engines, so the continued take-off distance (engine out) decreases. The V1 at which the two distances are equal gives the balanced field length — the shortest runway from which the take-off can be made at that mass. V1 may never be less than VMCG nor greater than VR or VMBE (maximum brake energy speed).
