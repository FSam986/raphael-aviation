GPWS Inputs & Outputs

fig_34_01Original redraw

What the GPWS uses. The ground proximity warning computer takes radio altitude (height above terrain and its rate of change), barometric altitude and vertical speed from the air data computer, glideslope deviation from the ILS, landing gear and flap position, and — for an enhanced system (EGPWS/TAWS) — GPS/FMS position compared with a worldwide terrain database. Outputs are voice alerts (“SINK RATE”, “PULL UP”), the GPWS/glideslope lights, and the terrain display on the ND.

GPWS Control Panel

fig_34_02Original redraw

Few controls — on purpose. The GPWS panel has the warning lights — red PULL UP (GND PROX) and amber BELOW G/S — a TEST switch, and guarded inhibit switches: FLAP INHIBIT and GEAR INHIBIT (to prevent nuisance “too low” alerts when deliberately landing with non-standard flap or in an emergency) and TERRAIN INHIBIT (to silence the database alerts where the database is known to be wrong, e.g. at an airport missing from it). The basic modes cannot be switched off.

Mode 1 — Excessive Sink Rate

fig_34_03Original redraw

Descending too fast for the height. Mode 1 compares barometric descent rate with radio altitude (roughly from 2 500 ft down to 50 ft). Entering the outer boundary gives the caution “SINK RATE”; continuing into the inner boundary gives the warning “PULL UP” (or “WHOOP WHOOP PULL UP”). It does not depend on gear or flap position. Response: reduce the rate of descent; for a pull-up warning, apply the full escape manoeuvre.

Mode 2 — Excessive Terrain Closure Rate

fig_34_04Original redraw

The ground is coming up fast. Mode 2 watches the rate at which radio altitude decreases — the closure rate with rising terrain, even if the aircraft is level. It first calls “TERRAIN TERRAIN” and then “PULL UP”. Mode 2A (flaps not in landing position) is active from about 2 450 ft radio altitude down; Mode 2B (landing flap) has a reduced envelope to avoid nuisance alerts during approaches over uneven ground. Because it sees terrain only directly below, it can give little warning of a steep cliff — the gap EGPWS look-ahead closes.

Mode 3 — Altitude Loss after Take-off

fig_34_05Original redraw

Sinking when you should be climbing. Mode 3 is active after take-off or go-around until the aircraft reaches a set radio altitude (about 1 500 ft on modern systems). If the aircraft loses barometric altitude — for example from a flap retraction or thrust reduction too early, or windshear — it calls “DON’T SINK”. The alert threshold is proportional to height: the higher the aircraft, the more height it may lose before the alert.

Mode 4 — Unsafe Terrain Clearance

fig_34_06Original redraw

Close to the ground in the wrong configuration. Mode 4 alerts when the aircraft is low but not configured to land. 4A (gear up): “TOO LOW GEAR” below about 500 ft radio altitude at low speed. 4B (gear down, flaps not in landing position): “TOO LOW FLAPS” below about 245 ft. At higher speeds the boundaries rise (to about 1 000 ft) and the alert becomes “TOO LOW TERRAIN”. The flap and gear inhibit switches suppress the configuration alerts when needed.

Mode 5 — Below Glideslope

fig_34_08Original redraw

Too far below the glide path. Mode 5 operates on an ILS approach below about 1 000 ft radio altitude with the gear down. If the aircraft goes more than about 1.3 dots below the glideslope, a soft “GLIDESLOPE” alert sounds, getting louder and faster as the deviation increases; below about 300 ft with more than 2 dots the alert becomes loud. The amber BELOW G/S light illuminates, and the crew can cancel the alert by pressing it (e.g. when deliberately flying a visual approach below the slope).

Mode 6 — Bank Angle & Altitude Callouts

fig_34_10Original redraw

Two kinds of advisory. Mode 6 provides altitude callouts on approach from the radio altitude — e.g. “FIVE HUNDRED”, “MINIMUMS” at the decision height, and “FIFTY, FORTY, THIRTY, TWENTY, TEN” to touchdown — chosen by the operator. It also gives “BANK ANGLE, BANK ANGLE” if the bank exceeds a limit that reduces with height (e.g. about 35° above 150 ft, about 10° at 5 ft) to protect against wingtip strikes and loss of control.

Mode 7 — Windshear

fig_34_11Original redraw

Reactive windshear warning. Mode 7 uses air data, inertial accelerations and angle of attack to detect a sudden loss of performance — typically a microburst, where the aircraft first meets an increasing headwind, then a downdraught and a tailwind. A decreasing-performance shear triggers the red WINDSHEAR light with a siren and “WINDSHEAR, WINDSHEAR, WINDSHEAR”: apply full thrust and fly the escape pitch attitude, following the flight director. (Predictive windshear from the weather radar warns before entering it.)

EGPWS / TAWS Terrain Display

fig_34_12Original redraw

Seeing the terrain. Enhanced GPWS (TAWS) compares the aircraft’s position and altitude with its terrain database and paints the ground on the navigation display relative to the aircraft’s altitude: green dots for terrain well below (more than ~500–2 000 ft below), amber dots within about 500 ft below to 2 000 ft above, red dots for terrain more than 2 000 ft above. When a look-ahead alert fires, the threatening terrain turns solid amber (“CAUTION TERRAIN”) or solid red (“TERRAIN, TERRAIN, PULL UP”) and the display pops up automatically.

EGPWS Look-Ahead Alert Envelope

fig_34_13Original redraw

Looking ahead along the flight path. EGPWS projects envelopes ahead and below the aircraft, based on ground speed, track and flight path. If terrain from the database penetrates the outer caution envelope — about 40–60 seconds ahead — it calls “CAUTION TERRAIN”; if it penetrates the inner warning envelope — about 20–30 seconds ahead — “TERRAIN, TERRAIN, PULL UP”. This gives far earlier warning of a steep mountain than the basic modes, which only see the radio altitude directly below.

EGPWS Terrain Clearance Floor

fig_34_14Original redraw

Protecting the approach. Using its database of runways, EGPWS builds a terrain clearance floor around each runway: a surface rising from close to the runway end to about 400 ft above it at around 4 nm, then higher further out. If the aircraft descends below the floor — for example landing short or a premature descent in the landing configuration, which basic mode 4 would not catch — it calls “TOO LOW TERRAIN”. A related “runway field clearance floor” uses runway elevation.

TCAS Protection Volumes (TA / RA)

fig_35_01Original redraw

Volumes defined by time, not distance. TCAS computes for each intruder the time to closest approach (tau = range ÷ closure rate) and the predicted vertical separation. When an intruder will reach closest approach in about 35–48 s (depending on altitude), it becomes a traffic advisory (TA); at about 20–35 s a resolution advisory (RA) with a vertical manoeuvre. Small fixed distance thresholds (DMOD) protect against slow-closing traffic. Because the volumes are times, they extend further ahead towards fast head-on traffic.

TCAS Directional Antenna Coverage

fig_35_02Original redraw

How TCAS finds traffic. TCAS interrogates the transponders of nearby aircraft (1 030 MHz) and listens for their replies (1 090 MHz). The time to reply gives range; the reply’s encoded altitude (Mode C or S) gives relative height; a directional antenna on top of the fuselage (and usually another underneath) gives bearing. TCAS cannot see aircraft without a working transponder, and needs altitude reporting to issue RAs against them. Bearing is the least accurate element — never manoeuvre laterally on the TCAS display.

TCAS System Architecture & Displays

fig_35_03Original redraw

The parts of TCAS II. The TCAS computer drives the top and bottom antennas, tracks intruders, and computes advisories. It works with the Mode S transponder, which also exchanges coordination messages with another TCAS aircraft so that their RAs are complementary (one climbs, the other descends). Outputs: the traffic display (on the ND or a dedicated display), the RA guidance on the VSI or PFD, and aural messages (“TRAFFIC, TRAFFIC”, “CLIMB, CLIMB”). The TCAS/transponder control panel selects STBY, TA ONLY or TA/RA.

TCAS TA / RA Timing Zones

fig_35_04Original redraw

The sequence of an encounter. As an intruder closes, TCAS first shows it as other or proximate traffic. Roughly 40 s before the closest point of approach (CPA), a TA: “TRAFFIC, TRAFFIC” — look for the traffic and prepare, but do not manoeuvre. Roughly 25 s before CPA, an RA: follow it promptly and smoothly within 5 seconds (2.5 s for an increase or reversal). When separation is assured: “CLEAR OF CONFLICT” — return to the clearance and inform ATC. (Exact times vary with altitude.)

TCAS RA on the VSI

fig_35_05Original redraw

Flying the RA on a TCAS VSI. On an RA, the VSI shows a red arc over the vertical speeds to be avoided and a green arc (“fly-to”) over the target range. Here the RA is “CLIMB, CLIMB”: red from level down through all descent rates, green from 1 500 to 2 000 ft/min climb. The pilot adjusts pitch to put the needle in the green, disconnecting the autopilot. On a PFD the same information appears as red and green bands on the vertical speed scale or as a pitch “avoid” region on the attitude display.

TCAS Traffic Display Explained

fig_35_06Original redraw

Reading the traffic display. Own aircraft is at the centre, with a ring of dots at 2 nm (or 3 nm). Each intruder shows a symbol, a data tag of relative altitude in hundreds of feet — + above, − below — and an arrow if it is climbing or descending at 500 ft/min or more. Example: “−05↑” = 500 ft below and climbing. The display shows position for situational awareness only; the bearing is not accurate enough for lateral avoidance.

TCAS Traffic Symbology

fig_35_07Original redraw

Four symbols. Red filled square: the intruder causing a resolution advisory. Amber filled circle: a traffic advisory intruder. Filled white (or cyan) diamond: proximate traffic — within about 6 nm and ±1 200 ft, but not a threat. Hollow diamond: other traffic in range. Traffic not reporting altitude shows no data tag and cannot generate RAs.

TCAS Traffic on the Navigation Display

fig_35_08Original redraw

Traffic in context. On EFIS aircraft TCAS traffic is overlaid on the navigation display together with the route, so the crew sees threats relative to their planned path. A TA or RA makes the traffic appear automatically even if the TFC option is off, with a “TRAFFIC” annunciation in amber (TA) or red (RA). In expanded modes, traffic behind the aircraft is not visible — the off-scale or no-bearing messages (e.g. “TA 2.5 +03”) show it.

Flight Data Recorder System

fig_36_01Original redraw

Recording the flight. Sensors throughout the aircraft send data to the flight data acquisition unit (FDAU), which formats it and passes it to the flight data recorder (FDR) — a crash-protected unit in the tail, painted orange, with an underwater locator beacon (37.5 kHz). It records at least the last 25 hours. An EVENT button on the flight deck lets the crew mark a point in the data for later analysis. The recorder starts automatically before the aircraft moves under its own power.

Digital FDR Architecture

fig_36_02Original redraw

Inside a digital FDR system. The acquisition unit samples analogue signals (e.g. synchro positions), discrete on/off states (e.g. gear, warnings) and digital bus data (ARINC 429), converts them, and multiplexes them into a serial data stream at a fixed rate (e.g. 64–1 024 words per second). The recorder writes the stream to solid-state memory in a crash-survivable memory unit designed to withstand impact (3 400 g), fire (1 100°C for 30 min) and deep-sea pressure. Older recorders used magnetic tape.

Cockpit Voice Recorder — Control Panel & Location

fig_37_01Original redraw

Recording what is heard. The CVR records the crew’s radio and intercom audio and a cockpit area microphone on separate channels, retaining at least the last 2 hours (modern requirements up to 25 hours), overwriting the oldest. The flight-deck control panel carries the area microphone, a TEST button (a meter or light confirms all channels), a headset jack for monitoring, and an ERASE button that works only on the ground with the parking brake set. The recorder itself is in the tail, crash-protected, with a locator beacon.

CVR Control Unit Face

fig_37_02Original redraw

The face of the CVR panel. Pressing TEST runs a tone through each channel; the monitor meter needle moves into the green band (or a light illuminates) if the recorder is working, and the tone can be heard on a headset plugged into the jack. ERASE, held for a couple of seconds, bulk-erases the recording — inhibited in flight and unless the parking brake is set. The area microphone grille picks up all cockpit sounds, including alerts and engine noise, which help investigators.

Engine Parameters Measured

fig_38_01Original redraw

Where the engine is measured. On a turbofan the main indications are N1 (fan/low-pressure spool speed) and N2 (high-pressure spool speed), EPR (engine pressure ratio, exhaust total pressure ÷ inlet total pressure) on some engines, EGT (exhaust gas temperature, measured between or after the turbines), fuel flow, oil pressure, temperature and quantity, and vibration. Thrust is set on N1 or EPR; EGT is the main limit on the hot section.

Engine Pressure Ratio (EPR)

fig_38_02Original redraw

Thrust as a pressure ratio. EPR is the ratio of total pressure in the exhaust (Pt7, after the turbine) to total pressure at the inlet (Pt2). Because thrust depends closely on it, EPR is used on some engines as the thrust-setting parameter. An inlet probe and a ring of exhaust probes feed a pressure-ratio transmitter (or the engine computer) that drives the gauge. Typical take-off EPR is about 1.3–1.6. An iced or blocked inlet probe reads too low a Pt2 and gives a falsely high EPR — so less thrust than indicated.

EGT Thermocouple (Chromel–Alumel)

fig_38_11Original redraw

Measuring the hottest gas. A thermocouple is two dissimilar metals — chromel (positive) and alumel (negative) for EGT — joined at a hot junction in the gas stream. A small voltage (about 41 µV per °C) appears that depends on the difference in temperature between the hot junction and the cold (reference) junction at the indicator, which is compensated automatically. Several probes round the jet pipe are wired in parallel to give an average. It needs no power supply, and leads must be of the correct material and resistance.

Manifold Pressure Gauge

fig_38_16Original redraw

Power on a piston engine. The manifold pressure gauge measures the absolute pressure in the induction manifold, in inches of mercury, using an evacuated aneroid capsule (like an altimeter) in a case connected to the manifold. With a constant-speed propeller, manifold pressure and RPM together set the power. With the engine stopped it reads the ambient pressure (about 30 inHg at sea level); at idle it reads low (strong suction); a normally-aspirated engine cannot exceed ambient.

Bourdon-Tube Pressure Gauge

fig_38_17Original redraw

Measuring high pressures. A Bourdon tube is a curved tube of oval (flattened) section, sealed at the free end. Pressure inside tries to make the section round, which straightens the curve; the free end moves, and a quadrant and pinion magnify the motion to turn the pointer. It is robust and suits higher pressures — oil, hydraulic, oxygen — while capsules suit low pressures (manifold, altimeter). Measured relative to the case (gauge pressure).

Torquemeter

fig_38_03Original redraw

Measuring shaft power on a turboprop. In a hydraulic torquemeter, the reduction gear’s helical teeth produce an axial thrust proportional to the torque. This thrust is balanced by oil pressure in a set of pistons; a valve meters oil until the pressure balances the thrust, so the oil pressure is proportional to torque and is shown on the gauge (psi, or % torque). Electronic systems measure the twist of the shaft by the phase shift between two toothed wheels. Shaft power = torque × RPM.

Phonic-Wheel Speed Probe

fig_38_04Original redraw

Speed from pulses. A phonic (toothed) wheel on the engine shaft passes a probe containing a permanent magnet and a coil. Each tooth changes the magnetic flux through the coil and induces a pulse; the frequency of the pulses is proportional to shaft speed. The electronics count them and display percentage RPM. There is no mechanical connection or drive, so it is ideal for N1 on fans. Multiple coils give independent signals for the indicator and engine control.

Drag-Cup (Eddy-Current) Tachometer

fig_38_05Original redraw

Turning speed into a steady pointer. A permanent magnet, driven at engine speed (by a flexible shaft, or by a synchronous motor in a tacho-generator system), rotates inside a light aluminium drag cup. The moving field induces eddy currents in the cup, which produce a drag torque trying to turn the cup with the magnet. A hairspring resists, so the cup and pointer turn through an angle proportional to speed. A fixed iron ring outside concentrates the field.

Tacho-Generator System

fig_38_06Original redraw

Remote indication of RPM. A small three-phase AC generator (permanent-magnet rotor) on the engine gearbox produces a voltage whose frequency is proportional to engine speed. Three wires carry it to the indicator, where it drives a synchronous motor that turns at exactly the generator’s speed. The motor spins the magnet of a drag-cup mechanism, which moves the pointer. Because only frequency matters, voltage drop in the wiring does not affect accuracy.

Percent-RPM Gauge (N1 / N2)

fig_38_07Original redraw

Spool speeds in percent. Twin-spool engines have two gauges: N1 (low-pressure spool, the fan) and N2 (high-pressure spool). Each reads in % of a reference RPM; the maximum may exceed 100% (e.g. 104%). A digital counter gives the exact value; a red line marks the limit and an amber band a time-limited range. On start, N2 rises first (driven by the starter), then N1 as the fan follows the gas flow.

N1 Fan-Speed Magnetic Sensor

fig_38_09Original redraw

Counting fan blades. On many turbofans the N1 signal comes from a magnetic speed sensor (a variable-reluctance probe) mounted on the fan case, either sensing the passing fan blade tips or a toothed wheel on the low-pressure shaft. Each blade or tooth induces a pulse; frequency ∝ N1. The sensor has several independent coils, supplying separate signals to the engine control (FADEC), the indicating system and the vibration monitor.

Propeller Synchroscope

fig_38_10Original redraw

Matching propeller speeds. On twins a synchroscope compares the RPM of the slave engine with the master. A small disc or pointer rotates at a speed proportional to the difference, in the direction of the faster engine: if it turns clockwise (towards FAST) the slave is faster; anticlockwise, slower. When the disc stops, the propellers are synchronised, eliminating the annoying “beat” in cabin noise. Many aircraft have automatic synchronisers; the synchroscope then confirms them.

Engine Vibration Monitoring

fig_38_18Original redraw

Spotting imbalance early. Accelerometers (piezoelectric) or velocity pickups on the engine casing measure vibration. A signal conditioner filters the signal at the frequencies of the N1 and N2 shafts, so the vibration from each spool can be separated, and displays it in arbitrary units (e.g. 0–5) or inches/second. A high or rising reading warns of fan damage (e.g. bird strike, ice), bearing wear or imbalance; an amber advisory appears above a set level.

Float-Type Fuel Gauge

fig_38_19Original redraw

The simplest fuel gauge. A float on a pivoted arm rides on the fuel surface; the arm moves the wiper of a variable resistor (potentiometer), whose resistance indicates the fuel level. A ratiometer gauge reads the ratio of currents, so supply voltage changes do not matter. It measures volume, not mass, and is affected by aircraft attitude and acceleration (fuel sloshing), so it reads accurately only in level, unaccelerated flight. Typical in light aircraft.

Capacitance Fuel Quantity & Flow System

fig_38_20Original redraw

Fuel by mass. Large aircraft use capacitance probes: concentric tubes standing in the tank. Fuel has a dielectric constant of about 2.1, air 1, so the capacitance rises with fuel height. Because the dielectric constant varies with fuel density, the system indicates mass (kg or lb), which is what matters for range and performance. Several probes across the tank average out attitude effects; a compensator probe (always immersed) corrects for fuel properties. A separate flowmeter in each engine feed gives mass fuel flow and fuel used.

Capacitance Fuel Probe Construction

fig_38_21Original redraw

A capacitor made of tubes. Each probe is two concentric tubes insulated from each other, open so fuel can enter the gap. The tubes are the plates; whatever fills the gap is the dielectric. Fuel (dielectric constant ≈ 2.1) raises the capacitance compared with air (1.0), so the total capacitance increases linearly with the height of fuel. Probes are shaped (“characterised”) to match the tank cross-section so the output is proportional to quantity. No moving parts and no spark risk with the tiny AC currents used.

Fuel Flow Transmitter (Mass Flowmeter)

fig_38_23Original redraw

Measuring mass flow. In a common mass flowmeter, an impeller driven at constant speed by a motor gives the fuel a swirl (angular momentum). The fuel then passes through a turbine restrained by a calibrated spring; removing the swirl deflects the turbine through an angle proportional to the mass flow (because angular momentum depends on mass). A transmitter sends the angle to the indicator, which shows kg/h or lb/h, and the system integrates flow to show fuel used.

EICAS Architecture

fig_39_01Original redraw

Engine Indicating and Crew Alerting System. Engine and system sensors feed two EICAS computers (either can run both displays). They drive the upper display (primary engine parameters and crew alert messages) and the lower display (secondary engine parameters, or system status and maintenance pages), selected on the display select panel. The computers also drive the master warning and caution lights and aural alerts. If a display fails, a compacted format puts everything on the remaining one.

EICAS Primary & Secondary Engine Displays

fig_39_02Original redraw

Primary and secondary parameters. The upper display carries the primary parameters needed to set and monitor thrust — N1 (or EPR) and EGT — as round dials with digital readouts, plus the crew alert messages on the right. The lower display carries the secondary parameters: N2, fuel flow, oil pressure, temperature and quantity, and vibration, shown as smaller dials or vertical tapes. Secondary parameters can be hidden in normal flight and pop up automatically if one goes out of limits.

EICAS Status Page

fig_39_04Original redraw

Is the aircraft fit to dispatch? The STATUS page, selected on the display select panel, lists status messages — equipment faults that affect dispatch under the minimum equipment list but need no immediate crew action — together with key system values such as hydraulic quantities, oxygen pressure, and APU data. It is checked before departure and after a failure in flight to understand the remaining capabilities.

EICAS Alert Message Levels

fig_39_05Original redraw

Priority by colour and position. EICAS messages are ranked: Warnings (level A, red) need immediate action — the red MASTER WARNING lights and a distinctive aural (bell, siren, voice). Cautions (level B, amber) need prompt awareness and possible action — amber MASTER CAUTION and a beeper. Advisories (level C, amber, indented one space) need crew awareness only — no master light or aural. Memo messages (white) remind the crew of selected states (e.g. PARK BRAKE SET). Higher levels always appear at the top of the list.

Complete EICAS Engine Display

fig_39_06Original redraw

Everything on one page. A typical upper EICAS shows, across the top, the thrust reference mode (TO, CLB, CRZ, CON, GA) with the limit value and the TAT. Below are the EPR (or N1) dials with the command and reference (target) bugs, then N1 and EGT, each with a digital readout that turns amber or red at a limit. The crew alert messages occupy the right-hand side, in priority order. Gear and flap positions may also appear here.

Standby Engine Indicator

fig_39_07Original redraw

Engine data if the main screens fail. A standby engine indicator — a small self-contained LCD unit — shows the essential engine parameters, typically N1, N2 and EGT for each engine as digital readouts. It is powered from the standby (battery) bus and takes its data from the engine sensors directly or through the engine control, so the crew can still set and monitor thrust after a total EICAS failure.

ECAM Architecture

fig_39_08Original redraw

Airbus’s Electronic Centralised Aircraft Monitor. Sensor data go to two system data acquisition concentrators (SDAC) — mainly for cautions and system pages — and to two flight warning computers (FWC), which generate warnings, aural alerts and the checklist actions. Three display management computers (DMC) draw the engine/warning display (E/WD, upper) and the system display (SD, lower). The ECAM control panel selects system pages and clears messages.

ECAM Status Page

fig_39_10Original redraw

What is left after a failure. After the crew has worked through the ECAM actions, the STATUS page appears on the system display. On the left: limitations (e.g. MAX SPEED 320 kt) and approach procedures or information (in blue/cyan); on the right: the list of INOP SYS — inoperative systems. A STS reminder remains on the E/WD while status messages exist. It is reviewed before the approach to plan the landing.

ECAM Control Panel

fig_39_13Original redraw

Driving the ECAM. The system page keys (ENG, BLEED, PRESS, ELEC, HYD, FUEL, APU, COND, DOOR, WHEEL, F/CTL) call up each synoptic on the lower display. CLR (lit when there is something to clear) acknowledges the current message and moves to the next; RCL recalls cautions that were cleared or inhibited; STS shows the status page; ALL scrolls through every page; EMER CANC cancels an aural warning or a caution for the rest of the flight; T.O CONFIG tests the take-off configuration warnings.
