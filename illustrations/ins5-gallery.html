Pointer & Counter Display

fig_01_04Original redraw

The best of both. A pointer moving round a scale shows trend and rate of change at a glance, while a digital counter gives an exact value that cannot be misread. Combining them — a counter in a window, plus a pointer for the last digits or the trend — gives fast, accurate reading. It is used on airspeed, altimeter and engine displays; the counter-pointer altimeter replaced the three-pointer type for exactly this reason.

Revolution Counter — Percentage RPM

fig_01_05Original redraw

Turbine speeds are shown as a percentage. Rather than 10 000 or 30 000 rpm, the gauge reads % RPM of a reference speed (100%), so engines of different sizes read alike and limits are easy to remember. The main pointer covers 0–100% with an expanded scale near the top; a small vernier dial gives each 1% in tenths. A red radial marks the maximum. N1 and N2 gauges on two-spool engines use this format.

The 'Basic Six' Layout

fig_01_07Original redraw

The standard six. Light aircraft panels group the six primary flight instruments in two rows: airspeed, attitude, altimeter on top; turn coordinator, heading indicator, vertical speed below. The pitot-static instruments (ASI, altimeter, VSI) and the gyroscopic ones (attitude, heading, turn) are each powered differently, so a single failure affects only part of the panel. The standard arrangement lets a pilot fly any aircraft with the same scan.

The 'Basic T' Layout

fig_01_08Original redraw

Four instruments in a T. The attitude indicator sits top centre with airspeed to its left, altitude to its right and heading directly below: the basic T, required by certification standards so that the most important information is always in the same place. The same arrangement survives on glass cockpits — speed tape left, attitude centre, altitude tape right, heading below.

Light-Twin Instrument Panel

fig_01_09Original redraw

A typical light twin. The flight instruments in the basic-six arrangement are directly in front of the pilot, radios and navigation displays in the centre stack, and the engine instruments in pairs — left engine on the left, right on the right — so a difference between engines stands out. Paired gauges are often combined in one case with two needles (manifold pressure, RPM, fuel flow). Switches and circuit breakers run along the bottom of the panel.

Analogue Altimeter

fig_01_10Original redraw

The counter-pointer altimeter. A single long pointer makes one revolution per 1 000 ft, read against a scale of 0–9 (hundreds of feet). A drum counter shows thousands (here 12 thousand), so the reading is 12 350 ft and cannot be misread by 10 000 ft like a three-pointer instrument. A striped flag may appear below 10 000 ft. The subscale window shows the pressure setting in hPa, changed with the knob.

Combined Digital & Analogue Display

fig_01_11Original redraw

Electronic gauges keep the pointer. Glass engine displays draw a circular arc with a moving pointer — giving instant trend information — together with a digital readout of the exact value. The arc carries the same coloured bands as an analogue gauge, and the digits and pointer change colour (amber, red) when a limit is exceeded, sometimes with a box round the digits. The best of analogue and digital in one symbol.

Traditional Analogue Flight Deck

fig_01_12Original redraw

The “steam gauge” flight deck. Before glass cockpits, each pilot had a full set of electromechanical instruments — attitude director, HSI, airspeed, altimeter, VSI, RMI — and the centre panel carried dozens of engine gauges, with more on a flight engineer’s panel. It worked, but scanning so many dials was demanding, each instrument could fail individually, and there was little integration of information. Many of these instruments’ principles survive as the logic behind the electronic displays.

Modern Glass Flight Deck

fig_01_13Original redraw

Large screens replace the dials. A modern airliner flight deck has a few large multi-function displays: each pilot has a primary flight display (attitude, speed, altitude, heading, flight modes) and a navigation display (map, route, weather, traffic); the centre screens show engine indications and crew alerts (EICAS/ECAM) and system synoptics. Information can be moved between screens if one fails, and standby instruments remain for total display failure.

Electronic Panel — Light Aircraft

fig_01_14Original redraw

Glass in light aircraft. Integrated systems put a PFD in front of the pilot — attitude with speed and altitude tapes, heading, and an HSI — and an MFD beside it showing a moving map, navigation and engine indications. The attitude and heading come from an AHRS (solid-state attitude and heading reference) and the air data from an air data computer. A battery-backed standby attitude, airspeed and altimeter (often one electronic unit) is required in case the main displays fail.

Colour Operating Ranges on Gauges

fig_01_15Original redraw

Gauge markings follow a standard code. A green arc is the normal operating range; a yellow (amber) arc a caution or take-off/limited-time range; a red radial line a limit that must not be exceeded (minimum or maximum). Airspeed indicators add a white arc (flap operating range) and, on light twins, a blue radial (best single-engine rate of climb, VYSE) and a red radial (minimum control speed, VMC). A slip mark on the glass shows if the glass has rotated relative to the case.

Bimetallic Strip

fig_03_01Original redraw

Two metals that expand differently. A bimetallic strip is two metals bonded together — typically Invar (which barely expands) and brass (which expands a lot). When heated, the brass grows more than the Invar and the strip bends towards the Invar side; when cooled, the other way. The movement is proportional to temperature, so it can drive a pointer directly (an OAT gauge) or open contacts (a thermostat or circuit breaker).

Bimetallic-Helix Thermometer

fig_03_02Original redraw

Making the strip turn a pointer. Winding the bimetallic strip into a helix (coil) converts its bending into rotation: as temperature rises, the helix winds or unwinds, turning a spindle and pointer directly over the dial — no linkage, no power. One end is fixed in the probe that projects into the airflow. Simple and reliable, it is the classic direct-reading outside air temperature gauge on light aircraft.

Direct-Reading OAT Gauge

fig_03_03Original redraw

The light-aircraft OAT gauge. The bimetallic probe is mounted through the windscreen (or a side panel) so the sensing element is in the outside airflow and the dial faces the pilot. It reads ram air temperature, slightly above the true static air temperature because of the airflow — negligible at light-aircraft speeds. It is needed for density altitude, true airspeed and icing judgements; it can be affected by sun heating the probe on the ground.

Total Air Temperature (TAT) Probe

fig_03_04Original redraw

Measuring temperature at high speed. A TAT probe brings a sample of air almost to rest around a platinum resistance element, so it measures total (stagnation) air temperature — static temperature plus the full ram rise. The air path turns sharply: heavier water drops and dust carry straight on and out, so they do not wet the sensor. The probe body is electrically de-iced, and the airflow design keeps the heater’s warmth away from the element. Recovery factor is close to 1.

TAT Probe Installation

fig_03_05Original redraw

Where the probe goes. The TAT probe is mounted on the forward fuselage, standing out clear of the boundary layer so it samples undisturbed air. Its resistance element is wired to the air data computer, which uses TAT with Mach number to compute static air temperature (SAT) and true airspeed. A probe heater prevents icing; on the ground with no airflow, the heated probe reads high unless it is aspirated.

Aspirated TAT Probe

fig_03_06Original redraw

Reading correctly on the ground. With no airflow, a heated TAT probe on the ground reads much too high — heat from the de-icing heater and sun soak the element. An aspirated probe has a small jet of bleed air blown through an ejector at its base, which draws outside air through the probe past the sensor even when stationary, giving a valid temperature for take-off performance calculations.

Ram Rise — SAT, RAT & TAT

fig_03_07Original redraw

Three temperatures. Static air temperature (SAT) is the true temperature of the undisturbed air — what forecasts and ISA use. Moving air brought to rest heats up by compression: the ram rise, which grows with the square of the TAS (about 1°C at 100 kt, 9°C at 300 kt, 25°C at 500 kt). A probe that recovers all of it reads total air temperature (TAT); a practical probe recovers a fraction, the recovery factor (K), and reads RAT = SAT + K × ram rise.

Temperature-Rise Correction on the Navigation Computer

fig_03_08Original redraw

Getting SAT from an indicated temperature. On the navigation computer’s temperature-rise scale, enter the TAS and read the ram rise to subtract from the indicated (ram) air temperature, allowing for the recovery factor quoted for the probe. Example: indicated −20°C at 400 kt TAS with a ram rise of about 16°C gives SAT about −36°C. Because TAS itself depends on temperature, the solution is refined by iteration — or done automatically by the air data computer.

Kelvin & Celsius Scales

fig_03_09Original redraw

Absolute temperature. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero (−273°C), so K = °C + 273. A 1 K step equals a 1°C step. Kelvin must be used whenever temperatures appear in ratios — the gas laws, density, the speed of sound (proportional to √T) and TAT/SAT calculations. ISA sea level is +15°C = 288 K; at the tropopause, −56.5°C = 216.5 K.

ISA Temperature vs Altitude

fig_03_10Original redraw

The standard atmosphere temperature profile. ISA starts at +15°C at mean sea level and falls at 1.98°C per 1 000 ft (6.5°C per km) up to the tropopause at 36 090 ft (11 km), where it is −56.5°C. Above that it stays constant to 65 600 ft (20 km). Instruments are calibrated to ISA, and the difference between actual and ISA temperature (ISA deviation) is used in performance and altimeter-error calculations.

ASI Colour Code & V-Speeds

fig_04_02Original redraw

Reading the ASI markings (light aircraft, IAS). White arc: flap operating range, from VS0 (stall, landing configuration) to VFE (maximum flap extended). Green arc: normal operating range, from VS1 (stall, clean) to VNO (maximum structural cruising speed). Yellow arc: caution range, VNO to VNE — smooth air only. Red line: VNE, never exceed. Twins add a red radial at VMC and a blue radial at VYSE.

Simple Altimeter Mechanism

fig_05_02Original redraw

An aneroid barometer marked in feet. A partially evacuated capsule sits in a sealed case connected to the static source. As the aircraft climbs, static pressure falls and the capsule expands; a leaf spring stops it collapsing and sets its rate. The movement is magnified by a linkage and gears to a pointer calibrated in feet to the ISA pressure–height relationship. A simple altimeter has one pointer and no pressure-setting facility, or only a coarse one.

Sensitive Altimeter Mechanism

fig_05_03Original redraw

More capsules, more precision. The sensitive altimeter has a stack of two or three evacuated capsules for greater movement, a bimetallic compensator for temperature effects on the mechanism, jewelled bearings and a precise multiplying gear train driving the pointers. A subscale knob rotates the whole capsule-and-dial system relative to the case so the pilot can set any datum pressure (QNH, QFE or 1013) in the window. It reads to about ±30 ft near sea level.

Three-Pointer Altimeter & Subscale

fig_05_04Original redraw

Reading the three-pointer altimeter. The long pointer shows hundreds of feet (one turn = 1 000 ft), the short pointer thousands (one turn = 10 000 ft) and the small pointer, often with a triangular tip, tens of thousands. Here: 13 460 ft. The subscale window shows the setting in hPa. Because the tens-of-thousands pointer is small and easily hidden, this design has caused 10 000 ft misreadings; it is being replaced by counter-pointer and electronic displays.

Altitude Tape on an EFIS Display

fig_05_06Original redraw

Reading the electronic altimeter. Altitude is shown as a moving vertical tape with the current value in a box (often with a rolling digit). The selected altitude from the autopilot panel is shown above the tape and as a bug on it; a vertical-speed scale may sit alongside. Below the tape is the barometric setting (QNH, or STD for 1013), which flashes if not reset at the transition. The radio altitude and minimums are shown nearby.

Servo-Assisted Altimeter

fig_05_07Original redraw

Removing friction errors. In a servo altimeter the capsule does not drive the pointer directly. Its tiny movement shifts an I-bar relative to an E-bar transformer; any misalignment produces an error signal that is amplified and drives a servo motor. The motor turns the pointer and counter and, through a cam, moves the E-bar until the signal is zero again. The capsule therefore moves almost no load, giving high accuracy and fast response; on power failure a warning flag appears.

Pressure Error — Flying from High to Low

fig_05_08Original redraw

“High to low, look out below.” An altimeter shows height above the pressure set on its subscale. Flying towards an area of lower pressure without resetting, the aircraft follows a constant-pressure surface that slopes down: the altimeter keeps reading the same but the aircraft is getting lower, and the instrument over-reads. About 30 ft per hPa: a 10 hPa drop means about 300 ft lower than indicated. The same applies to flying into colder air (“high to low temperature”). Regular QNH updates remove the error.

Blocked Static Source — Altimeter Freezes

fig_05_09Original redraw

Effects of a blocked static source. If ice or debris blocks the static vent, the pressure trapped in the instruments no longer changes. The altimeter freezes at the altitude where the blockage occurred; the VSI reads zero; the ASI becomes a reverse altimeter — in a climb the trapped static is higher than the real static, so it under-reads; in a descent it over-reads. Remedy: select the alternate static source, or break the VSI glass in an emergency. Cross-check with GPS altitude.

VSI Instrument Face

fig_06_04Original redraw

Reading the VSI. Zero is at the 9 o’clock position; the pointer moves up for a climb and down for a descent, scaled in hundreds (or thousands) of feet per minute. The scale is usually non-linear — expanded near zero for precise level-flight and approach work, compressed at high rates. A conventional VSI lags by several seconds, so it shows trend; the pilot sets pitch with the attitude indicator and uses the VSI to confirm.

Instantaneous VSI — Accelerometer Dashpots

fig_06_05Original redraw

Removing the lag. The IVSI adds an accelerometer unit: two small cylinders (dashpots) with weighted pistons held by springs, in the line to the capsule. When the aircraft starts to climb, the pistons lag under inertia and immediately change the pressure in the capsule, so the pointer moves at once; by the time the pistons settle, the normal metered pressure difference has built up and takes over. Turns can produce a brief false climb indication as the pistons respond to g.

CAS, TAS & Mach Number with Speed

fig_07_03Original redraw

Why high-altitude aircraft fly Mach. Climbing at constant CAS, TAS rises (lower density) and Mach rises faster still (the speed of sound falls with temperature). At high altitude the limiting factor becomes Mach number — the effects of compressibility — rather than dynamic pressure, so the crew changes from flying IAS/CAS to flying Mach at the crossover altitude, and the limits change from VMO to MMO. Mach = TAS ÷ local speed of sound (LSS = 38.94 √T kt, T in kelvin).

Combined Airspeed / Mach Indicator

fig_07_06Original redraw

One instrument, two limits. The combined ASI/Machmeter shows IAS on the dial and Mach in a window, and carries a striped “barber’s pole” pointer that shows the current maximum operating speed. At low altitude it shows VMO (a constant IAS); higher up, the barber’s pole moves down the scale as it tracks MMO, because a constant Mach number means a falling IAS with altitude. The crew keeps the pointer below the barber’s pole; exceeding it sounds the overspeed warning (clacker).

Machmeter

fig_07_07Original redraw

Mach number from two capsules. Mach = TAS ÷ LSS, and the instrument computes it as a ratio of pressures: an airspeed capsule fed with pitot (expanding with dynamic pressure) and an altitude capsule (evacuated, expanding as static falls) both act on a ratio arm, so the pointer shows (pitot − static) ÷ static — which depends only on Mach. Temperature does not enter, so the machmeter needs no temperature correction. It suffers position error and, like the ASI, errors from blockages.

Air Data System — Inputs & Outputs

fig_08_01Original redraw

One computer, many users. The air data computer (ADC) takes pitot and static pressure, total air temperature and sometimes angle of attack, applies corrections (position error, compressibility), and computes altitude, vertical speed, IAS/CAS, Mach, TAS, SAT and overspeed warnings. Its outputs drive the displays, the flight management system, the autopilot and autothrottle, the transponder (altitude reporting), the GPWS, the pressurisation and the flight data recorder — replacing many separate pneumatic instruments.

Air Data Computer — Servo Mechanism

fig_08_02Original redraw

How an analogue ADC computes. Each channel uses a pressure transducer (a capsule with an E-and-I pickoff, or a solid-state sensor), an amplifier and a servo motor that drives an output shaft until the pickoff signal is nulled. Cams on the shaft shape the output to match the required function (e.g. pressure to altitude), and synchros or digital encoders on the shaft send the result to the instruments and systems. Modern digital ADCs use solid-state sensors and software, but the principle — measure, compute, output — is the same.

Magnetising a Bar by Stroking

fig_09_03Original redraw

The simplest way to make a magnet. Stroking a steel bar repeatedly from one end to the other with one pole of a magnet, always in the same direction, gradually aligns the bar’s domains. The end where each stroke finishes acquires the opposite polarity to the stroking pole: stroking with a north pole leaves a south pole at the finishing end. Hard steel keeps its magnetism (a permanent magnet); soft iron loses it.

A Magnet as Aligned Molecular Magnets

fig_09_05Original redraw

Breaking a magnet makes more magnets. Cut a bar magnet in half and each half has its own north and south pole; cut again and the same happens. This is because magnetism comes from countless tiny aligned domains (molecular magnets) throughout the material. In the middle of the bar the poles of neighbouring domains cancel; only at the ends are free poles left — so it is impossible to isolate a single magnetic pole.

The Earth as a Tilted Bar Magnet

fig_09_06Original redraw

Why a compass points north. The Earth’s field is roughly that of a huge bar magnet inside it, tilted about 11° from the spin axis. Because a compass needle’s north-seeking end is attracted to it, the pole near the geographic North Pole is magnetically a south pole — the “blue” pole — even though it is called the north magnetic pole. Lines of force leave the southern hemisphere, run north over the surface and enter near the north magnetic pole.

Variation — True vs Magnetic Meridian

fig_09_07Original redraw

Variation is the angle, at any place, between the true meridian (towards the geographic pole) and the magnetic meridian (the direction a freely suspended needle settles). It is east if magnetic north lies east of true north and west if it lies west, and is shown on charts by isogonals. To convert: true = magnetic + easterly variation, or − westerly. “Variation east, magnetic least; variation west, magnetic best.”

Magnetic Dip — H, Z & T Components

fig_09_08Original redraw

The field is not horizontal. Except at the magnetic equator, the Earth’s total field T slopes down into the ground at an angle — the dip (inclination). It resolves into a horizontal component H, which aligns the compass with the magnetic meridian, and a vertical component Z, which tilts the needle down and causes compass errors. tan(dip) = Z ÷ H. As H weakens towards the magnetic poles, the compass becomes sluggish and unreliable; within about 1 000 nm of the poles it is not usable.

Dip Varies with Magnetic Latitude

fig_09_09Original redraw

Dip goes from 0° to 90°. At the magnetic equator (aclinic line) the field is horizontal: dip is 0°, H is at its maximum and Z is zero — the compass works best. Moving towards either magnetic pole the field tilts more steeply; at the poles it is vertical: dip 90°, H zero, and a compass has no directive force at all. Lines joining places of equal dip are isoclinals. In the northern hemisphere the north-seeking end dips; in the southern, the south-seeking end.

Forces on the Pendulous Magnet

fig_10_02Original redraw

Keeping the magnet level. To stop the vertical component Z pulling the magnets down (dip), the magnet system is hung below its pivot — pendulous. Its weight W acts through the centre of gravity, displaced slightly from the pivot towards the equator, and balances Z, leaving only a small residual dip of a few degrees. But because the CG is now displaced from the pivot, any acceleration or turn makes the inertia of the magnet act through the CG and swing it — the origin of acceleration and turning errors.

Direct-Reading Compass — Magnet & Card

fig_10_06Original redraw

Inside the E2B-type compass. Two or more magnets are fixed to a card (marked in degrees) and a float that reduces the weight on the jewelled pivot. The assembly sits in a bowl of liquid (a non-freezing fluid) that damps oscillations, with an expansion chamber to allow for temperature changes. The pilot reads the heading against the lubber line, fixed to the bowl and aligned with the aircraft’s longitudinal axis. Deviation-compensating magnets are adjusted during a compass swing.

The Spinning Gyro Rotor

fig_11_01Original redraw

Rigidity in space. A spinning rotor resists any change in the direction of its spin axis. That rigidity increases with the rotor’s mass — especially mass concentrated at the rim (moment of inertia) — and with its speed of rotation. Instrument gyros use a heavy brass or steel rotor spun at 10 000–25 000 rpm. The second property, precession, is what happens when a force does manage to act on it.

Single-Gimbal (Rate) Gyro

fig_11_14Original redraw

Measuring a rate. A rate gyro has only one gimbal, so it is free to move about just one axis, and that movement is restrained by springs. When the aircraft turns about the measured axis, the gyro is forced to turn with it; precession tilts the gimbal against the springs until the spring force balances the precessing force — and that happens at a deflection proportional to the rate of turn. It is the heart of the turn indicator and of yaw dampers and stabilisation systems.

DGI Apparent Wander from Earth Rotation

fig_12_08Original redraw

Why a perfect DGI still drifts. The gyro holds its direction fixed in space, but the Earth turns beneath it at 15° per hour. At the pole a DGI therefore appears to wander by 15°/h; at the equator, by zero; in between, by 15 × sin(latitude) °/h. It decreases the reading in the northern hemisphere and increases it in the southern. Instrument makers fit a latitude nut to cancel the drift for one latitude, and pilots realign the DGI with the compass every 10–15 minutes.

Air-Driven Horizon — Vane Erection Reaction

fig_13_07Original redraw

How the erection force works. Air leaving the gyro housing escapes through four slots, each half-covered by a pendulous vane. Upright, the four jets balance. If the gyro tilts, gravity keeps the vanes hanging vertically, so one slot opens fully and the opposite one closes. The unbalanced jet produces a reaction force on the housing; because of precession that force acts 90° on, moving the spin axis back to the vertical. In a sustained acceleration or turn the vanes swing falsely, causing the classic pitch and bank errors of the air-driven horizon.

Rate Gyro — Precession Against the Scale

fig_14_02Original redraw

From turn to pointer. The turn indicator’s gyro spins about the aircraft’s lateral axis in a gimbal free to tilt about the longitudinal axis. When the aircraft yaws (turns), the gyro is forced round; precession makes it tilt about the longitudinal axis, against a calibrated spring. A linkage turns the tilt into pointer movement, so the pointer shows the rate of turn. At the rate-one mark the aircraft turns at 3° per second — 360° in 2 minutes.

Level Flight — Lift Balances Weight

fig_14_03Original redraw

The starting point for the balance ball. In straight and level, unaccelerated flight, lift acts straight up and equals weight acting straight down. There is no sideways force, so a ball in a curved tube — the slip indicator — rests in the centre under gravity alone.

The Balance Ball (Slip Indicator)

fig_14_04Original redraw

A pendulum in a tube. The slip indicator is a curved glass tube filled with a damping liquid (kerosene-type) containing a steel or agate ball. Gravity and any sideways acceleration act on the ball; it settles where the resultant force points. Two vertical reference wires mark the centre. The ball is centred whenever the resultant of gravity and centrifugal force acts straight down through the floor of the aircraft — balanced (coordinated) flight.

Forces in a Banked Turn

fig_14_05Original redraw

Banking provides the turning force. In a turn, lift is tilted with the wings. Its vertical component must still equal weight, so total lift — and the load factor — must increase (1.15 g at 30° bank, 2 g at 60°). Its horizontal component provides the centripetal force that pulls the aircraft round the turn. Inside the aircraft, the occupants and the ball feel an equal and opposite centrifugal reaction. In a balanced turn the resultant of weight and centrifugal force acts straight down through the floor.

Ball Centred in a Balanced Turn

fig_14_06Original redraw

Coordinated flight. When the angle of bank is correct for the rate of turn, the resultant of weight and centrifugal reaction acts exactly perpendicular to the cabin floor. The occupants are pressed straight down into their seats and the ball sits in the centre. This is a balanced, coordinated turn: no slip or skid, minimum drag.

Ball Off-Centre — Slip & Skid

fig_14_07Original redraw

Step on the ball. In a slip the bank is too steep for the rate of turn: the aircraft slides inward and the ball moves to the inside of the turn. In a skid the rate of turn is too high for the bank (too much rudder): centrifugal force throws the ball to the outside. In either case, apply rudder on the side the ball has moved to — “step on the ball” — to centre it (or adjust bank).

Turn & Slip Indicator Face

fig_14_09Original redraw

Reading the turn and slip indicator. The needle (pivoted at the bottom) shows rate of turn: deflected to one doghouse mark it indicates a rate-one turn of 3°/s (a 2-minute turn). It shows rate only — not bank. The ball below shows balance (slip or skid). Instruments marked “2 MIN” are calibrated for rate one; “4 MIN” (on some fast aircraft) for half-rate. The gyro is usually electrically driven, so it serves as a backup to a vacuum-driven attitude indicator.

Turn Coordinator Face

fig_15_01Original redraw

Rate of turn and rate of roll. The turn coordinator’s gyro is canted upwards about 30°, so it senses roll as well as yaw: it responds as soon as the aircraft starts to roll into a turn, giving an earlier indication. The display is a miniature aircraft that banks to the L or R mark for a rate-one turn. Despite its look it does not show bank angle or pitch — it is still a rate instrument (“NO PITCH INFORMATION” is often printed on it). The ball shows balance as usual.

Deviation — Magnetic vs Compass Heading

fig_16_01Original redraw

Deviation is the angle between magnetic north and compass north — the direction the compass actually points, deflected by the aircraft’s own magnetism (steel parts, electrical currents). It is east (positive) if compass north is east of magnetic north, west (negative) if west, and it changes with heading. Residual deviation after compensation is recorded on the deviation card beside the compass. Magnetic = compass + easterly deviation (− westerly).

Hard-Iron Magnetism — Components

fig_16_02Original redraw

Hard iron — steel parts that hold permanent magnetism — behaves like a magnet fixed in the aircraft. Its field at the compass is resolved into three components: P along the fore-and-aft axis, Q athwartships and R vertical. P causes coefficient B deviation, greatest on east and west headings; Q causes coefficient C, greatest on north and south. They are removed by adjusting the compensating magnets in the compass during a compass swing. Soft iron (temporarily magnetised by the Earth’s field) causes deviations that change with latitude and is harder to compensate.

Flux-Valve Signal vs Heading

fig_17_04Original redraw

The flux valve outputs a heading. The flux valve has three legs 120° apart, each picking up the component of the Earth’s horizontal field along it. As heading changes, the signal from each leg varies like a sine wave, the three 120° out of phase — exactly like a three-phase synchro transmitter. The combination of the three signals defines the direction of magnetic north relative to the aircraft, which the receiver synchro reproduces as a heading.

Flux Valve to Receiver Synchro

fig_17_10Original redraw

Reproducing the field direction. The flux valve’s three pick-off coils are wired to the three stator windings of a synchro in the remote compass system. The currents recreate, inside the synchro, a field in the same direction as the Earth’s field relative to the aircraft. The synchro rotor, driven by a servo through the slaving amplifier, is turned until it is aligned (null), and its position — combined with the directional gyro — drives the heading card.

Remote-Indicating Compass Display (HSI)

fig_17_11Original redraw

The output of the remote compass. The slaved heading drives the rotating compass card of an RMI or HSI, read against the lubber line at the top. A heading bug, set by the pilot, feeds the autopilot heading mode. A small annunciator (dot and cross, or + / −) shows the slaving signal oscillating gently when the system is synchronised. A slaved / free (DG) switch lets the pilot disconnect the flux valve and use the gyro alone, for example in polar regions.
