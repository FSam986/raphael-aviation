Structural Loads — the Five Types

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Structural loads — every airframe member must resist five basic loads: tension pulling it apart, compression crushing it, shear sliding one layer past another, bending (which is tension on one face and compression on the other at once), and torsion twisting it. A real structure, like a wing, carries all of these together, which is why it is built as a box that resists bending and torsion efficiently.

Semi-Monocoque Fuselage

fig_01_12Original redraw

Semi-monocoque — the modern fuselage. A pure monocoque carries all loads in its skin alone; the semi-monocoque adds an internal framework so the skin can be thin. Frames (formers) give the cross-section its shape and hoop strength, stringers and longerons run lengthwise to carry bending and stop the skin buckling, and the stressed skin itself carries the shear and torsion loads. The result is light, strong, and damage-tolerant.

Fail-Safe & Damage-Tolerant Design

fig_01_05Original redraw

Fatigue philosophies — three ways to keep a structure safe for its life. A safe-life part is simply retired after a conservative number of cycles. A fail-safe structure has multiple load paths, so if one member cracks the others carry the load until the damage is found. A damage-tolerant structure is designed so any crack grows slowly and is arrested at a stiffener, guaranteed to be detected at a scheduled inspection before it becomes critical.

Fatigue & the S–N Curve

fig_01_06Original redraw

Fatigue — repeated loads well below the static strength can still fail a part by growing a tiny crack cycle by cycle. The S–N curve plots the stress amplitude against the number of cycles a specimen survives: the higher the stress, the fewer cycles it lasts. Many materials have a fatigue (endurance) limit — a stress below which the part lasts indefinitely. Stress concentrations (holes, sharp corners, corrosion) start fatigue cracks early.

Wing Spar Sections

fig_01_23Original redraw

Spar sections — the spar is the wing's main spanwise beam, resisting the bending that lift imposes. Its cross-section puts most material in the top and bottom flanges (booms), which take the tension and compression of bending, joined by a thin web that carries the shear. The I-section, box and built-up forms all follow this rule — material far from the neutral axis, where it does the most good per unit weight.

Wing Torsion Box

fig_01_24Original redraw

Torsion box — the structural heart of a wing. The front and rear spars, the ribs and the upper and lower skins together form a closed box that runs out along the span. A closed box is enormously stiff in torsion, so it resists the twisting that aerodynamic loads and control deflections impose, while the spars take the bending. Fuel is usually carried inside this sealed box.

The Empennage

fig_01_26Original redraw

Empennage — the tail assembly that gives the aircraft stability and pitch/yaw control. The fixed fin (vertical stabiliser) gives directional (weathercock) stability and carries the rudder for yaw; the fixed tailplane (horizontal stabiliser) gives longitudinal stability and carries the elevator for pitch. Each is a small wing structure in its own right, with spars, ribs and skin.

Oxidation — Oxide Growth with Time

fig_01_28Original redraw

Oxidation — the commonest corrosion: oxygen combines with the metal surface to form an oxide. The oxide thickness grows quickly at first, then levels off, because on many metals (notably aluminium) the oxide forms a tight, adherent protective film that seals the surface and slows further attack. On iron/steel the oxide (rust) is porous and flakes away, so oxidation continues — which is why ferrous parts need an applied protective finish.

Hydraulic Principle (Pascal)

fig_02_03Original redraw

Pascal's principle — pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished throughout it. In the U-tube the two cylinders share one continuous body of fluid, so the pressure under piston A equals the pressure under piston B. Because force = pressure × area, the larger piston B produces a proportionally larger force — the hydraulic force multiplier behind brakes, flaps and landing gear. The large piston moves less in the same ratio, so work is conserved.

Basic Hydraulic System

fig_02_05Original redraw

Basic hydraulic system — the pump draws fluid from the reservoir and delivers it under pressure; a relief valve protects the system by returning fluid to the reservoir if pressure gets too high. The selector directs pressure to one side of an actuator (jack) to move a load — gear, flaps, brakes — while the other side returns fluid to the reservoir. Filters, accumulators and non-return valves complete a real system.

Light-Aircraft Braking System

fig_02_04Original redraw

Brakes — pressing the top of the rudder pedal (the toe brake) pushes a piston in the master cylinder, raising the pressure of the hydraulic fluid in the brake line. That pressure is carried to the wheel, where a caliper clamps friction pads onto a disc that rotates with the wheel, converting the aircraft's energy into heat. Each main wheel has its own independent circuit, so differential braking can steer on the ground.

Open-Centre Hydraulic System

fig_02_06Original redraw

Open-centre system — the selectors are connected in series, and when they are all in neutral their open centres let the pump's output flow straight back to the reservoir at low pressure, so the pump is unloaded whenever nothing is being operated. Selecting a service blocks that free path and diverts pressure to the actuator. It is simple and light, but only one service can be worked at a time without a pressure drop.

Closed (Pressurised) Hydraulic System

fig_02_07Original redraw

Closed system — the selectors are fed in parallel from a pressurised manifold, so any service can be operated at full pressure at any time. A non-return valve traps the pump's output and an accumulator stores pressurised fluid, so system pressure is maintained even when the pump is idle; a relief valve guards the maximum. It is more capable than the open-centre system, at the cost of more components.

Hydraulic Reservoir

fig_02_08Original redraw

Reservoir — stores the system fluid, allows for thermal expansion and the volume difference as actuators extend and retract, and lets air separate out. It is vented (or pressurised, to prevent pump cavitation at altitude). A stack (standpipe) arrangement draws the main-system fluid from a higher point and reserves the fluid below it for the engine-driven pump, so a leak in the main system cannot empty the engine-pump supply — protecting essential services like the brakes.

Hydraulic Filter & Bypass

fig_02_09Original redraw

Filter — removes the fine particles that wear pumps, valves and seals, keeping the fluid clean. The element traps dirt; if it clogs, the pressure drop across it rises and a spring-loaded bypass valve opens so the system still gets fluid — dirty fluid is better than none — usually after lighting a clogged-filter warning. Filters are fitted in the pressure, return and sometimes case-drain lines.

Hand Pump (Double-Acting)

fig_02_10Original redraw

Hand pump — a manual backup to raise pressure for essential services (gear, brakes, flaps) if the engine-driven pump fails, or on the ground with engines off. Moving the lever drives a piston between two chambers; inlet and outlet check (non-return) valves make the flow one-way, and a double-acting pump delivers fluid on both strokes of the handle, so each pull and push does useful work.

Spur-Gear Pump

fig_02_11Original redraw

Spur-gear pump — the common constant-delivery (fixed-volume) hydraulic pump. Two meshing gears turn inside a close-fitting casing: where the teeth unmesh at the inlet a space opens and fluid is drawn in, it is carried round the outside in the tooth gaps, and where the teeth mesh at the outlet the fluid is squeezed out. It delivers a fixed volume per revolution, so it needs a relief valve or an automatic cut-out to control system pressure.

Automatic Cut-Out Valve (ACOV)

fig_02_15Original redraw

Automatic cut-out valve — lets a fixed-delivery pump run continuously without wasting power or overheating. When the accumulator and system are fully charged, the ACOV diverts the pump's output back to the reservoir at low pressure (the pump is "cut out", or unloaded), a non-return valve holding the system pressure meanwhile. When pressure falls to the cut-in value as services are used, the valve directs the pump back to the system. It cycles between cut-out and cut-in pressures.

Hydraulic Accumulator

fig_02_16Original redraw

Accumulator — a pressure vessel split by a free piston (or a diaphragm/bladder) with a nitrogen gas charge on one side and system fluid on the other. The compressed gas acts as a spring: it stores hydraulic energy to help meet a heavy demand, maintains system pressure when the pump is cut out, and damps the pressure surges and pump pulsations that would otherwise hammer the system. Its gas pre-charge must be checked at the correct pressure.

Actuators — Single, Balanced & Double-Acting

fig_02_17Original redraw

Actuators (jacks) — convert fluid pressure into a push or pull. A single-acting jack has only one port: pressure drives it one way and a return spring (or gravity/air load) pushes it back. A balanced actuator has the rod passing out both ends, so the piston area — and therefore the force and speed — is the same in each direction. A double-acting jack has a port at each end: pressure to one side extends the ram while the other side returns to the reservoir, and reversing the ports retracts it — powered movement both ways, as gear and flaps need.

Non-Return (Check) Valve

fig_02_20Original redraw

Non-return (check) valve — allows flow in one direction only. Forward flow pushes a spring-loaded ball (or poppet) off its seat and passes freely; any tendency to flow backwards seats the ball and blocks it. It is used everywhere in a hydraulic system — to trap the pump's delivery so pressure is held, to stop one service feeding back into another, and to force fluid through a filter or cooler rather than around it.

Four-Port Selector Valve

fig_02_22Original redraw

Four-port selector — the valve that controls a double-acting service. It has four connections: pressure in, return to reservoir, and two service ports to the ends of the jack. Moving the selector (a rotary plug or sliding spool) connects pressure to one end of the jack and return to the other, driving the ram one way; the opposite selection reverses both, driving it back. A centre (neutral) position isolates or by-passes the service.

Oleo-Pneumatic Shock Strut

fig_03_01Original redraw

Oleo-pneumatic strut — the standard shock absorber. On landing the lower cylinder telescopes up and forces oil through a small orifice, turning the impact energy into heat (damping); a gas (nitrogen) charge, kept separate from the oil by a free piston, acts as the spring that supports the weight and returns the strut. A flutter/metering valve restricts the oil more on the rebound, and torque links stop the lower cylinder rotating while allowing it to slide.

Nose Landing Gear & Steering

fig_03_03Original redraw

Nose gear — a single oleo strut carrying a steerable wheel, taking no braking but guiding the aircraft on the ground. A steering actuator (hydraulic, through the torque links, or via the rudder pedals/tiller) turns the lower strut; a shimmy damper resists the rapid self-excited oscillation ("shimmy") a castoring nose wheel can develop at speed. It retracts forward so that, if hydraulics fail, airflow and gravity help it fall and lock down.

Divided Wheel & Fusible Plug

fig_04_02Original redraw

Divided wheel — a modern wheel is made in two halves bolted together at the rim, because a one-piece wheel could not be fitted over the bead of a modern tubeless tyre. The halves are sealed with an O-ring. Set into the wheel are fusible (thermal) plugs: if the brakes overheat badly, the plug's core melts and lets the tyre deflate in a controlled way rather than bursting explosively — a key safety feature after a heavy or rejected-take-off braking.

Tyre Construction

fig_05_01Original redraw

Tyre construction — a tyre is built up in layers. The casing plies (fabric cords set in rubber, laid cross-ply or radial) carry the inflation and ground loads and give the tyre its strength. The tread provides the wearing, gripping surface with grooves to shed water; the flexible sidewall protects the casing; and the stiff bead wires anchor the tyre onto the wheel rim. Aircraft tyres run at high pressure and are usually inflated with nitrogen.

Regions of the Tyre

fig_05_02Original redraw

Regions of the tyre — inspection and damage limits are defined by region. The crown is the central tread area that contacts the runway; the shoulder is the curved transition where tread meets sidewall; the sidewall is the flexing side region carrying the markings; and the bead is the reinforced inner edge that seats and seals on the wheel rim. A cut's seriousness depends on which region it is in and whether it reaches the casing cords.

Tread Wear & Creep Markers

fig_05_03Original redraw

Wear & creep — tyres are replaced on condition, not just hours. Tread-wear indicators are small holes or the base of the grooves; when the tread wears down to them the tyre is removed. A pair of painted creep (slip) marks are aligned across the tyre-to-wheel joint: if they move apart, the tyre has crept round the rim (which can shear the inflation valve on a tubed tyre), and the tyre must be checked and re-fitted.

Multiple-Disc Brake

fig_06_03Original redraw

Multiple-disc brake — big aircraft need far more friction area than a single disc gives, so the brake is a stack of discs. Alternate rotors are keyed to and spin with the wheel, while the stators between them are keyed to the fixed axle. Hydraulic pistons clamp the whole stack together, and the friction between every rotor/stator face absorbs the aircraft's energy as heat. The discs are steel or, on modern jets, lighter and more heat-tolerant carbon.

Brake Wear Indicator

fig_06_02Original redraw

Brake wear indicator — as the brake discs wear, the pistons have to travel further to clamp them, which draws a wear pin steadily back into its housing. Wear is checked by measuring how far the pin protrudes with the brakes applied (parking brake on): a long protrusion means plenty of life, and when the pin is flush with the housing the brake pack has reached its wear limit and must be changed. It is a simple, direct go/no-go check.

Anti-Skid System

fig_06_04Original redraw

Anti-skid — a locked wheel gives less braking and can burst the tyre, so the system keeps each wheel turning just below the point of skidding. A wheel-speed sensor feeds the controller; if a wheel decelerates too rapidly (an impending skid), the controller signals that wheel's modulating valve to release brake pressure momentarily, then reapply it, many times a second. This gives the shortest stop the runway allows and protects the tyres — the same idea as car ABS.

Autobrake Deceleration Levels

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Autobrake — automatically applies braking to achieve a selected deceleration rate without the pilot on the pedals, freeing them to manage the rollout. On landing, LO/MED/HI (or MAX) trade stopping distance for passenger comfort and brake heat. The RTO (rejected take-off) setting arms maximum braking that triggers automatically if the throttles are closed at high speed during a take-off abort. Pressing the pedals or advancing the throttles disarms it.

Brake Heat & Cooling

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Brake heat — braking turns the aircraft's huge kinetic energy into heat in the discs, and the temperature often peaks several minutes after stopping as heat soaks outward to the wheel and tyre. A hot brake needs a cooling period (or brake fans) before the next take-off, so it still has capacity to absorb a rejected take-off. If it overheats badly, the wheel's fusible plugs melt to deflate the tyre safely rather than let it burst.

Gear Over-Centre Down-Lock

fig_03_06Original redraw

Over-centre down-lock — the gear must stay down even if hydraulic pressure is lost, so it is held by a folding side-stay (drag/side brace) whose knee joint is pushed just past the straight (over centre) position and held there by a spring or a mechanical lock. Any landing load then tends to tighten the joint rather than fold it, so the leg cannot collapse. Unlocking needs a deliberate actuator pull to break it back over centre before retraction.

Gear Retraction & Sequencing

fig_03_10Original redraw

Retraction sequencing — raising the gear is a timed sequence, not a single motion: the doors open, then the gear retracts and engages its uplock, then the doors close (on aircraft with sequenced doors). Sequence valves — operated by the gear/door positions — enforce the order so a leg never swings into a closed door. Lowering reverses it, with gravity and airloads assisting so the gear free-falls and locks down if the normal system fails.

Nose-Wheel Steering (Hydraulic)

fig_03_04Original redraw

Nose-wheel steering — a tiller (and, for small angles, the rudder pedals) moves a metering valve that ports hydraulic pressure to steering actuators acting on a collar around the lower nose strut, turning the wheel. The valve meters pressure in proportion to the input, so steering is progressive, and a follow-up linkage centres the valve once the commanded angle is reached. In towing or on castoring, the system is de-pressurised so the wheel can swivel freely.

Landing-Gear Position Indication

fig_07_09Original redraw

Gear indication — each leg has an up-lock and a down-lock microswitch feeding the cockpit. The classic display shows a green light for each leg down and locked ("three greens"), a red light while a leg is in transit or unlocked, and no light when up and locked. A warning horn sounds if the throttles are closed (approach) with any gear not down and locked, guarding against a gear-up landing — the lights and horn are independent of the gear-selector position.

Carbon vs Steel Brakes

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Carbon vs steel brakes — steel disc stacks are cheap and robust but heavy, and their friction can fade at very high temperatures. Carbon (carbon-fibre reinforced carbon) brakes are much lighter, have a far higher heat capacity, and actually hold their friction better when hot, which is why airliners use them despite the cost — the weight saving and performance pay off. Carbon wears mainly by the number of brake applications rather than distance, so taxiing technique affects its life.

Primary Flight Controls & Axes

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Primary controls — three surfaces move the aircraft about three axes through the centre of gravity. Ailerons (outboard on each wing, moving in opposition) roll it about the longitudinal axis; the elevator (or all-moving tailplane) pitches it about the lateral axis; the rudder yaws it about the normal (vertical) axis. Each is moved by the pilot's controls through cables or pushrods, and each generates its force by changing the camber of its surface.

Hinge Moment

fig_08_04Original redraw

Hinge moment — when a control surface is deflected, the aerodynamic force it generates acts at some distance behind the hinge, creating a moment (F × d) that tries to return the surface to neutral. The pilot feels this as the stick force. It grows with airspeed (force) and surface size, so on fast or large aircraft the hinge moment becomes too great to manage by muscle alone — which is why controls are aerodynamically balanced, or powered.

Aerodynamic Balance

fig_08_05Original redraw

Aerodynamic balance — reduces the hinge moment (and so the stick force) by placing some of the control surface's area ahead of the hinge line. The airflow on that forward area produces a moment that helps deflect the surface, offsetting part of the restoring moment. A set-back (inset) hinge moves the whole hinge line aft; a horn balance adds a forward-projecting area at the tip. Overbalancing is avoided, as it would make the control unstable.

Internal Balance (Balance Seal)

fig_08_06Original redraw

Internal balance — hides the balance area inside the wing so there is no draggy horn in the airstream. The control surface's nose sits in a vented bay, divided by a flexible seal. When the surface deflects, the pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces acts across the seal on the nose area and produces a moment that assists the deflection, reducing the hinge moment — the same effect as a horn balance, but aerodynamically clean.

Mass Balance (Anti-Flutter)

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Mass balance — prevents flutter, the destructive coupling of bending and twisting that can occur when a control surface's centre of gravity lies behind its hinge. A mass (balance weight) is fixed ahead of the hinge line to bring the surface's C of G onto, or just ahead of, the hinge, so inertia no longer drives the surface in a diverging oscillation. It is a safety-of-flight item: damaged or re-painted surfaces must be re-balanced to limits.

Trim & Fixed Tabs

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Trim tabs — a small hinged tab at the trailing edge of a control surface that lets the pilot hold the surface deflected without a continuous stick force. A controllable trim tab is moved from the cockpit and deflects opposite to the desired surface movement; the little aerodynamic force on it holds the big surface over, giving hands-off trimmed flight. A fixed tab is a simple metal strip bent on the ground to correct a permanent out-of-trim tendency.

Balance, Servo & Anti-Servo Tabs

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Tab variations — a balance tab is geared to move opposite to the surface automatically as it deflects, lightening the stick force. A servo tab is moved directly by the pilot's controls while the main surface floats free — the tab's force flies the surface, so no muscle acts on it (used on large manual aircraft). An anti-servo (anti-balance) tab moves the same way as the surface to increase the stick force, adding feel and preventing over-control on an all-moving tailplane.

Spring Tab

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Spring tab — a balance tab whose linkage contains a spring, so it only comes into play when it is needed. At low airspeeds and small stick forces the spring is stiff and the tab stays neutral, giving good feel; at high speeds or large deflections, where the hinge moment is large, the spring flexes and the tab deflects to act as a balance tab, keeping the stick force manageable across the whole speed range.

Variable-Incidence Tailplane Trim

fig_08_17Original redraw

Variable-incidence tailplane — large aircraft trim in pitch by moving the whole tailplane (stabiliser) rather than a small tab, because at high speed a tab would be ineffective or overloaded. A screw jack, driven by the trim control, changes the tailplane's angle of incidence; the elevator then only has to make small corrections about this trimmed datum. It gives a large, powerful trim range and reduces drag compared with holding the elevator deflected.

Flap Types

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Flaps — trailing-edge high-lift devices that let the wing fly slower for take-off and landing. A plain flap simply hinges down, adding camber. A split flap lowers only the lower surface, adding drag usefully for landing. A slotted flap opens a gap that feeds high-energy air over the top, delaying the stall for a larger lift increase. A Fowler flap slides aft and down, adding both area and camber — the most effective, used on airliners (often double- or triple-slotted).

Leading-Edge Slats & Slots

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Slats & slots — leading-edge high-lift devices that delay the stall to a higher angle of attack, raising the maximum lift. A slot is a gap near the leading edge; a slat is a small aerofoil that extends forward to open one. Air from the high-pressure underside is ducted through the slot to re-energise the boundary layer on the upper surface, keeping the flow attached at angles where it would otherwise separate. Slats may be fixed, automatic (suction-operated) or powered.

Air Brakes & Spoilers

fig_08_14Original redraw

Air brakes & spoilers — surfaces that deliberately increase drag (and, for spoilers, reduce lift). A spoiler rises from the wing's upper surface to spoil the lift there: used asymmetrically it rolls the aircraft (assisting or replacing the ailerons), symmetrically in flight it acts as a speed brake, and fully up on touchdown it dumps lift to plant the wheels and make the brakes bite. A dedicated air brake (fuselage- or wing-mounted) adds drag without the lift change.

Mach Trim

fig_08_18Original redraw

Mach trim — as a jet accelerates toward its critical Mach number the wing's centre of pressure moves aft, which pitches the nose down ("Mach tuck") — a dangerous, worsening tendency. A Mach trim system senses Mach number and automatically applies a nose-up correction, either by moving the stabiliser or, on some aircraft, by transferring fuel aft-to-forward to shift the C of G, keeping the aircraft in trim and the controls conventional through the high-speed range.

Powered Flying Control & Artificial Feel

fig_09_03Original redraw

Powered flying control — when hinge moments are too large for muscle, a hydraulic jack moves the surface and the pilot only operates a servo valve. Moving the column ports pressure and return to the jack, which drives the surface and, through a feedback linkage, re-centres the valve when the commanded position is reached. Because the pilot no longer feels the real air loads, an artificial-feel (Q-feel) unit applies a stick force that rises with dynamic pressure, so the controls still feel natural and over-stressing is discouraged.

Fly-by-Wire & Envelope Protection

fig_09_11Original redraw

Fly-by-wire — there is no mechanical link from the controls to the surfaces. The sidestick (or column) sends an electrical signal of the pilot's demand to flight-control computers, which work out the surface movements and command the hydraulic (or electric) actuators. Because a computer is in the loop, it can enforce flight-envelope protection — limiting g, angle of attack, bank and speed — so the aircraft will not normally be flown outside its safe limits, while saving weight and enabling relaxed-stability designs.

Light-Aircraft Hot & Cold Air

fig_10_01Original redraw

Light-aircraft heating — a simple, cheap system. Ram air is ducted around a section of the exhaust pipe inside a heat muff, where it is warmed, then mixed with cold ram air in proportions the pilot selects to set cabin temperature. Because the heated air has passed close to the exhaust, a crack in the exhaust is a carbon-monoxide hazard — which is why a CO detector is carried and carb/cabin heat is treated with respect.

Combustion Heater

fig_10_02Original redraw

Combustion heater — a small fuel-fired furnace used where exhaust-muff heat is insufficient. Fuel is burned in a sealed combustion chamber and the products are vented overboard; the cabin ventilation air passes over the outside of that hot chamber and is warmed without ever mixing with the combustion gases. This keeps the cabin air clean, but the heater has its own fuel, ignition and overheat-protection systems that must work correctly.

Bleed-Air Sources & Uses

fig_10_04Original redraw

Bleed air — hot, high-pressure air tapped from the engine compressor is the workhorse of the airframe systems. From a common manifold it feeds air conditioning and pressurisation, thermal anti-icing of wings and engine intakes, engine starting (and cross-bleed start), and the pressurisation of hydraulic and water tanks. On the ground or with engines off, the APU or a ground cart supplies the same manifold, so the services work without the main engines running.

Air-Cycle (Bootstrap) System

fig_10_06Original redraw

Air-cycle (bootstrap) system — the standard jet air conditioner, using no refrigerant. Hot bleed air is first cooled in the primary heat exchanger (by ram air), then compressed in the air-cycle machine (which heats it again), cooled once more in the secondary heat exchanger, and finally expanded through the turbine — doing work that drives the compressor on the same shaft (the "bootstrap") and dropping the temperature sharply. A water separator removes the condensate, and a hot-air trim valve sets the final cabin temperature.

Cabin Air Distribution

fig_10_11Original redraw

Cabin air distribution — conditioned air is fed in (usually from the ceiling), flows down and across the cabin, and leaves near the floor. Much of it is recirculated through fans and HEPA filters to save bleed air, and blended with fresh conditioned air for an even temperature and good air quality. The amount discharged overboard through the outflow valve is what the pressurisation controller modulates to hold the chosen cabin altitude.

Pressurised & Unpressurised Areas

fig_11_01Original redraw

Pressurised hull — only the part of the fuselage containing people is pressurised, sealed between a forward and an aft pressure bulkhead (the aft one often a curved dome). The nose radome, the tail cone and the wheel bays stay unpressurised. The pressure cabin is a strong, fatigue-critical structure because it is inflated and deflated on every flight; its windows, doors and the bulkheads carry the pressure loads, and plug-type doors seal tighter as pressure rises.

Outflow, Safety & Dump Valves

fig_11_02Original redraw

Pressurisation valves — cabin pressure is set by how much air is let out, since the inflow is roughly constant. The outflow valve modulates that discharge to hold the scheduled cabin altitude. A safety (max-differential) relief valve opens if the differential pressure ever exceeds the structural limit; a separate negative-pressure relief valve lets outside air back in if the cabin ever becomes lower than ambient (e.g. a rapid descent), preventing the hull being crushed inwards.

Pressurisation Control

fig_11_03Original redraw

Pressurisation control — an automatic controller holds the cabin to a schedule. Given the cruise altitude and the selected landing-field elevation, it computes a smooth cabin-altitude profile and drives the outflow valve to follow it, keeping the cabin rate of change gentle (around 300–500 ft/min) for comfort. It also respects the maximum differential-pressure limit. Modern systems are electronic and digital, with a standby/manual mode the crew can take over if the auto controller fails.

Pressurisation Profile

fig_11_04Original redraw

Pressurisation profile — the cabin does not follow the aircraft up and down; it rises much more gently to a maximum cabin altitude of about 8000 ft at cruise, even though the aircraft is far higher. The controller keeps the cabin rate of change comfortable and never lets the differential pressure exceed the structural maximum. On descent the cabin is brought down to arrive at (or just below) the destination field elevation, so the doors open with no residual pressure.

Where Airframe Icing Occurs

fig_12_02Original redraw

Icing-prone areas — ice forms first on the thin, forward-facing parts where the airflow curves most sharply: the leading edges of the wings, tailplane and fin, the engine intakes, the propeller and spinner, the windscreen, and the pitot/static and aerials. Ice there is most damaging — it spoils lift, adds weight and drag, unbalances the propeller, and blocks the pressure instruments — so these are exactly the surfaces that carry anti-ice or de-ice protection.

Ice Detector (Vibrating Probe)

fig_12_06Original redraw

Ice detector — gives the crew (or the system) a positive signal that ice is forming, rather than relying on the eye. A common type is a small probe driven to vibrate at its resonant frequency; as ice accretes, the added mass lowers the frequency, and when the drop passes a threshold the unit flags an ICE warning and can switch on the anti-ice automatically. It then heats to shed the ice and re-arms. Other types sense the ice optically or by a rotating element.

Pneumatic De-Icer Boots

fig_12_09Original redraw

De-icer boots — a de-icing (not anti-icing) method: ice is allowed to form, then removed in cycles. Inflatable rubber boots bonded to the leading edges are periodically pumped up with bleed or pump air; as they balloon, the brittle ice layer is cracked and flung off by the airflow, and the boots deflate for the next build-up. They are light and low-power, but must not be operated too early (thin ice can "bridge" over the inflated shape) — wait for a worthwhile layer.

Thermal Anti-Icing (Hot Air)

fig_12_12Original redraw

Thermal anti-icing — keeps the surface warm so ice never forms (anti-ice), rather than removing it after the fact. Hot bleed air is ducted along the inside of the leading edge through a perforated "piccolo" tube that sprays it against the skin, holding it above freezing, before it is vented overboard. It is used on wing and tailplane leading edges and engine intakes. Because it draws continuous bleed air, it costs engine performance — so it is selected only when needed.

Electric Heating (Mats & Windscreen)

fig_12_13Original redraw

Electric heating — used where hot air is impractical. Thin heating elements are built into propeller/spinner boots (cycled around the blades so the system load stays manageable, the ice shed by centrifugal force) and laminated into windscreens, where a transparent conductive film both prevents ice/mist and keeps the glass warm and less brittle against bird strike. Pitot heads, static ports, stall vanes and drain masts are electrically heated too. Rain is cleared by wipers, a rain-repellent coating, or a high-pressure air blast.

Continuous-Flow Oxygen System

fig_13_01Original redraw

Continuous-flow oxygen — the simple system used for passengers and light aircraft. Oxygen is stored at high pressure in a cylinder, protected by a shut-off valve, a non-return valve and a filter, and its pressure is dropped by a pressure-reducing valve to feed a low-pressure line to the mask outlets. Each mask simply flows oxygen continuously into a rebreather bag; the user breathes a mix of that oxygen and cabin air. It is recharged through a charging valve, and the contents read on the HP gauge.

Diluter- & Pressure-Demand Regulators

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Demand regulators — crew systems supply oxygen only when the user inhales, saving the supply. A diluter-demand regulator mixes oxygen with cabin air, automatically increasing the oxygen proportion as cabin altitude rises until it delivers 100% at high altitude. A pressure-demand regulator goes further, forcing oxygen into the lungs under positive pressure above about 40 000 ft, where even pure oxygen at ambient pressure is not enough. A 100% / emergency selection gives pure, slightly pressurised oxygen for smoke or fumes.

Chemical Oxygen Generator

supplementaryOriginal redraw

Chemical oxygen generator — most airliners supply passenger oxygen not from bottles but from a chemical "candle" above each row. When the cabin loses pressure the masks drop; pulling a mask down removes a firing pin that ignites a sodium-chlorate core, which burns to release oxygen for a fixed time (typically 10–20 minutes — enough for an emergency descent). It cannot be turned off once started and gets hot. The crew, by contrast, use a gaseous system they can regulate.

Ionisation Smoke Detector

fig_14_03Original redraw

Ionisation smoke detector — watches for a change in a tiny electric current. A small radioactive source ionises the air between two charged plates, so a very small current flows. When smoke particles enter, they attach to the ions and slow them, reducing the current; the circuit senses the drop and raises the alarm. This type reacts quickly to the invisible particles of early, flaming fires. A different optical (light-scatter) type is better at detecting the larger particles of smouldering smoke; both are used, in lavatories, cargo holds and avionics bays.

Cargo Smoke Detection & Suppression

fig_14_05Original redraw

Cargo fire protection — holds are sealed and lined so a fire is starved of air, and air is continuously sampled past smoke detectors. On a warning, the crew discharge an extinguisher bottle into the hold: a "knockdown" shot quickly floods it, then a slow metered flow maintains an inert concentration for the rest of the flight (sized to the ETOPS diversion time). The hold stays sealed — opening it would feed the fire — so the strategy is contain, suppress and land.

The Fire Triangle

fig_15_01Original redraw

Fire triangle — a fire needs three things at once: fuel to burn, oxygen to support combustion, and enough heat to reach and sustain ignition. Remove any one side and the fire goes out — which is exactly how extinguishing works: cool it below ignition (water), starve it of fuel (shut the fuel/LP cock), or smother it by displacing the oxygen (CO₂, Halon). Knowing which side a given agent attacks tells you which agent to use on which fire.

Continuous-Wire (Fire-Wire) Detector

fig_15_03Original redraw

Fire-wire detector — a single continuous sensing element routed all around the hot zones (engine, APU) so it responds to a fire anywhere along its length, and to a general overheat as well as a local flame. Inside the tube a material changes its electrical resistance or capacitance with temperature; when any section gets hot enough the control unit sees the change and raises the FIRE warning. It is averaging and self-restoring — the warning clears when it cools — and systems are usually dual-loop so a single broken wire cannot give a false alarm.

Dual Fire-Detection Loops

fig_15_05Original redraw

Dual loops — a false fire warning (e.g. a single chafed wire) is costly, so critical zones carry two independent detection loops. In normal "dual-loop" logic both loops must detect the fire before the warning is given, which rejects a fault in either one. If one loop itself fails, the system reverts to single-loop operation on the remaining good loop, so protection is never lost. The crew can also test the loops, and a fault is annunciated separately from a genuine fire.

Engine Fire Protection

fig_15_09Original redraw

Engine fire drill — the fire handle (or switch) does several things at once. Pulling it isolates the engine: it shuts the LP fuel cock, closes the hydraulic and bleed-air shut-off valves, and trips the generator — cutting off everything that feeds the fire. It also arms the extinguisher bottles, so rotating or pressing the handle then discharges a Halon (or equivalent) bottle into the engine zone. A typical installation has two shots (two bottles, or one into each of two engines), used in sequence if the first does not clear the warning.

Extinguisher Agents

fig_15_13Original redraw

Extinguishing agents — matched to the fire. Water cools and suits solid (Class A) fires but must never go on electrical or fuel fires. CO₂ smothers by displacing oxygen and leaves no residue, good for electrical and small liquid fires. Dry powder chemically interrupts the combustion reaction and covers several classes but is messy. Halon (BCF) also breaks the chemical reaction, is clean and very effective, and is the classic hand and engine-bay agent — now being replaced by newer clean agents for environmental reasons.

Gravity-Feed Fuel System

fig_16_01Original redraw

Gravity-feed fuel — the simplest system, used on high-wing light aircraft where the tanks sit above the engine. Fuel flows down by gravity through a fuel selector (Left / Right / Both / Off), a gascolator (a strainer and water-drain bowl at the lowest point) and on to the carburettor — no fuel pump is strictly needed. Tanks are vented so they can breathe, each has a drain for the pre-flight water check, and the selector lets the pilot manage the tanks and shut the fuel off in an emergency.

Jet Engine Fuel System

fig_16_05Original redraw

Jet fuel system — fuel is drawn from the tank by a boost pump and pushed through the low-pressure pump, an LP filter and a fuel-oil heater (which warms the fuel to prevent ice and cools the engine oil). The high-pressure pump raises it to burner pressure, and the fuel control unit (mechanical, or electronic FADEC) meters exactly the right flow to the burners for the thrust demanded, limiting acceleration and over-temperature. A flow meter indicates consumption, and surplus fuel is spilled back to the pump inlet.
