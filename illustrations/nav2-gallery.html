Local Mean Time Varies with Longitude

nav_401_figure_01Original redraw

Local Mean Time (LMT) is based on the mean Sun crossing the local meridian: it is 1200 LMT when the mean Sun is overhead. The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours — 15° of longitude per hour, or 1° every 4 minutes — so every meridian has a different LMT. Places east of Greenwich are ahead (later); places west are behind. To convert: LMT = UTC + longitude in time if east, − if west. In the example it is 0900 UTC: at 45°E it is 1200 LMT, at 90°E 1500, at 180° 2100, while at 45°W it is only 0600.

Standard Time Zones

nav_405_figure_01Original redraw

Standard time avoids every town keeping its own LMT. The world is divided into 24 zones, each nominally 15° wide and centred on a meridian divisible by 15°, keeping the time of that meridian. Zone Z (Zulu) is centred on Greenwich and keeps UTC; zones to the east are ahead (+1 to +12) and to the west behind (−1 to −12). Countries bend the boundaries to follow their borders, and some use half-hour offsets. South Africa keeps UTC + 2 (SAST) all year, with no daylight saving. Standard times for every State are published in the Air Almanac and the AIP.

The International Date Line

nav_409_figure_01Original redraw

Somewhere the date has to change. Travelling east round the world the clock is advanced an hour every 15°, gaining a whole day by the time the traveller returns; west, a day is lost. The International Date Line, which follows the 180° meridian but bends to keep island groups on one date, is where that day is corrected. Crossing it westbound (from the Americas towards Asia) the date advances one day; crossing eastbound it goes back one day. Either side, the clock time is the same — only the date differs. UTC is never affected: always work in UTC and convert at the end.

The Earth's Orbit — Perihelion & Aphelion

nav_386_figure_02Original redraw

The orbit is an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus. The Earth is closest to the Sun — perihelion, about 91.4 million statute miles — in early January, and farthest — aphelion, about 94.6 million — in early July. By Kepler’s second law it moves fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion, which is one reason the length of the apparent solar day varies. The difference in distance is small: it is the tilt of the axis, not distance, that causes the seasons — the Southern Hemisphere has summer when the Earth is nearest the Sun.

The Earth's Axial Tilt

nav_387_figure_01Original redraw

The spin axis is tilted about 23½° from the perpendicular to the orbital plane (the axis makes 66½° with the plane itself), and it stays pointing the same way in space — towards the Pole Star — all year. As the Earth goes round the Sun, first one hemisphere and then the other is tilted towards it, so the Sun’s rays strike each hemisphere more or less directly and the days are longer or shorter. That, not the change in distance, is what produces the seasons. The tilt also fixes the tropics (23½°N and S) and the polar circles (66½°N and S).

Solstices & Equinoxes

nav_387_figure_02Original redraw

Four turning points of the year (Northern-Hemisphere names; the seasons are reversed in the south). At the summer solstice, about 21 June, the Sun is overhead at 23½°N (the Tropic of Cancer) and the Northern Hemisphere has its longest day. At the winter solstice, about 21 December, it is overhead at 23½°S (the Tropic of Capricorn) — midsummer in South Africa. At the equinoxes, about 21 March and 23 September, the Sun is overhead at the equator and day and night are equal everywhere. Between them the Sun’s declination moves steadily north and south.

The Sun's Declination Through the Year

nav_389_figure_01Original redraw

Declination is the latitude of the point on the Earth where the Sun is directly overhead. It swings smoothly between 23½°N at the June solstice and 23½°S at the December solstice, passing through 0° at the equinoxes. The curve is steepest at the equinoxes — declination changes by about 0.4° a day in March and September — and flattest at the solstices, where it barely changes for several days (hence “sol-stice”, the Sun standing still). Declination, with latitude, sets the times of sunrise and sunset and the length of daylight.

Day, Night & the Terminator

nav_427_figure_01Original redraw

The terminator is the great circle separating the sunlit half of the Earth from the dark half. Because the axis is tilted, at the June solstice (shown) the terminator does not pass through the poles: everywhere north of 66½°N (the Arctic Circle) stays in daylight all day, everywhere south of 66½°S stays dark, and the further north a place is, the longer its day. At the equator day and night are always about 12 hours each. At the equinoxes the terminator passes through both poles and every place has 12 hours of day and night.

Polar Stereographic Chart

nav_290_figure_03Original redraw

The chart for polar regions. The projection is azimuthal: the paper touches the Earth at the pole and the light is at the opposite pole. Meridians are straight lines radiating from the pole and parallels are concentric circles. The chart is conformal; scale is correct at the pole and expands away from it (about 2% at 75°, 7% at 60°), so it is used for latitudes above about 70°. A straight line is very nearly a great circle near the pole. Convergency on the chart equals change of longitude, the same as at the pole itself — so it overstates the real convergency at lower latitudes.

Mercator — The Cylindrical Projection

nav_291_figure_02Original redraw

Mercator wraps a cylinder round the Earth, touching it at the equator. On the unrolled chart the meridians are straight, parallel and equally spaced, and the parallels are straight lines at right angles to them. Because real meridians converge but chart meridians do not, east–west distances are stretched by the secant of the latitude; to keep the chart conformal the parallels are spaced further apart in exactly the same proportion. The result: scale is correct only at the equator and expands as sec(latitude) — doubling at 60° — and the poles cannot be shown at all. A rhumb line is a straight line, which is why Mercator charts are used for plotting.

Mercator — Expansion of the Latitude Scale

nav_300_figure_02Original redraw

Why Greenland looks enormous. On a Mercator chart the spacing between parallels grows with latitude: the chart scale at any latitude is the equatorial scale × sec(latitude) — × 1.15 at 30°, × 1.41 at 45°, × 2.0 at 60°, × 3.9 at 75°. Equal steps of latitude on the Earth therefore become longer and longer steps on the chart. Because scale varies across the chart, distance must always be measured on the latitude scale at the side, level with the mid-latitude of the line being measured — never at the equator and never on the longitude scale at the top or bottom.

Mercator — Area Exaggeration at High Latitude

nav_304_figure_01Original redraw

Equal areas on the Earth are not equal on a Mercator chart. Both dimensions of a small area are stretched by sec(latitude), so its area is multiplied by sec²(latitude): × 2 at 45°, × 4 at 60°, × 15 at 75°. A country near the pole therefore appears far bigger than an equal one near the equator — the reason Greenland looks as large as Africa, although Africa is about 14 times larger. Shapes of small areas are correct (the chart is conformal), but sizes are not, so distances must always be measured with the latitude scale, never by eye.

Great Circle vs Rhumb Line on a Mercator Chart

nav_306_figure_01Original redraw

On a Mercator chart the rhumb line is straight and the great circle is curved, concave towards the equator — that is, it bows towards the nearer pole. Northern-hemisphere routes bow north, southern-hemisphere routes bow south, and a route crossing the equator has a great circle that changes its side of curvature where it crosses. Because the great circle is not straight, radio bearings (which travel along great circles) must be converted to rhumb-line bearings, using the conversion angle, before they are plotted on a Mercator chart.

Great Circle vs Rhumb Line — A Real Route

nav_032_figure_01Original redraw

Example: Vancouver (49°N 123°W) to Moscow (56°N 037°E). On a Mercator chart the rhumb line is a nearly horizontal straight line, but the great circle arcs far to the north, over Greenland and close to the Arctic. Although it looks much longer on the chart, the great circle is the shorter route on the Earth — the chart stretches high latitudes. For long-range flight planning a great circle is plotted on a Lambert or polar chart (where it is nearly straight) and broken into rhumb-line legs of a few hundred miles, each flown on a constant heading, so the aircraft follows the great circle closely.

The Conic Projection

nav_292_figure_02Original redraw

A cone fits the middle latitudes. In a simple conic projection a cone is placed over the globe touching it along one parallel, the parallel of origin; meridians project as straight lines meeting at the cone’s apex and parallels as arcs of circles. Cut the cone along one meridian and unroll it, and the result is a fan-shaped chart. Scale is correct along the parallel of origin. The Lambert conformal chart refines this with a cone that cuts the Earth along two standard parallels, giving very small scale error over a wide band of latitude — ideal for mid-latitude navigation charts, on which a great circle is very nearly a straight line.

Lambert Chart — Convergence Factor

nav_344_figure_01Original redraw

On a Lambert chart the meridians converge by a fixed proportion. The angle between two meridians on the chart is change of longitude × n, where n is the constant of the cone (the convergence factor), equal to sin(parallel of origin). It is the same anywhere on the chart, which is why a straight line on a Lambert chart closely approximates a great circle. Example: parallel of origin 45°N, n = sin 45° = 0.707; meridians 100° of longitude apart converge by 100 × 0.707 = 70.7° on the chart. Chart convergency is correct at the parallel of origin, too small nearer the equator and too large nearer the pole.

Lambert Chart — Scale & the Standard Parallels

nav_348_figure_01Original redraw

Scale on a Lambert chart. It is correct along the two standard parallels; between them it is slightly too small, contracting most at the parallel of origin midway between; outside them it is slightly too large and expands increasingly towards the edges. With the standard parallels at one-sixth and five-sixths of the band the error is under about 1% — small enough that distances may be measured with a constant scale line, and why ICAO 1:1 000 000 and 1:500 000 aeronautical charts use this projection. In the example the standard parallels are 47°N and 43°N, with the parallel of origin at about 45°N.

Great Circle ≈ Straight Line on a Lambert Chart

nav_351_figure_02Original redraw

Why Lambert charts are used for radio navigation. Because chart convergence equals real convergency at the parallel of origin and is very close to it elsewhere, a great circle is very nearly a straight line on a Lambert chart — exactly straight only where it crosses the parallel of origin, and curving very slightly, concave towards the parallel of origin, elsewhere. Radio bearings, which follow great circles, can therefore be plotted with a ruler, measured from the meridian at the station. A rhumb line, by contrast, appears as a curve, concave towards the nearer pole.

Crossing a Current — The Vector Solution

nav_132_figure_02Original redraw

Head into the current. To cross 10 nm from A to B against a 5 kt current, the boat must point upstream so that the sideways part of its own speed exactly cancels the current. The three vectors form a triangle: boat through the water (its heading and speed), current (set and rate) and path over the ground (the resultant). With a boat speed of 10 kt the crossing needs a heading 30° upstream and makes good about 8.7 kt over the ground, taking about 69 minutes. This is the triangle of velocities: replace boat with aircraft, water speed with TAS and current with wind.

Relative Motion — Two Aircraft from One Point

nav_165_figure_01Original redraw

How fast are two aircraft separating? Two aircraft leave point A together: one on 040° at 120 kt, the other on 075° at 140 kt. Their positions after any time are found by plotting each track and distance, and the line joining them is their separation. The rate at which that separation grows is the relative velocity: the vector difference of the two ground velocities. Draw both velocity vectors from one point; the line joining their tips is the relative velocity, in both direction and speed. The same method solves interception, closing speed and “where will the other aircraft be when I get there” problems.

Crosswind & Headwind Components

nav_181_figure_02Original redraw

Resolving the wind for take-off and landing. Split the wind into a component along the runway and one across it, using the angle between wind direction and runway direction. Headwind = wind speed × cos(angle); crosswind = wind speed × sin(angle). Example: runway 27 (270°M), wind 300°M at 30 kt: angle 30°, headwind 30 × cos 30° = 26 kt, crosswind 30 × sin 30° = 15 kt from the right. Rule of thumb: for angles of 30°, 45° and 60° the crosswind is ½, ¾ and 9/10 of the wind speed. Compare the crosswind with the aircraft’s maximum demonstrated crosswind.

The Triangle of Velocities

nav_134_figure_02Original redraw

The basis of dead-reckoning navigation. Three vectors form a closed triangle. The air vector is the aircraft’s heading and true airspeed (one arrow). The wind vector is the wind velocity — direction it blows towards and speed (three arrows). Their sum is the ground vector: track and ground speed (two arrows). The angle between heading and track is the drift — wind from the left drifts the aircraft right (starboard drift). Know any four of the six quantities and the triangle gives the other two, on the navigation computer or by drawing. The wind arrows always run from the end of the air vector to the end of the ground vector.

Track Error — Track Made Good vs Planned Track

nav_205_figure_03Original redraw

Three directions from the same start point. The planned track (here 090°) is the line intended. The heading (here 100°) is where the nose was pointed. The track made good (TMG) is the line actually achieved over the ground, found from a fix. The angle between planned track and TMG is the track error (TE); the angle between heading and TMG is the actual drift. If the TMG is left of the planned track the aircraft is drifting left more than planned and the correction is to turn right. Track error is found with the 1-in-60 rule from the distance off track and the distance gone.

Regaining Track with the 1-in-60 Rule

nav_207_figure_03Original redraw

The two-part correction. After 30 nm the aircraft is found 4 nm right of track. Track error = 4 × 60 ÷ 30 = 8°: alter heading 8° left and the aircraft flies parallel to the planned track, 4 nm off. To regain track at the destination 30 nm further on, the closing angle = 4 × 60 ÷ 30 = another 8°. Total alteration: 8° + 8° = 16° left to reach the destination. Once on track, turn back by the closing angle (8°) to hold it. When the distance to go equals the distance gone, the total correction is simply twice the track error.

1-in-60 Applied to Descent Gradient

nav_217_figure_01Original redraw

Angles and gradients by the same rule. One nautical mile is about 6 080 ft, so on a 1° slope the height changes by about 100 ft per nm (6 080 ÷ 60). A 3° glide path therefore loses 300 ft per nm. Height to lose ÷ 300 gives the descent distance; and rate of descent (ft/min) ≈ ground speed (kt) × 5 on a 3° path (300 ft per nm × GS ÷ 60). Example: 3 000 ft above the runway on a 3° approach, start descent 10 nm out; at 120 kt GS descend at about 600 ft/min. A 5% gradient is about 300 ft per nm too.

Fixing by Position Lines — The 'Cocked Hat'

nav_489_figure_02Original redraw

Three position lines rarely meet exactly. A position line is a line on which the aircraft is known to lie — from a bearing, a radial or a DME range. Two lines crossing give a fix; a third line checks it, and because every bearing carries a small error the three usually form a little triangle, the cocked hat. Take the fix at the point found by visually bisecting the angles of the triangle. A small cocked hat means good bearings; a large one means at least one bearing is wrong and the fix should be treated with caution. Lines that cross at angles close to 90° give the most accurate fix; shallow crossing angles magnify errors.
