Simple AC Generator — Slip Rings

ch11_01Original redraw

Slip rings give alternating current. A loop turning in a magnetic field generates an EMF that reverses every half-turn. If each end of the loop is connected to its own continuous slip ring, with a brush bearing on each, the connections are never swapped, so the output at the brushes alternates — a sine wave, one cycle per revolution in a two-pole machine. Compare the DC generator, where a split-ring commutator swaps the connections to make the output one-directional.

Frequency — Cycles per Second

ch11_04Original redraw

Frequency is the number of complete cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). The time for one cycle is the period: T = 1 ÷ f. Aircraft AC systems use 400 Hz — much higher than the 50 or 60 Hz of mains supplies — because at higher frequency transformers, motors and generators can be made far smaller and lighter for the same power. The frequency of an alternator is set by its speed and number of poles: f = (rpm × pole pairs) ÷ 60, so constant frequency requires a constant-speed drive.

AC Waveform — Peak, RMS & Cycle

ch11_05Original redraw

Describing a sine wave. The peak (maximum) value is reached twice a cycle, once in each direction; peak-to-peak is twice the peak. The useful value is the RMS (root mean square): the DC value that would produce the same heating in a resistor. For a sine wave RMS = 0.707 × peak, and peak = 1.414 × RMS. Meters and system ratings quote RMS: the aircraft’s 115 V AC has a peak of about 163 V. The average over a whole cycle is zero; over a half-cycle it is 0.637 × peak.

Resistive Circuit — Voltage & Current in Phase

ch11_06Original redraw

In a pure resistance, current follows voltage exactly. The current rises and falls in step with the applied voltage, reaching its peaks and passing through zero at the same instants: they are in phase. All the power drawn is converted to heat or light — it is true (real) power, measured in watts, and the power factor is 1. Lamps and heaters behave like this. Ohm’s law applies to the RMS values just as for DC: I = V ÷ R.

Mutual Induction — The Transformer Principle

ch11_07Original redraw

A changing current in one coil induces a voltage in another. Alternating current in the primary coil produces an alternating magnetic flux. A common iron core carries almost all of that flux through the secondary coil, where the changing flux induces an alternating EMF — with no electrical connection between the coils. This is mutual induction, and it only works with a changing current: steady DC produces a steady flux and no output (except a brief pulse at switch-on and switch-off). It is the principle of the transformer.

Self-Inductance & Back-EMF

ch11_08Original redraw

A coil opposes any change in its own current. When current through a coil changes, its own changing flux induces an EMF in it — self-induction — that opposes the change (Lenz). At switch-on this back-EMF makes the current rise gradually instead of instantly. At switch-off the collapsing field induces a large voltage spike that tries to keep the current flowing, causing arcing at switch contacts and possible damage to electronics — which is why relay coils often have a diode across them. Inductance is measured in henries.

Inductive Circuit — Current Lags by 90°

ch11_09Original redraw

In a pure inductance the current lags the voltage by 90°. The back-EMF opposes every change, so the current reaches its peak a quarter-cycle after the voltage. The opposition is inductive reactance, XL = 2πfL ohms, which rises with frequency. Because voltage and current are 90° out of phase, energy is stored in the magnetic field and returned each half-cycle: a pure inductance consumes no true power, only reactive power. Memory aid: CIVIL — in a capacitor (C) current (I) leads voltage (V); voltage (V) leads current (I) in an inductor (L).

Capacitor Charge & Discharge

ch11_10Original redraw

A capacitor charges and discharges gradually through a resistance. Connected to a supply through a resistor, its voltage rises quickly at first and then more slowly: it reaches 63% of the supply voltage in one time constant, τ = R × C seconds, and is effectively fully charged after about 5τ. Discharging through a resistor, the voltage falls to 37% in one time constant. Time constants are used for timing circuits and to smooth the output of rectifiers.

Capacitor in an AC Circuit

ch11_11Original redraw

A capacitor blocks DC but passes AC. On DC the capacitor charges once and then no further current flows: the lamp stays dark. On AC the capacitor charges, discharges and charges the opposite way every half-cycle, so current flows back and forth through the lamp continuously and it lights, although no current actually crosses the dielectric. The opposition is capacitive reactance, XC = 1 ÷ (2πfC), which falls as frequency rises. Capacitors are used to block DC while passing signals, and for suppression and filtering.

Capacitive Circuit — Current Leads by 90°

ch11_12Original redraw

In a pure capacitance the current leads the voltage by 90°. The current is greatest when the voltage is changing fastest — as it passes through zero — and zero when the voltage is at its peak, so the current reaches its peak a quarter-cycle before the voltage. Like an inductance, a pure capacitance stores and returns energy each half-cycle and consumes no true power. Memory aid: CIVIL — in C, I leads V.

Impedance Triangle

ch11_13Original redraw

Adding resistance and reactance. Resistance and reactance are 90° apart in phase, so they cannot simply be added. They combine like the sides of a right-angled triangle: resistance R along the base, reactance X (XL − XC) at right angles, and the total opposition — the impedance Z — as the hypotenuse: Z = √(R² + X²). The angle φ between R and Z is the phase angle between voltage and current, and cos φ is the power factor. Example: R = 30 Ω, X = 40 Ω gives Z = 50 Ω and a power factor of 0.6.

Reactance vs Frequency & Resonance

ch11_14Original redraw

Two reactances that move in opposite directions. Inductive reactance XL = 2πfL rises in proportion to frequency; capacitive reactance XC = 1 ÷ (2πfC) falls as frequency rises. At one frequency they are equal and cancel: the resonant frequency, f = 1 ÷ (2π√LC). A series circuit at resonance has its minimum impedance (just R) and maximum current; a parallel circuit has its maximum impedance. Resonant (tuned) circuits select one frequency from many — the basis of radio tuning and filters.

True (Real) Power

ch11_15Original redraw

True power does useful work. Instantaneous power is voltage × current at each instant. In a resistive circuit, voltage and current are in phase, so their product is always positive — power flows from the source to the load the whole cycle, at twice the supply frequency, averaging half the peak. This is true (real) power, measured in watts (W): P = V × I × cos φ, where cos φ (the power factor) is 1 for a pure resistance. It is the power converted to heat, light or mechanical work.

Reactive & Apparent Power — The Power Triangle

ch11_17Original redraw

Three kinds of power in an AC circuit. Apparent power, V × I, is measured in volt-amperes (VA, kVA) — what the generator and cables must actually carry. Of it, true power (watts) is the part doing useful work, V × I × cos φ; reactive power, V × I × sin φ, measured in volt-amperes reactive (VAR, kVAR), simply surges back and forth between source and the inductances and capacitances each cycle. The three form a right-angled triangle, and power factor = true ÷ apparent = cos φ. That is why aircraft AC generators are rated in kVA, not kW.

Rotating-Field Alternator

ch12_01Original redraw

Aircraft alternators spin the magnet, not the windings. The field — an electromagnet fed with DC — is on the rotor; the output windings are fixed in slots in the stator. As the field sweeps past each stator coil, an alternating EMF is induced in it. Because the output windings are stationary, the heavy output current is taken off through fixed connections, with no slip rings or brushes carrying it, and the windings are easy to insulate and cool. Only the small field current needs to reach the rotor.

Single-Phase Alternator Output

ch12_02Original redraw

One winding, one sine wave. With a single set of stator coils the alternator produces one alternating output — single-phase AC — rising to a peak as a pole passes the coil and reversing as the opposite pole passes. Single-phase power is used in aircraft for many smaller loads (instruments, lighting, avionics), taken from one phase of the three-phase system. Its disadvantage for large machines is that its power pulsates at twice the supply frequency.

Three-Phase Alternator — Phases 120° Apart

ch12_03Original redraw

Three windings, three outputs. The stator carries three separate windings spaced 120° apart round the machine. The rotating field passes each in turn, producing three sine waves of the same voltage and frequency, each 120° out of phase with the next — phases A, B and C. At any instant the three add to zero. Three-phase generation gives constant total power (no pulsation), lets motors produce a rotating field without starting devices, and transmits more power per weight of cable. Aircraft systems are typically 115/200 V, 3-phase, 400 Hz.

Star vs Delta Connection

ch12_04Original redraw

Two ways to join three windings. In a star (wye) connection one end of each winding is joined to a common neutral point and the other ends are the three line outputs; a fourth wire can be taken from the neutral, giving two voltages — phase-to-neutral and line-to-line. Aircraft generators are star-connected with the neutral earthed to the airframe. In a delta connection the windings are joined end to end in a closed triangle with the lines taken from the corners; there is no neutral and only one voltage. Transformer windings and some motors use delta.

Star Connection — Line & Phase Values

ch12_05Original redraw

In a star, line voltage is √3 times phase voltage. The phase voltage is measured across one winding — between a line and the neutral; the line voltage is between any two lines. Because the two phase voltages that make up a line voltage are 120° apart, Vline = √3 × Vphase = 1.732 × Vphase. So the aircraft’s 115 V phase-to-neutral system gives 200 V line-to-line. The line current equals the phase current, because each line connects straight to one winding.

Delta Connection — Line & Phase Values

ch12_06Original redraw

In a delta, line voltage equals phase voltage. Each winding is connected directly between two lines, so Vline = Vphase. But each line current is the combination of two winding currents 120° apart, so Iline = √3 × Iphase. For the same power, delta windings carry less current and star windings less voltage; transformers often have one of each (for example a delta primary and star secondary). Power in any balanced three-phase system is √3 × Vline × Iline × cos φ.

Alternator Excitation & Voltage Regulation

ch12_07Original redraw

The field current sets the voltage. An alternator’s output voltage depends on its speed and on the strength of its rotating field. The voltage regulator (in a generator control unit) continuously senses the output voltage, compares it with a reference, and adjusts the DC excitation current to the rotor field: more field current if the voltage is low, less if it is high. Frequency, by contrast, depends only on speed and is controlled separately by the constant-speed drive.

Brushless Alternator — Exciter & Rotating Rectifier

ch12_08Original redraw

No brushes at all. The modern aircraft generator has three stages on one shaft. A permanent-magnet generator (PMG) produces AC to power the generator control unit, independent of any other supply. The GCU feeds controlled DC to the stationary field of an exciter, whose rotating armature produces AC that is converted to DC by rectifier diodes mounted on the shaft. That DC feeds the rotating main field, which induces the 115/200 V output in the stationary main stator. With no brushes or slip rings there is nothing to wear or spark, allowing high speed and reliability at altitude.

Paralleling AC Generators — Synchronising Conditions

ch12_10Original redraw

Four things must match before two AC generators are connected together. Their voltages must be equal, their frequencies equal, their waveforms in phase at the instant of connection, and their phase sequence (A-B-C) the same. If they are not, large circulating currents and torque shocks result when the breaker closes. The generator control system checks these conditions automatically — synchronisation — before allowing the bus-tie breaker to close. Many twin-engine aircraft avoid the problem by running their AC generators in split-bus (non-parallel) mode.

Real-Load Sharing — Speed Governors

ch12_12Original redraw

Real (kW) load is shared by adjusting the drive torque. Paralleled AC generators are locked to the same frequency, so neither can simply run faster. The one whose drive applies more torque pushes its rotor slightly ahead in phase and takes more of the real load. The load-sharing system compares the real load of each generator and trims each constant-speed drive (CSD or IDG governor): the more heavily loaded drive is reduced and the lighter one increased until the kW loads are equal.

Reactive-Load Sharing — Field Control

ch12_13Original redraw

Reactive (kVAR) load is shared by adjusting the excitation. Changing a paralleled generator’s field current does not change its real load; it changes how much reactive current it supplies. The generator with the stronger field takes more of the kVAR. The reactive load-sharing circuit compares the reactive currents and adjusts each voltage regulator: the generator taking too much reactive load has its excitation reduced, the other’s increased, keeping the bus voltage constant while the kVAR is shared equally.

AC Generation & Distribution Layout

ch13_01Original redraw

A typical twin-engine AC system. Each engine drives a generator (through a constant-speed drive or IDG) that feeds its own AC bus through its generator circuit breaker (GCB). The APU generator and external (ground) power can supply either bus through the bus-tie breakers (BTB), which also let one engine generator power both buses if the other fails. Transformer-rectifier units (TRUs) convert AC to 28 V DC for the DC buses and battery charging, and the static inverter can supply essential AC from the battery if all generation is lost.

Split AC/DC Bus Architecture

ch13_02Original redraw

Keeping failures on one side. In a split-bus system each engine generator normally supplies only its own side — left AC and DC buses, right AC and DC buses — and the two are not paralleled, so a fault on one side cannot take down the other and the generators need not be synchronised. If a generator fails, the bus-tie closes automatically and the remaining generator (or the APU) supplies both sides. The essential and battery buses carry the most important loads and can be fed from either side or, in the last resort, from the battery and static inverter.

Electrical Control Panel — Twin Generator

ch13_03Original redraw

Reading the overhead electrical panel. Most modern panels are synoptic: the switches are laid out on a line diagram of the system, so the pilot can see at a glance how power flows. Typical controls: a generator switch for each engine generator and the APU, bus-tie switches (usually AUTO), an external power switch lit AVAIL when ground power is connected, the battery switch, and a generator drive disconnect (guarded) to separate a failing IDG from the engine — which can only be reset on the ground. FAULT and OFF legends in the switch-lights show abnormal states.

Electrical System Display Page

ch13_04Original redraw

The electrical synoptic on the systems display. Glass-cockpit aircraft show the electrical system on an ECAM or EICAS page. For each AC generator the page shows load (percentage of rating, or kVA), voltage (about 115 V) and frequency (about 400 Hz), and a line diagram shows which buses are powered and from where — powered lines in green, unpowered or failed items in amber. DC values show TRU and battery voltage and current. Parameters going out of limits change colour, giving an early warning before a failure.

Four-Generator Electrical Control Panel

ch13_06Original redraw

Four engines, four generators. On a four-engine aircraft each generator feeds its own AC bus through its GCB, and each bus can be connected to a common synchronising (tie) bus through its bus-tie breaker (BTB). Depending on the design the generators are run paralleled through the sync bus, with real and reactive load sharing, or split. Each generator has its own field and drive disconnect controls, and the panel includes split-system breakers to divide the system into halves if a fault occurs. The layout is synoptic, matching the system diagram.

Four-Generator Electrical Synoptic

ch13_07Original redraw

The four-generator systems page repeats the panel layout as a live diagram: each generator box shows load, voltage and frequency; breakers are drawn closed (in line) or open (across the line); powered buses and lines are green. In the example all four generators are on line and the bus-ties are closed, so the generators are paralleled on the synchronising bus and share the load equally. A tripped breaker, an off-line generator or a parameter out of limits would be shown in amber.

Transformer Construction

ch14_01Original redraw

Two windings on a laminated core. The primary winding receives the supply; the secondary delivers the output. Both are wound on a common core of thin, insulated laminations of silicon steel, which carries the alternating flux from one winding to the other while the insulation between laminations blocks the eddy currents that would otherwise heat a solid core. Transformers have no moving parts and are very efficient (often 95–98%). They work only on AC.

Step-Up vs Step-Down Transformer

ch14_02Original redraw

The turns ratio sets the voltage. Vs ÷ Vp = Ns ÷ Np: the secondary voltage is the primary voltage multiplied by the ratio of turns. More turns on the secondary steps up the voltage; fewer steps down. Power is (almost) conserved, so current changes the opposite way: Is ÷ Ip = Np ÷ Ns — stepping the voltage down steps the current up. Example: 115 V with a 4 : 1 turns ratio gives 28.75 V, the basis of the TRU supply for the DC system.

Autotransformer

ch14_03Original redraw

One winding does both jobs. An autotransformer has a single winding with a tapping: the supply is connected across the whole winding (or part of it) and the output taken from a tapping point, so part of the winding is common to both input and output. It is smaller, lighter and cheaper than a two-winding transformer for small voltage changes — for example 115 V to 26 V for instruments — but there is no electrical isolation between input and output, so a fault in the winding can put the full input voltage on the output.

Half-Wave Rectifier

ch14_04Original redraw

One diode, half the wave. A diode conducts in one direction only. In series with the load, it passes the positive half-cycles and blocks the negative ones, so the output is a series of positive humps with gaps between — half-wave rectified DC. It is simple, but wastes half of each cycle and has a large ripple at the supply frequency, so it is used only for small, undemanding loads. The arrow in the diode symbol points in the direction of conventional current flow.

Full-Wave Bridge Rectifier

ch14_05Original redraw

Four diodes use the whole wave. In a bridge rectifier, on one half-cycle two diodes conduct and on the other half-cycle the other two, and in both cases the current passes through the load in the same direction. The output is a continuous series of positive humps at twice the supply frequency — much smoother than half-wave and easily smoothed further with a capacitor. Bridges are used in alternator output stages and battery chargers.

Three-Phase Rectifier — Transformer-Rectifier Unit (TRU)

ch14_06Original redraw

The TRU makes the DC. A transformer-rectifier unit takes the aircraft’s 115/200 V three-phase AC, steps it down with a three-phase transformer, and rectifies it with a six-diode bridge to about 28 V DC, typically 100–200 A. With three phases overlapping, the output has six pulses per cycle and very little ripple, so little or no smoothing is needed. TRUs feed the DC buses and charge the battery; they are cooled by a fan, with overheat warning, and have no voltage regulation of their own — their output varies slightly with load.

Rotating Magnetic Field from a Three-Phase Stator

ch15_01Original redraw

Three phases make a field that turns. Feed three stator windings, 120° apart round the machine, with three-phase current. Each produces a pulsating field along its own axis, and as the three phases peak in turn, their combined field has constant strength and rotates round the stator at the synchronous speed: n = (60 × f) ÷ pole pairs — 24 000 rpm for a two-pole machine on 400 Hz. Swapping any two supply leads reverses the direction of rotation. This rotating field is what drives every three-phase AC motor, with no commutator or brushes.

Squirrel-Cage Induction Motor

ch15_02Original redraw

The workhorse AC motor. The rotor is a squirrel cage: copper or aluminium bars set in a laminated iron core and shorted together by end rings — no electrical connection to the outside at all. The stator’s rotating field sweeps past the bars and induces currents in them; those currents, in the field, produce torque (motor principle), and the rotor follows the field. It must always run slightly slower than the field — the difference is the slip, a few per cent — otherwise no current would be induced. Simple, rugged and brushless, it drives aircraft fuel pumps, hydraulic pumps and fans.

The Silicon Atom

ch16_01Original redraw

Why silicon is a semiconductor. A silicon atom has 14 electrons in three shells, the outermost containing four valence electrons. Germanium, the other common semiconductor, also has four. Those four outer electrons are shared with neighbouring atoms in a crystal, and at very low temperature none is free, so pure silicon is an insulator. At room temperature a few gain enough energy to break free and conduct — far fewer than in a metal, hence “semi”-conductor. Adding impurities (doping) changes this dramatically.

Covalent Bonds in a Silicon Crystal

ch16_02Original redraw

Atoms share electrons to complete their shells. In a silicon crystal each atom sits at the centre of four neighbours and shares one of its four valence electrons with each, so every bond is a pair of shared electrons — a covalent bond. Each atom then behaves as if it had a full outer shell of eight, and the electrons are tightly held: pure (intrinsic) silicon conducts very poorly. Heat or light can break a bond, freeing an electron and leaving a gap — a hole — so conductivity rises with temperature, the opposite of a metal.

N-Type Doping — A Free Electron

ch16_03Original redraw

Adding electrons. If a tiny amount of a pentavalent impurity — one with five valence electrons, such as phosphorus, arsenic or antimony — is added, each impurity atom bonds with four silicon neighbours and has one electron left over, very loosely held and free to move. The impurity is called a donor. The material now conducts mainly by these free negative electrons, so it is N-type silicon. It is still electrically neutral overall: the donor atom has an extra proton for its extra electron.

P-Type Doping — A Hole

ch16_04Original redraw

Adding holes. A trivalent impurity — with only three valence electrons, such as boron, gallium or indium — bonds with four silicon neighbours but leaves one bond short of an electron: a hole. A neighbouring electron can jump in to fill it, leaving a hole where it came from, so the hole effectively moves through the crystal like a positive charge. The impurity is an acceptor, and the material is P-type, with holes as the majority carriers.

The PN Junction & Depletion Layer

ch16_05Original redraw

Where P meets N. When P-type and N-type silicon are formed in one crystal, free electrons near the junction diffuse across and fill holes on the other side. This leaves a thin region on both sides with no free carriers — the depletion layer — containing fixed negative ions on the P side and positive ions on the N side. Their charge creates a barrier potential (about 0.6–0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for germanium) that stops further diffusion. The junction is now a one-way valve: the basis of the diode.

Forward vs Reverse Bias

ch16_06Original redraw

The junction conducts one way. Forward bias: positive to the P side, negative to the N side. The supply pushes carriers towards the junction, the depletion layer narrows, and once the barrier potential (about 0.6 V for silicon) is overcome a large current flows. Reverse bias: positive to N, negative to P. Carriers are pulled away from the junction, the depletion layer widens, and only a tiny leakage current flows — until, at the breakdown voltage, the junction suddenly conducts heavily (avalanche), which normally destroys an ordinary diode.

The Diode & Its Symbol

ch16_07Original redraw

Reading a diode. The circuit symbol is an arrow (anode) meeting a bar (cathode); the arrow points in the direction of conventional current. On the component itself the cathode end is marked with a band. The characteristic shows almost no forward current until about 0.6 V (silicon), then a steep rise; in reverse only a tiny leakage until breakdown. A Zener diode is designed to break down at a precise voltage without damage and is used as a voltage reference; a light-emitting diode (LED) gives off light when forward-biased.

Transistor — NPN & PNP

ch16_08Original redraw

Three layers, three leads. A bipolar transistor is a sandwich of two PN junctions: NPN (a thin P-type base between two N-type layers) or PNP. The three terminals are the collector, base and emitter. In the symbol the arrow on the emitter points in the direction of conventional current: out for NPN (“Not Pointing iN”), in for PNP. A small base current controls a much larger collector current, so the transistor can act as an amplifier or a switch.

Transistor as a Switch

ch16_09Original redraw

A small current switches a large one. With no base current, the base-emitter junction is not forward-biased and almost no collector current flows: the transistor is OFF (cut-off) and the lamp is dark. Apply a small base current through a resistor and the transistor turns fully ON (saturated): a collector current many times larger — typically 20 to 200 times — flows and the lamp lights. With no moving parts, transistor switches are fast, silent and do not wear; they replace relays throughout modern avionics and power control.

AND Gate

ch17_01Original redraw

AND: all inputs must be true. The output Q is 1 (high) only when input A and input B are both 1; any 0 input gives a 0 output. It is equivalent to two switches in series controlling a lamp: the lamp lights only if both are closed. Typical use: a warning that should come on only when two conditions exist together, e.g. throttle at take-off power AND flaps not set — the take-off configuration warning.

OR Gate

ch17_02Original redraw

OR: any input true. The output is 1 when input A or input B (or both) is 1; only when all inputs are 0 is the output 0. It is equivalent to two switches in parallel: either one lights the lamp. Typical use: a master caution light that comes on if any one of several faults is present.

NOT Gate (Inverter)

ch17_03Original redraw

NOT: the inverter. A single input, and the output is always the opposite: 1 in gives 0 out, 0 in gives 1 out. The small circle (bubble) on the output is the symbol for inversion, and appears on NAND and NOR gates too. Typical use: turning a “gear down and locked” signal into a “gear not locked” warning.

NAND Gate

ch17_04Original redraw

NAND: NOT-AND. An AND gate with its output inverted: the output is 0 only when all inputs are 1, and 1 otherwise. NAND is a universal gate — any other logic function can be built from NAND gates alone — so it is widely used in integrated circuits.

NOR Gate

ch17_05Original redraw

NOR: NOT-OR. An OR gate with its output inverted: the output is 1 only when all inputs are 0; any 1 input gives 0. Like NAND, NOR is a universal gate. Typical use: an “all normal” indication that is lit only when none of several fault signals is present.

XOR (Exclusive-OR) Gate

ch17_06Original redraw

XOR: exclusive OR. The output is 1 when the inputs are different — one or the other, but not both — and 0 when they are the same. That makes it a natural comparator: fed with the outputs of two duplicated systems, an XOR gate signals a disagreement between them, as in comparator warnings between two attitude or air-data sources. It is also the core of binary addition.
