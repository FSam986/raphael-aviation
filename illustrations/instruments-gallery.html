Linear Circular Scale

fig_01_01Original redraw

Linear scale — the graduations are spaced evenly around the dial, so each unit of the quantity takes the same angular distance and the pointer rotates at a constant rate with the value. It is the easiest scale to read accurately, used where the instrument's mechanism gives an output proportional to the quantity (for example a well-designed rate-of-climb or a percent-rpm gauge over its working range).

Non-Linear Circular Scale

fig_01_02Original redraw

Non-linear scale — the graduations are unevenly spaced, because the instrument's mechanism responds non-linearly to the quantity. The classic example is the airspeed indicator, which measures dynamic pressure proportional to the square of the speed: the low speeds are cramped together near the start and the high speeds spread out, so an ASI reads coarsely at the bottom of its range and finely at the top.

Single-Pointer with Drum Counter

fig_01_03Original redraw

Pointer + drum — a combined display that gives the best of both worlds. The moving pointer around the dial lets the pilot judge a rate and trend at a glance (is it rising, how fast), while a digital drum (counter) window shows the exact value without having to interpolate between marks. It is widely used on airspeed and altitude displays, where you need both the quick picture and the precise figure.

Pitot & Static Pressure

fig_02_01Original redraw

Pitot & static — the two pressures every air-data instrument needs. The pitot tube points directly into the airflow and senses total (pitot) pressure — the static pressure of the air plus the dynamic pressure of bringing the moving air to rest. The static port is mounted flush to the skin, at right angles to the flow, so it feels only the ambient static pressure. Their difference is the dynamic pressure that drives the airspeed indicator; static alone drives the altimeter and VSI.

Combined Pitot/Static Head

fig_02_02Original redraw

One probe, two pressures. The open front of the head faces the airflow and brings the air to rest, so the central tube senses total (pitot) pressure — static plus dynamic. Static slots in the side of the head, where the flow is undisturbed, feed a separate chamber with static pressure. Each has its own pipe to the instruments. An electric heater keeps the head free of ice, and a drain hole lets water out of the pitot tube. A blocked pitot tube or static slot gives false airspeed, altitude and vertical-speed readings.

Flush Static Vent

fig_02_04Original redraw

Flush static vent — a small hole set flush with the fuselage skin, away from the propeller slipstream and in smooth airflow, so it senses true static pressure with no ram effect. Vents are fitted on both sides of the fuselage and cross-connected so that any pressure difference caused by sideslip (yaw) is averaged out. Keeping the vent clean and unobstructed — never painted over or taped — is essential, as a blocked static freezes the altimeter and corrupts airspeed.

Alternate Static Source

fig_02_06Original redraw

Alternate static source — if the external static ports block (ice, water, insects), a cockpit selector switches the static instruments to an alternate source, usually the cabin interior. Because cabin pressure is slightly lower than true static (venturi effect past the fuselage), selecting alternate makes the altimeter read a little high and the airspeed a little high, so the pilot applies the published corrections. In an unpressurised aircraft, breaking the glass of the VSI is a last-ditch alternate static.

Airspeed Indicator Mechanism

fig_04_01Original redraw

The ASI measures dynamic pressure. Pitot pressure — dynamic plus static (Dy + S) — is fed inside the capsule; static pressure (S) fills the case around it. The static part cancels, so the capsule expands in proportion to dynamic pressure alone, ½ρV². A lever, toothed quadrant and pinion magnify the tiny movement and turn the pointer over a scale marked in knots. Because it is calibrated for ISA sea-level density, the ASI shows indicated airspeed, which under-reads true airspeed as altitude increases.

Altimeter Datums — QNH, QFE, QNE

fig_05_01Original redraw

Altimeter datums — the altimeter measures height above whatever pressure you set on its sub-scale. Set QFE (aerodrome pressure) and it reads height above the aerodrome (zero on the runway). Set QNH (sea-level pressure) and it reads altitude above mean sea level (aerodrome elevation on the ground). Set the standard 1013.25 hPa (QNE) and it reads a pressure altitude expressed as a Flight Level, so every aircraft in the cruise uses the same reference and stays vertically separated regardless of the actual pressure.

VSI — Level Flight

fig_06_01Original redraw

How the VSI works — static pressure is fed directly to the capsule and, through a metering unit (choke), slowly to the case. In level flight the pressure is not changing, so capsule and case are equal and the pointer rests at zero. The instrument measures the rate of change of static pressure, not the pressure itself. The choke is compensated for temperature and density so the calibration holds at all altitudes.

VSI — Descent

fig_06_02Original redraw

Descent — static pressure rises. The capsule receives the higher pressure immediately, but air can only enter the case slowly through the choke, so the case lags at a lower pressure. The capsule expands and the linkage moves the pointer to show a rate of descent. The faster the descent, the bigger the pressure difference. When the aircraft levels off, the case catches up after a few seconds (the instrument’s lag) and the pointer returns to zero.

VSI — Climb

fig_06_03Original redraw

Climb — static pressure falls. The capsule follows it immediately, but air can only escape from the case slowly through the choke, so the case stays at a higher pressure. The capsule is compressed and the pointer shows a rate of climb. If the static source blocks, capsule and case equalise and the VSI reads zero whatever the aircraft is doing.

Pitot/Static Probe Installation

fig_02_05Original redraw

Probe installation — where the probe sits matters as much as the probe itself. It is mounted on a short mast so the openings stand clear of the slow-moving boundary layer against the skin, and sited away from the propeller slipstream, doors, vents and skin curvature that would disturb the pressure. Any residual error from imperfect siting is the position (pressure) error, which is measured in flight test and published as a correction. Heaters keep it ice-free.

Dual Air-Data Architecture

fig_08_03Original redraw

Dual air-data architecture — modern aircraft replace the direct pressure instruments with air-data computers that take pitot and static (and temperature) and compute airspeed, altitude, Mach and vertical speed electronically. For integrity there are two independent chains — each with its own pitot/static and ADC feeding the captain's and first officer's displays — plus a wholly separate standby instrument on its own source. A disagreement between the two alerts the crew, and no single blockage or failure can remove all air-data information.

Gyro Rotor in Gimbals

fig_11_04Original redraw

Gyro in gimbals — a gyroscope is a rotor spun at high speed and carried in a set of pivoted rings (gimbals) so the spin axis is free to hold its direction while the aircraft moves around it. The rotor sits in the inner gimbal, which pivots inside the outer gimbal, which pivots in the case — giving the spin axis freedom in two planes. This mounting, plus the rotor's mass and speed, gives the two properties every gyro instrument lives on: rigidity (it stays pointing where it was set) and precession.

Precession — Torque Acts 90° Round

fig_11_05Original redraw

Precession — when a force (torque) is applied to try to tilt a spinning rotor, the rotor does not move where pushed. Instead the effect is felt 90° further round in the direction of spin, so the axis moves off at a right angle to the force. This is how a gyro instrument is deliberately controlled — an erection system applies small forces to precess the gyro back to the vertical or to a heading — and it is also the cause of real-wander errors when unwanted friction torques act on the gimbals.

Apparent (Earth-Rate) Wander

fig_11_10Original redraw

Apparent wander — a free gyro's rigidity holds its axis fixed in space, not relative to the Earth. Because the Earth is turning underneath it, an observer on the ground sees the axis slowly drift even though the gyro itself has not moved. This Earth-rate drift is greatest at the poles and zero at the equator, following 15°/hour × sine of the latitude. Directional gyros are given a latitude-nut correction (or are tied to a magnetic reference) to cancel it.

Transport Wander

fig_11_13Original redraw

Transport wander — carrying the gyro across the curved Earth is the second source of apparent wander. As the aircraft flies over the surface, the local vertical and the local meridian change direction underneath the (space-rigid) gyro, so its axis appears to wander relative to the new local references. It depends on groundspeed and track and, for a directional gyro, on the change of longitude. Together with Earth-rate drift it is why a DGI must be re-synchronised to the compass regularly.

Directional Gyro — Construction

fig_12_01Original redraw

Directional gyro (DGI) — gives a steady heading reference free of the turning and acceleration errors that plague the magnetic compass. The rotor spins about a horizontal axis held rigid in azimuth; a heading card is geared to it and read against a fixed lubber line. The DGI has no north-seeking ability of its own, so the pilot sets it from the magnetic compass in steady flight and re-synchronises it periodically to cancel the gyro's apparent wander.

DGI Heading Card

fig_12_07Original redraw

DGI face — the rotating compass card sits behind a fixed miniature aircraft, and the current heading is read where the card meets the lubber line at the top. Because the card is driven by the rigid gyro, it stays steady during turns, so you can roll out accurately onto a heading without the lead/lag of a magnetic compass. The pilot uses the caging/setting knob to align it to the compass, and must re-check that alignment every 10–15 minutes.

Artificial Horizon — Face

fig_13_01Original redraw

Artificial horizon (attitude indicator) — shows the aircraft's pitch and bank against an artificial horizon in a single, intuitive picture. A gyro-stabilised horizon bar (with a blue "sky" and brown "ground") stays level with the real horizon while the fixed miniature aircraft moves relative to it: the gap between them shows pitch, and rotation of the whole display against the top bank scale shows angle of bank. It is the primary instrument for flying without outside reference.

Artificial Horizon — Construction

fig_13_05Original redraw

AH construction — it is an earth gyro with its spin axis held vertical. The rotor stays rigid pointing at the centre of the Earth, and the aircraft (with the gimbal frame and case) pitches and rolls around it. That relative movement is transmitted through the gimbal linkage to the horizon bar, which therefore always displays the attitude relative to the true vertical. An erection system keeps the spin axis vertical against the slow drift caused by friction and manoeuvres.

AH Acceleration & Turn Errors

fig_13_09Original redraw

Acceleration error — the device that keeps the AH's spin axis vertical is pendulous, so it cannot tell gravity from acceleration. On a forward acceleration (e.g. take-off) the pendulous erection swings aft, applies a torque, and precesses the gyro, giving a small false nose-up (and, with the real turning errors, a slight false bank). The errors are transient and settle once speed is steady; modern erection cut-outs reduce them. The pilot allows for them during acceleration and in turns.

Turn Indicator — Rate Gyro

fig_14_01Original redraw

Turn indicator — a rate gyro that measures how fast the aircraft is yawing. The rotor is carried on a single gimbal restrained by a spring; when the aircraft turns, the yaw precesses the gyro against the spring until the spring force balances the precession. The gimbal angle — and so the needle deflection — is therefore proportional to the rate of turn, calibrated so a standard "rate one" turn (3° per second, a full 360° in two minutes) reads at the marked index. A separate balance ball shows slip or skid.

Magnetic Field of a Bar Magnet

fig_09_02Original redraw

Magnetic field — a magnet is surrounded by an invisible field whose shape is shown by lines of force that leave the north pole, curve round through the surrounding space and return to the south pole, completing the loop inside the magnet. The field is strongest (lines closest together) at the poles. A freely-suspended magnet — a compass needle — aligns itself along these lines, which is what lets it point along the Earth's own magnetic field toward the magnetic poles.

Direct-Reading Compass

fig_10_01Original redraw

Direct-reading compass — a self-contained north-seeking instrument. A pair of magnets is fixed to a card assembly, pivoted on a jewelled bearing and floating in a liquid that both supports its weight (reducing pivot friction) and damps its swinging. The assembly aligns with the Earth's field, and the heading is read where the card meets the lubber line. An expansion chamber allows for the liquid's change of volume with temperature. It needs no power, so it is the standby heading reference.

Pendulous Suspension

fig_10_03Original redraw

Pendulous suspension — the magnet/card assembly is hung so its centre of gravity sits below the pivot, like a pendulum. Its weight keeps the card roughly horizontal despite the Earth's field trying to pull the needle down along the dip. This pendulous hang is essential for a readable card, but it is exactly what causes the compass's turning and acceleration errors: any acceleration or bank swings the low-slung assembly, momentarily tilting the card and letting the vertical component of the field deflect it.

Flux Valve — Exciter & Secondary

fig_17_05Original redraw

Flux valve — the heading sensor of a remote-indicating (gyro-magnetic) compass. A ring or spider of highly permeable permalloy legs is driven to magnetic saturation twice per cycle by an exciter coil fed with AC. Between saturations the legs are free to collect the Earth's horizontal field; the amount collected by each leg depends on the aircraft's heading, so a second-harmonic signal is induced in the secondary windings whose phase and amplitude encode the heading. Having no moving parts, it is mounted in a wingtip or fin away from aircraft magnetism.

Flux-Valve Output vs Heading

fig_17_07Original redraw

Flux-valve output — as the aircraft's heading changes, the field collected by each leg varies sinusoidally, so the amplitude and phase of the induced second-harmonic signal trace out a smooth wave against heading. The receiver synchro in the compass compares this three-wire signal to find the one position that nulls the error, and drives the heading card (or HSI) to match. The gyro element then smooths the flux-valve reading, giving the steady, error-free heading of a gyro-magnetic compass.

Aircraft Magnetism & the Compass

fig_16_03Original redraw

Aircraft magnetism — the aeroplane carries its own magnetism, from permanently magnetised hard iron (steel parts magnetised during manufacture) and temporarily induced soft iron. This local field adds to the Earth's and pulls the compass needle away from magnetic north; the angle between the two is deviation. It is reduced by small corrector magnets during a "compass swing" and the residual is recorded on a deviation card by the pilot so that compass heading can be converted to magnetic.

Deviation Varies with Heading

fig_16_04Original redraw

Deviation vs heading — deviation is not constant; it changes as the aircraft turns, because the direction of its own magnetism relative to the Earth's field changes. Analysed mathematically it is the sum of a constant term (coefficient A, a misalignment) plus a sine term (coefficient B, fore-and-aft hard iron) and a cosine term (coefficient C, athwartships hard iron). The compass swing measures these on the cardinal headings and the correctors null B and C, leaving the small residual that is tabulated on the deviation card.

Air-Driven Gyro Rotor

fig_12_03Original redraw

Air-driven gyros are spun by the aircraft’s vacuum (suction) system. Air drawn into the instrument case is directed by a nozzle onto buckets cut into the rim of the rotor, driving it like a small turbine at roughly 10 000–15 000 rpm. The rotor is made with most of its mass in the rim, which maximises its moment of inertia and therefore its rigidity. Low suction — an engine at idle, a failed pump or a blocked filter — means low rotor speed, less rigidity and an unreliable, slow-to-erect instrument; always check the suction gauge.

Gyro Erection

fig_12_06Original redraw

Erection — left alone, a gyro slowly drifts off its datum through friction and wander, so every attitude/heading gyro has an erection system that nudges it back. It works through precession: a small force (from a pendulous device, levelling switches and torque motors, or air-reaction ports) is applied so that its 90° precession moves the spin axis back toward the correct datum — vertical for the artificial horizon, or the set heading for the DGI. The erection rate is deliberately slow so that normal manoeuvres do not drag the gyro off.

Remote Servo-Driven Horizon

fig_13_11Original redraw

Remote horizon — large aircraft put the gyro away from the panel. A rugged remote vertical gyro (or the inertial reference system) senses pitch and roll in the avionics bay and sends the attitude as synchro signals; an amplifier and servo drive the cockpit display to follow. This lets one accurate gyro feed several displays and the autopilot, removes the fragile mechanism from behind the panel, and allows a far larger, clearer electronic attitude display with no limits on pitch or roll.

Gyroscopic Precession

fig_11_06Original redraw

Precession — a force applied to a spinning gyro does not move it in the direction of the force. The rotor behaves as if the force had been applied 90° further on in the direction of spin. Here a weight pulls down on one end of the spin axis (trying to tilt it about Y–Y); instead of tilting, the whole gyro turns about the vertical axis Z–Z. The rate of precession rises with a bigger force and falls with higher rotor speed or mass (more rigidity). Every gyro erection and slaving system uses this effect deliberately.

Earth-Rate (Apparent) Wander at the Pole

fig_11_12Original redraw

Apparent wander — a perfect gyro holds its spin axis fixed in space, but we measure it against the Earth, which turns at 15° per hour. Seen from above the North Pole, a horizontal-axis gyro is lined up with a meridian at position 1; four hours later the Earth has carried it to position 2 and the meridian has swung 60°, while the gyro still points the same way in space. To an observer on the Earth the gyro appears to have drifted. Apparent drift is 15 × sin(latitude) °/h: maximum at the poles, zero at the equator.

DGI Apparent Wander at High Latitude

fig_12_10Original redraw

DGI apparent wander — a stationary directional gyro with no real wander still changes its reading. At A it is aligned with the meridian and reads 360°. Three hours later the Earth has turned 45° (15°/h × 3 h, at the pole); the gyro still points the same way in space, but the aircraft’s north has rotated, so the DGI now reads 045°. The rate is 15 × sin(latitude) °/h: readings increase in the Southern Hemisphere and decrease in the Northern. The latitude nut (or regular re-synchronising with the compass) removes it.

DGI Total Wander vs Latitude (Latitude Nut Set for 60°N)

fig_12_13Original redraw

Latitude-nut compensation — Earth-rate drift (15 × sin latitude °/h) is cancelled by a small latitude nut that makes the gyro precess at an equal and opposite rate. But the nut gives a fixed correction, exact only at the latitude it was set for. Here it is set for 60°N (+13°/h): the total wander is zero at 60°N, +13°/h at the equator (no Earth rate to cancel), −2°/h at the North Pole and +28°/h at the South Pole, where the correction and the Earth rate add. Away from the set latitude the DGI must be re-synchronised more often.

Artificial Horizon — Climb Indication

fig_13_03Original redraw

Reading pitch — the miniature aircraft is fixed to the instrument case (and so to the real aircraft); the horizon bar is held level by the gyro. In a climb the nose — and the miniature aircraft — rise above the real horizon, so the horizon bar appears below the aircraft symbol. In a descent the bar sits above it. The display is “inside-looking-out”: it shows exactly what the pilot would see through the windscreen.

Artificial Horizon — Bank Indication

fig_13_04Original redraw

Reading bank — when the aircraft rolls, the case and miniature aircraft roll with it while the gyro holds the horizon bar level, so the bar appears tilted the opposite way. In a right bank the right wing of the miniature aircraft drops toward the ground half of the display and the horizon slopes up to the right. The bank pointer stays at the true vertical; read the bank angle where it sits against the fixed scale (marks at 10°, 20°, 30° and 60°).

Pendulous-Vane Erection Unit

fig_13_06Original redraw

Air-driven erection — air that has driven the rotor exhausts through four ports in a pendulous unit under the rotor housing. Each port is half covered by a vane that hangs freely, opposite vanes sharing one spindle. With the gyro vertical all four ports are equally open and the jet reactions cancel. If the gyro tilts, the vanes stay vertical under gravity: one port opens and its opposite closes. The unbalanced reaction acts at 90° to the tilt, and precession brings the gyro back to the vertical. In an acceleration or a turn the vanes swing falsely — the cause of the air-driven horizon’s acceleration and turning errors.

Electric Artificial Horizon — Levelling Switches & Torque Motors

fig_13_10Original redraw

Electric erection — the electric horizon keeps its spin axis vertical with levelling (mercury) switches and torque motors. Because a gyro moves at 90° to an applied torque, each motor sits on the other axis: the fore-and-aft switch senses a pitch error and energises the pitch torque motor on the roll axis; the lateral switch senses a roll error and energises the roll torque motor on the pitch axis. The higher rotor speed (about 22 000 rpm) gives more rigidity, and the erection can be cut off during accelerations and turns — so the electric horizon has far smaller errors than the air-driven type.

Radio Altimeter — FM-CW Principle

fig_20_01Original redraw

How the radio altimeter measures height — it transmits a continuous wave straight down whose frequency is swept up and down around 4 300 MHz (band 4 200–4 400 MHz, SHF). The echo returns after a short delay Δt, by which time the transmitter has moved on to a new frequency. The difference between transmitted and received frequency (Δf) is proportional to the delay, and so to the height above the surface directly below. Δf drops to zero for an instant at each sweep reversal; the sweep rate is low so this has no effect on the reading. Range 0–2 500 ft, accuracy about ±3% or ±1 ft.

Radio Altimeter Indicator

fig_20_02Original redraw

The analogue indicator — the scale is expanded at low level (0–500 ft takes half the dial) where precision matters most, then compressed up to 2 500 ft. Above 2 500 ft, or with no valid signal, the pointer hides behind the mask. The pilot sets a decision height with the DH bug; the DH lamp lights (often with an audio tone) as the aircraft descends through it. A failure flag covers the face if power or signal is lost. The reading is height above the terrain directly below — not altitude — and it feeds the GPWS and the autoland flare.

Radio Altitude on an EFIS Attitude Display (Ring Scale)

fig_20_04Original redraw

Electronic radio-height display — on many EFIS attitude displays the radio height is shown as digits only above 1 000 ft. Below 1 000 ft a circular scale appears around the digits: its circumference represents 1 000 ft, and the white ring unwinds as the aircraft descends, giving a strong sense of closure rate. The selected decision height is shown by a magenta marker; on reaching DH the ring and digits change colour (amber) and flash as the alert.

Radio Altitude on the Primary Flight Display

fig_20_06Original redraw

Where to find it on a glass cockpit — the PFD shows radio altitude as a digital readout at the bottom of the attitude display, appearing automatically below 2 500 ft. It usually changes colour (or the ground “rises” to meet the aircraft symbol) near the selected minimums. Do not confuse it with the barometric altitude tape on the right: the tape shows altitude above the pressure datum set (QNH), the radio altitude shows actual height above the ground beneath the aircraft.

Radio Altimeter — Residual Height

fig_20_07Original redraw

Residual height — the radio-altimeter aerials are on the fuselage belly, well above the wheels, and the signal also takes time to travel through the aerial cables. That built-in offset — the residual height — is calibrated out, so the indicator shows the height of the main wheels above the runway in the landing attitude and reads zero at touchdown. On the ground, with the oleos compressed and the aircraft level, the true gap is slightly smaller, so the indicator may show a small negative value.

Flight Management System — Architecture

fig_21_01Original redraw

The FMS — two flight management computers (FMC), each with its own navigation and performance database, are programmed by the pilots through the control display units (CDU). Each FMC blends position from the IRS, GPS and DME/VOR updating, air data from the ADC, and fuel/engine data to compute position, the lateral and vertical flight path (LNAV/VNAV) and performance. The two FMCs cross-talk so they agree, and each can take over from the other. Outputs go to the autopilot/flight director, the autothrottle and the EFIS map.

EFIS — Architecture (Symbol Generators)

fig_22_01Original redraw

EFIS architecture — each pilot has two displays: an EADI (attitude/flight director — the PFD) and an EHSI (navigation — the ND). The pictures are drawn by symbol generators (SG), which take raw data from the sensors and turn it into display symbology. The left SG drives the captain’s displays and the right SG the first officer’s; a centre SG is a hot spare that can replace either. Each pilot’s EFIS control panel selects the display mode, range and decision height. Light sensors adjust brightness automatically, and the SGs cross-compare to flag disagreements.

Closed-Loop Control Principle

fig_26_01Original redraw

Closed-loop control — every autopilot channel is a closed loop. A demand (input) is compared with what the aircraft is actually doing; the error detector produces a signal proportional to the difference; this is amplified and drives a servo that moves the control. The result is measured and fed back to the detector, so the loop keeps acting until the error is zero. Without feedback (open loop) the system could not know when to stop — feedback is what makes the control accurate and stable.

Autopilot Servo Loop to the Control Surface

fig_26_03Original redraw

One autopilot channel — attitude sensors detect a disturbance and send an electrical signal to the autopilot computer, which processes it and commands the servomotor. The servo moves the control surface through a mechanical coupling. Two feedbacks close the loop: rate and position feedback from the servo tells the computer how far and how fast the surface has moved (so it does not over-control), and aerodynamic feedback — the aircraft’s response — is sensed again by the attitude sensors. The pilot engages the system and selects modes through the control panel.

Autopilot Inner & Outer Loops

fig_26_04Original redraw

Two loops — the inner loop is the stabilisation loop: attitude sensor → error detector → autopilot computer → servo → control surface → aircraft attitude, and round again. It holds the attitude it is given. The outer loop provides guidance: a flight-path demand (here a heading selected on the mode control panel) is compared with a reference (the gyro-magnetic compass), and the difference is fed into the inner loop as a new attitude demand — e.g. bank to turn. Heading, altitude, speed, VOR/LOC, glideslope and LNAV/VNAV are all outer-loop modes; the outer loop never drives the servos directly.

Autopilot Actuator Connected in Parallel

fig_26_05Original redraw

Parallel actuator — the autopilot servo is connected alongside the pilot’s control run, so when it moves the run it moves both the control surface and the pilot’s controls. The pilot can see and feel what the autopilot is doing, and it has full control authority — but the pilot cannot make inputs without overriding or disconnecting it. (A series actuator, used for yaw dampers and stability augmentation, sits in the run and moves the surface without moving the cockpit controls, with limited authority.) The artificial-feel unit gives control forces where a powered flying control unit (PFCU) removes the natural feel.

Autoland — Approach Sequence

fig_27_01Original redraw

Automatic landing — the sequence is driven first by the ILS and then by radio height. After localiser and glideslope capture, the remaining autopilot channels engage at about 1 500 ft RA and FLARE/ROLLOUT arm; from here the system must be fail-operational or fail-passive. Near 330 ft the aircraft is trimmed nose-up. At about 45 ft the flare mode commands a reducing rate of descent while the autothrottle retards; at about 5 ft the flare gives way to touchdown and rollout guidance on the localiser. Exact heights are type-specific; the order of events is what matters.

ILS / Autoland Categories & Minima

fig_27_02Original redraw

Operating minima by category — the lower the decision height (DH) and runway visual range (RVR), the more capable and redundant the equipment must be. CAT I: DH not below 200 ft, RVR not less than 550 m (or visibility 800 m). CAT II: DH below 200 ft but not below 100 ft, RVR not less than 300 m. CAT IIIA: DH below 100 ft or none, RVR not less than 200 m. CAT IIIB: DH below 50 ft or none, RVR less than 200 m but not less than 75 m. CAT IIIC: no DH and no RVR limit. CAT II/III need a radio altimeter for DH; CAT III needs an automatic landing system. Not drawn to scale.
