Sleep Credit & Deficit — The Sleep/Wake Cycle

hp-40Original redraw

Sleep works like a bank account. Each hour of sleep earns about two hours of wakefulness: eight hours asleep builds a credit of 16 points, which is then spent at one point per hour through a 16-hour day. The credit cannot be stored beyond about 16 points, so “sleeping in advance” does not work, and a shortened night leaves a sleep deficit that carries into the next duty. Performance falls as the credit runs out, worst when a low credit coincides with the circadian low between about 0200 and 0600. A deficit is repaid with slightly longer, deeper sleep, not hour for hour.

Circadian Rhythm — Body Temperature

hp-41Original redraw

The body runs on a daily clock. Body temperature, alertness, hormone levels and performance all follow a circadian rhythm. Temperature is lowest at about 0500 and highest at about 1700; alertness and performance follow it, so the hours between about 0200 and 0600 — the “window of circadian low” — are when errors are most likely and staying awake is hardest. Left without time cues the clock runs slow, with a period of about 25 hours; it is reset each day by zeitgebers (“time givers”): light and dark above all, plus meals, activity and social routine.

Sleep Stages & REM Cycles

hp-42Original redraw

A night’s sleep is a series of cycles, each about 90 minutes. Sleep deepens from stage 1 (drowsy) through stage 2 to stages 3 and 4, the deep slow-wave sleep that restores the body, then lightens into REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, when dreaming occurs and which restores the mind and consolidates learning. Slow-wave sleep dominates the early cycles and REM the later ones, so cutting sleep short loses mostly REM. After deprivation the body takes slow-wave sleep first. Waking from deep sleep causes sleep inertia — grogginess for several minutes — so in-flight naps are best kept short (about 20–30 minutes).

Jet Lag — Westbound vs Eastbound

hp-43Original redraw

Crossing time zones leaves the body clock behind. Flying west lengthens the day: the body must delay its clock, which is the direction it drifts naturally (its free-running period is about 25 hours), so adjustment is easier — roughly 1.5 hours per day. Flying east shortens the day and the clock must be advanced, which is harder — roughly 1 hour per day. Until the clock resynchronises the result is circadian dysrhythmia: poor sleep, daytime fatigue, reduced performance and digestive upset. On a short stopover (under about 24 hours) stay on home time; on a long one, adopt local time and seek daylight.

The Stress Model — Perceived Demand vs Perceived Ability

hp-19Original redraw

Stress comes from the mismatch a person perceives, not from the task itself. The mind weighs the perceived demand of a situation against its perceived ability to cope. If the two balance, the person is comfortable and performs well. If demand appears to exceed ability, stress results — and it is the perception that counts: an experienced pilot and a novice face the same crosswind but feel very different stress. Feedback matters too: coping successfully raises perceived ability for next time, while failure lowers it. Training, preparation and experience all work by raising perceived ability.

Arousal & Performance — The Inverted U

hp-20Original redraw

Performance is best at a moderate level of arousal (the Yerkes–Dodson law). When arousal is too low — boredom, a long quiet cruise, fatigue — attention wanders and events are missed. As arousal rises performance improves to an optimum. Beyond that, over-arousal degrades it: attention narrows onto one item (tunnelling or fixation), other information is shed, and in the extreme the pilot freezes or panics. The optimum is lower for complex or unfamiliar tasks than for simple, well-practised ones, which is why emergencies are trained until the response is automatic.

Sources of Stress — Stressors

hp-21Original redraw

Stress is cumulative. A stressor is anything that produces stress, and the effects of different stressors add together: a pilot already loaded by a domestic problem and a poor night’s sleep has less capacity left for a technical failure. Environmental (physical) stressors include heat, noise, vibration and low humidity. Physiological ones include fatigue, hunger and illness. Psychological stressors include fear, workload and time pressure. Life stress — bereavement, divorce, money, moving house — is carried into the cockpit. The comfortable cockpit is about 21°C with 40–60% relative humidity.

The Digestive System

hp-18Original redraw

Digestion turns food into fuel. Food passes from the mouth down the oesophagus to the stomach, where acid and enzymes begin to break it down. Most nutrients are absorbed in the small intestine, helped by bile from the liver and enzymes from the pancreas; the large intestine recovers water. Carbohydrate becomes glucose, the brain’s only fuel: a missed meal can cause hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar) — headache, shakiness, poor concentration. For flight: gas in the gut expands with altitude (avoid fizzy drinks and gas-forming food), and food poisoning is a leading cause of in-flight incapacitation, which is why the two pilots eat different meals.

The Information-Processing Model

hp-24Original redraw

How a pilot turns a stimulus into an action. The senses detect a stimulus and hold it briefly in sensory memory (about 1 second for sight, up to 8 for sound). Perception gives it meaning, coloured by experience and expectation. The central decision-maker then chooses a response — it is a single channel, handling one conscious decision at a time, so attention is the limiting resource. It draws on working (short-term) memory, which holds about 7 ± 2 items for 10–20 seconds unless rehearsed, and on long-term memory. The response is carried out and feedback confirms the result, closing the loop.

Geometric Optical Illusions

hp-28Original redraw

The eye measures by comparison, and context fools it. In the Müller-Lyer figure the two shafts are the same length, but the one with outward-pointing fins looks longer. In the Ponzo figure two identical bars lie between converging lines; the upper one looks longer because the brain reads the converging lines as depth — exactly the cue used to judge a runway on approach. In the vertical–horizontal illusion a vertical line looks longer than an equal horizontal one. The lesson for flying: judgements of size, distance and slope made by eye are unreliable when the surroundings are unfamiliar, so cross-check with instruments.

Impossible Figures

hp-29Original redraw

The brain insists on building a 3-D object from a 2-D picture. An impossible figure such as the Penrose triangle is drawn so that each corner, taken alone, is a perfectly reasonable solid joint — yet the three corners cannot belong to the same object. The eye accepts each part and only notices the contradiction when it tries to follow the whole shape round. It shows that perception is an active construction based on assumptions, not a recording, and that the brain will happily supply a confident interpretation that is wrong. In flight the equivalent is building a convincing mental picture from incomplete cues and failing to test it.

Expectation — Seeing What We Expect

hp-25Original redraw

Read the phrase in the triangle, then read it again word by word. Most people read “a bird in the hand” and miss the repeated word, because the brain recognises a familiar pattern and fills in what it expects instead of processing what is actually there. This is expectancy (or “mental set”), and it is strongest when we are busy, tired or doing something routine. In the cockpit it produces the classic errors: hearing the clearance you expected, not the one given; seeing three greens because the gear is always down by now; reading back a frequency or level incorrectly without noticing. Defence: read the instrument, do not assume it — and cross-check with the other pilot.

The Basic-T Instrument Layout & Scan

hp-49Original redraw

The standard layout puts the key instruments where the eye needs them. The attitude indicator is top centre — it is the master instrument and the hub of the scan. The airspeed indicator is to its left, the altimeter to its right and the heading indicator directly below: together they form the basic T, the same in every aircraft so that a pilot’s scan transfers between types. The turn co-ordinator and vertical speed indicator fill the lower corners. The usual scan is selective radial: start at the attitude indicator, glance out to one instrument, return to the attitude indicator, then out to the next.

Three-Pointer Altimeter — The 10 000 ft Misreading

hp-50Original redraw

A notorious design-induced error. The three-pointer altimeter uses a long pointer for hundreds of feet, a short pointer for thousands and a third, very small pointer for tens of thousands. The small pointer is easily overlooked or hidden by the others, so the instrument can be misread by exactly 10 000 ft — and it has caused fatal accidents, with crews descending into terrain believing they were 10 000 ft higher. The reading here is 13 460 ft; a hurried glance gives 3 460 ft. Modern altimeters fix it with a counter-pointer display (digits for thousands, one pointer for hundreds) and a striped low-altitude flag below 10 000 ft.

Glass-Cockpit Primary Flight Display

hp-51Original redraw

The basic T, rebuilt on one screen. The attitude display stays in the centre. The round airspeed dial becomes a vertical speed tape on the left and the altimeter an altitude tape on the right, each with a fixed window showing the current value in digits; heading runs along the bottom and vertical speed down the right edge. The advantages are a compact scan and extra cues (trend arrows, speed limits, flight-mode annunciations). The cost is that a moving tape gives rate and trend information less intuitively than a pointer sweeping round a dial, and that the flight-mode annunciator must be read deliberately — mode confusion is the characteristic error of the glass cockpit.

Pattern Scanning — Spotting the Odd Gauge

hp-52Original redraw

The eye is very good at spotting the one that does not fit. If a bank of gauges is arranged so that every needle points the same way when things are normal — typically 9 o’clock or 12 o’clock — then an abnormal reading stands out instantly as a break in the pattern, and the whole panel can be checked at a glance without reading a single number. If each gauge’s normal position is different, every one must be read and compared with a remembered value, which is slow and error-prone. This is why engine instruments are aligned on multi-engine aircraft and why pilots cross-check “all needles matched”.

Airline Accident Rate Over Time

hp-01Original redraw

Flying became dramatically safer, then the improvement slowed. The accident rate per million departures fell steeply through the 1950s and 1960s as jet engines, pressurisation, radar and better navigation aids removed many technical causes. From about the 1970s the curve flattened: the aircraft had become so reliable that the remaining accidents were mostly due to people — the crew, and the organisation behind them. Because traffic keeps growing, a flat rate would mean a rising number of accidents, so further gains have to come from human factors: crew resource management, better procedures, error-tolerant design and safety management.

Causes of Accidents — The Human Factor

hp-03Original redraw

About 70% of aircraft accidents are attributed to human factors — the proportion usually quoted is 70–75%. The remainder are mainly technical failures and environmental causes such as weather. The human share is so large not because pilots have become worse but because the machine has become so reliable. And “human factors” is wider than pilot error: it includes maintenance, air traffic control, design, training and management. Most accidents have several contributing causes in a chain, and breaking any one link would have prevented them — which is the whole purpose of studying human performance.

The Swiss-Cheese Model (Reason)

hp-04Original redraw

Accidents happen when the holes line up. James Reason pictured an organisation’s defences as slices of Swiss cheese. Each layer — organisational decisions, supervision, the preconditions of the day (fatigue, weather, pressure) and the acts of the crew — has weaknesses, the holes. Usually a hazard that passes one layer is stopped by the next. An accident needs holes in every layer to align at once. Latent failures are holes created long before, by designers and managers, lying dormant; active failures are the errors and violations of front-line people, with immediate effect. Blaming the last person in the chain leaves the latent holes in place.

Error-Tolerant vs Vulnerable Systems

hp-54Original redraw

People will always make errors; a good system survives them. In a vulnerable system a single error leads straight to an unsafe outcome — nothing stands between the slip and the accident. An error-tolerant system is designed on the assumption that errors will occur: it makes them hard to commit (guards, interlocks, clear design), easy to detect (warnings, cross-checks by a second pilot, checklists) and recoverable before harm is done (margins, time to correct). Examples: the gear lever that cannot be raised on the ground, the altitude alerter, the read-back of a clearance, the two-pilot challenge-and-response checklist.

Design-Induced Error — Control/Display Compatibility

hp-55Original redraw

Good design makes the right action the natural one. When the arrangement of the controls matches the arrangement of the displays they affect (spatial compatibility), the pilot reaches for the correct one without thinking. When it does not — four displays in a square but their knobs in a row — the pilot has to remember an arbitrary mapping, and under stress will sooner or later operate the wrong one. Controls should also move in the expected direction (forward or up for “more”), and controls that must not be confused should feel different — hence the wheel-shaped gear lever and the flap-shaped flap lever. Errors invited by the equipment are design-induced.

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

hp-26Original redraw

Needs are satisfied from the bottom up. Maslow arranged human motivation as a hierarchy: a person is driven first by physiological needs (air, food, water, sleep), then safety and security, then belonging (love, acceptance by the group), then esteem (respect, recognition, self-respect) and finally self-fulfilment (achieving one’s potential — “self-actualisation”). A lower need must be reasonably satisfied before the next one becomes a motivator, and if a lower need is threatened attention drops back to it. A crew member who is exhausted, frightened or feels excluded is not going to be motivated by pride in the job.

Motivation — Effort, Performance & Reward

hp-27Original redraw

People put in effort when they expect it to pay off. In this model effort leads to performance only if the person also has the ability and understands the task. Performance leads to a reward — extrinsic (pay, promotion, praise) or intrinsic (the satisfaction of a job well done). If the reward is seen as fair and worth having it produces satisfaction, which feeds back and strengthens the willingness to make the effort next time. Break any link — no ability, no recognition, a reward that is not valued — and motivation falls. Performance is roughly ability multiplied by motivation; too much motivation (“press-on-itis”) is as dangerous as too little.

Somatotypes — Body Types

hp-44Original redraw

Sheldon’s three body types. The endomorph is round and soft with a tendency to store fat; the mesomorph is muscular and broad-shouldered; the ectomorph is thin and lightly built. Sheldon believed each type went with a temperament — easy-going, assertive and reserved respectively — but that link is not supported by evidence and is no longer accepted. What does matter in aviation is body size: cockpits are designed by anthropometry to fit the range from the 5th to the 95th percentile, and the seat is adjusted to bring every pilot’s eyes to the design eye position. Body mass index (weight ÷ height²) over 30 is classed as obese, a risk factor for heart disease and diabetes.

Eysenck's Personality Dimensions

hp-45Original redraw

Two independent scales describe personality. Eysenck measured it along an extravert–introvert axis (outgoing and sociable, or quiet and reserved) and a stable–unstable axis (calm and even-tempered, or anxious and moody; the unstable end is also called neurotic). The four quadrants correspond to the classical temperaments. The stable extravert — sociable, easy-going, responsive — is the type most often associated with a good pilot and crew member, though a stable introvert is equally safe. Personality is largely fixed by early adulthood and changes little; attitudes and behaviour, by contrast, can be changed by training.

Leadership Styles — Goal vs Person Orientation

hp-46Original redraw

Two things a commander must care about. Goal-directed (G) behaviour is concern for getting the task done; person-directed (P) behaviour is concern for the people doing it. A leader high on both, P+ G+, is the ideal: the job is done and the crew are involved and willing to speak up. P− G+ is the autocrat: efficient but overbearing, the crew stop offering information. P+ G− is too democratic: a pleasant atmosphere but no direction and decisions drift. P− G− is laissez-faire: the leader has opted out. A good commander shifts style with the situation — more directive in an emergency, more consultative when time allows.

Cockpit Authority Gradient

hp-47Original redraw

The “slope” of authority between the two pilots. A gradient that is too steep — a domineering captain and a timid or very junior first officer — stops the first officer questioning a mistake; many accidents show a co-pilot who saw the error and said nothing, or said it too gently. A gradient that is too flat — two captains together, or a captain who will not lead — means nobody takes the decision, or both act at once. The optimum is a moderate slope: the captain is clearly in command but the first officer feels free, and is expected, to speak up. Captains set it by inviting input; first officers keep it by stating concerns clearly.

The SHELL Model

hp-48Original redraw

Human factors is about interfaces. In the SHELL model the human operator — Liveware — is at the centre, and must fit with four surrounding elements. L–S, Software: procedures, checklists, manuals, symbology. L–H, Hardware: the machine — seats, controls, displays. L–E, Environment: noise, temperature, weather, time of day, the organisation’s climate. L–L, Liveware: other people — crew, ATC, ground staff. The edges of the blocks are drawn ragged because people vary; the other elements must be matched to the human, not the other way round. A mismatch at any interface is where human error arises. (Hawkins’ model, developed from Edwards.)
