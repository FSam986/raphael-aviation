Radio Waves — Wavelength, Period & Frequency

rn_1964Original redraw

A radio wave is a repeating oscillation. One complete oscillation is a cycle. The period (T) is the time one cycle takes; the frequency (f) is the number of cycles per second, in hertz, so f = 1 ÷ T. The wavelength (λ) is the distance travelled during one cycle. All radio waves travel at the speed of light, c = 300 000 000 m/s, which links the two: λ = c ÷ f. Higher frequency means shorter wavelength: 300 kHz is 1 000 m, 300 MHz is 1 m. Amplitude is the maximum displacement from the mean and represents the strength of the signal.

Amplitude Modulation vs Frequency Modulation

rn_12037Original redraw

Modulation impresses information (an audio tone, speech, data) onto a steady radio-frequency carrier. In amplitude modulation (AM) the carrier’s amplitude rises and falls with the signal while its frequency stays constant — simple, but vulnerable to static. In frequency modulation (FM) the carrier’s frequency swings above and below its mean while its amplitude stays constant — much less affected by static, but needs more bandwidth and more complex receivers. VHF aviation voice and the NDB use AM; the VOR reference signal uses FM.

Radio Magnetic Indicator (RMI)

rn_13186Original redraw

The RMI combines heading and bearing in one display. The compass card is driven by the gyro-magnetic compass, so the aircraft’s magnetic heading is always under the top index. The needle points at the station (NDB or VOR): its head reads the QDM — the magnetic track to fly to the station — and its tail reads the QDR, the magnetic bearing from the station (the radial, for a VOR). No mental arithmetic is needed, unlike a fixed-card ADF where relative bearing must be added to heading. Here the heading is 090°, the QDM is 270° and the QDR is 090°.

Radio Bearings — QDM, QDR, QTE & QUJ

rn_12044Original redraw

Four ways to state one bearing line. Measured at the station, QDR is the magnetic bearing of the aircraft from the station and QTE the true bearing from the station. Their reciprocals are flown by the pilot: QDM, the magnetic heading to steer to the station in still air, and QUJ, the true track to the station. Here the aircraft is south-east of the station: QDR 110°, so QDM 290°. With 10°W variation at the station, QTE = 110° − 10° = 100° and QUJ 280°. VDF bearings are classed A ±2°, B ±5°, C ±10°, D worse than C.

VOR — Phase Comparison

rn_1965Original redraw

How a VOR gives bearing. The station transmits two 30 Hz signals. The reference signal has the same phase in every direction (it is frequency-modulated on a 9 960 Hz sub-carrier). The variable signal comes from a rotating pattern, so its phase is different in every direction (amplitude-modulated). The two are in phase on magnetic north, and the variable signal lags by one degree for every degree clockwise round the station. The receiver measures the phase difference, which equals the radial — the magnetic bearing from the station: 90° of phase difference means the 090 radial.

VOR — Cone of Confusion

rn_13192Original redraw

Directly above the station there is no usable signal. The VOR aerial radiates very little energy vertically, leaving an inverted cone overhead — the cone of confusion. The system must give usable signals up to at least 40° of elevation, so the cone can extend up to 50° either side of the vertical; its width grows with height (at 30 000 ft it can be several miles across). Passing through it, the CDI needle swings from side to side, the TO/FROM flag changes and the OFF flag may show briefly. Hold the heading and wait for the indications to settle on the far side.

VOR Course Deviation Indicator (CDI) & OBS

rn_13193Original redraw

Reading the CDI. The pilot selects a course with the omni bearing selector (OBS); it appears under the top index. The deviation needle shows whether the selected course is to the left or right: fly towards the needle to regain it. Full-scale deflection is 10° either side, so each of the five dots is 2°. The TO/FROM flag shows whether the selected course would take the aircraft to or from the station. The indication is independent of heading — it depends only on where the aircraft is. Here course 090 is selected, flag TO, needle two dots right: the aircraft is 4° left of course.

Tracking a VOR Radial

rn_13197Original redraw

The needle shows where the track is. With the correct course selected and the aircraft heading roughly along it, the CDI is a command instrument: needle centred, on track; needle left, the track is to the left — turn left to intercept; needle right, turn right. Once back on track, apply a wind-correction angle and keep the needle centred. If the reciprocal course were selected by mistake, the needle would work in the reverse sense — always check the TO/FROM flag agrees with the direction of flight.

VOR Course Deviation — Degrees Off Track

rn_13201Original redraw

Converting dots to degrees and distance. Each dot on the CDI is 2°. Here the course selected is 360° FROM the station and the aircraft is actually on the 006 radial: it is 6° right of the required course, so the needle is three dots left. To turn degrees into distance use the 1-in-60 rule: one degree is one nautical mile off track at 60 nm, so 6° at 30 nm from the station is 3 nm off track. The deviation is angular, so the same number of dots means a smaller distance error closer to the station.

ILS Localiser — 90 Hz and 150 Hz Lobes

rn_12038Original redraw

The localiser gives guidance in azimuth. Its aerial is at the far (stop) end of the runway and radiates two overlapping lobes on one carrier in the band 108–111.975 MHz (odd first decimals). Seen from an approaching aircraft, the lobe on the left is modulated at 90 Hz and the lobe on the right at 150 Hz. The receiver compares the depth of the two tones: equal means on the centreline; more 90 Hz means the aircraft is left of it, and the needle shows fly right. Coverage is 25 nm within ±10° of the centreline and 17 nm out to ±35°.

Marker Beacons — Patterns & Identification

rn_13216Original redraw

Marker beacons transmit a narrow vertical beam on 75 MHz, heard and seen only when the aircraft is directly overhead. The beam is fan-shaped — narrow along track, wide across it — and may be elliptical or “bone” shaped in plan; a Z marker is a small circular cone, once used over a range station. On an ILS: the outer marker (about 4 nm from the threshold) gives low-pitched dashes and a blue light; the middle marker (about 1 050 m) alternate dots and dashes, amber; the inner marker (75–450 m) high-pitched dots, white. Many are now replaced by a DME.

ILS Approach Profile

rn_13217Original redraw

The ILS in the vertical plane. The glide path transmitter (329.15–335 MHz, UHF, paired automatically with the localiser frequency) sits about 300 m in from the threshold beside the runway and defines a slope, normally 3°, that crosses the threshold at about 50 ft. The aircraft intercepts it from below, at the final approach point, to avoid false glide paths that exist at steeper multiples of the true angle. The outer marker gives a height check at about 4 nm, the middle marker at about 1 050 m (roughly the CAT I decision height), and the inner marker close to the threshold. Rule of thumb: on a 3° slope, height is about 300 ft per nm.

Airborne Radar — Beam & Ground Illumination

rn_12041Original redraw

Radar measures range by timing an echo: a pulse travels out and back at the speed of light, so range = (c × time) ÷ 2. What the aircraft measures is the slant range along the beam, not the distance along the ground. The beamwidth depends on wavelength and aerial size (roughly 70 × λ ÷ D degrees), so a small airborne dish needs a short wavelength — about 3 cm (9 375 MHz, X-band) — to make a narrow beam. The beam illuminates a footprint on the surface that grows with range; two targets inside one beamwidth merge into a single echo. Tilting the beam down maps the ground; tilting it up looks for weather.

Radar Aerial Feed Types

rn_13234Original redraw

A parabolic reflector turns a point source into a beam. Energy leaving the focus of a parabola is reflected as parallel rays, so the job of the feed is to put the transmitter’s energy at the focus. A dipole feed is a small aerial at the focus with a reflector plate behind it. A horn feed is the open, flared end of a waveguide pointing at the dish. A Cassegrain feed radiates from a horn at the centre of the dish onto a small convex sub-reflector at the focus, which throws it back onto the main dish — keeping the heavy waveguide short and behind the dish. Modern weather radars use a flat-plate (slotted planar array) aerial instead, with fewer side lobes.
