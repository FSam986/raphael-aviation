Semi-Circular Cruising Levels (below FL290)

al_126_01Original redraw

The semi-circular rule separates opposite-direction traffic by its magnetic track. On tracks 000°–179° (eastbound) IFR flights use odd flight levels (FL30, 50, 70…); on 180°–359° (westbound) they use even levels (FL40, 60, 80…). VFR flights fly 500 ft higher: odd + 500 eastbound (FL35, 55, 75…), even + 500 westbound (FL45, 65, 85…). This gives 1 000 ft between opposite-direction IFR traffic and 500 ft between IFR and VFR. Memory aid: “east is odd”.

Cruising Levels from FL290 — RVSM and Non-RVSM

al_126_02Original redraw

At and above FL290 the spacing depends on whether RVSM (reduced vertical separation minimum) applies. In RVSM airspace, FL290–FL410, separation stays at 1 000 ft: eastbound odd levels FL290, 310, 330 … 410; westbound even levels FL300, 320 … 400. Where RVSM does not apply, and always above FL410, separation is 2 000 ft: eastbound FL290, 330, 370, 410, 450; westbound FL310, 350, 390, 430. RVSM needs approved aircraft: two independent altimeters, an altitude-hold autopilot, an altitude alerter and a mode C transponder.

Transition Altitude — Climbing

al_229_01Original redraw

Transition altitude (TA) is the altitude at or below which the vertical position of an aircraft is controlled by reference to altitude (QNH set). Climbing through the TA, the pilot changes the altimeter from QNH to the standard setting 1013.25 hPa and from then on reports flight levels. The TA is fixed for each aerodrome or State, published in the AIP and on charts, and should be as low as possible but normally not below 3 000 ft.

Transition Level — Descending

al_230_01Original redraw

Transition level (TL) is the lowest flight level available for use above the transition altitude. Descending through the TL, the pilot changes from 1013.25 hPa to the aerodrome QNH and from then on reports altitude. Unlike the TA, the TL is not fixed: it varies with the QNH of the day and is determined by ATC (the approach controller or aerodrome), then passed to pilots in the approach clearance or on the ATIS.

Transition Layer

al_231_01Original redraw

The transition layer is the airspace between the transition altitude and the transition level. Its depth varies with QNH but is normally at least a nominal 1 000 ft, so that aircraft on QNH below and on 1013.25 hPa above stay separated. Aircraft do not cruise in the layer. Climbing through it, vertical position is reported as a flight level; descending through it, as an altitude.

Declared Distances — TORA, TODA, ASDA & LDA

al_381_01Original redraw

Four distances are declared for every runway direction. TORA is the length of runway available for the take-off ground run. TODA adds the clearway — an obstacle-free area beyond the runway over which the aircraft may make its initial climb to 35 ft — so TODA = TORA + clearway. ASDA adds the stopway, a prepared area for stopping after a rejected take-off: ASDA = TORA + stopway. LDA is the runway available for landing, measured from the threshold; a displaced threshold shortens it.

Runway-Holding Position Marking — Pattern A

al_407_01Original redraw

Pattern A marks the runway-holding position closest to the runway and is the one used for visual operations. It is four yellow lines across the taxiway: two solid lines on the holding (taxiway) side and two broken lines on the runway side. An aircraft approaching the solid lines must stop and may not cross without ATC clearance; coming off the runway, the broken lines are met first and may be crossed. A red mandatory sign with the runway designator stands beside the marking. (Pattern B, a “ladder”, marks CAT II/III holding positions further back.)

Aerodrome Signs — Mandatory & Information

al_411_01Original redraw

Two families of sign. Mandatory instruction signs have white characters on a red background: runway designation at a holding position, CAT I/II/III holding positions, and NO ENTRY. An aircraft must not pass one without ATC clearance. Information signs use yellow and black: a location sign is yellow on black (“where you are”), while direction and destination signs are black on yellow with an arrow (“where it goes”). Memory aid: black square, you’re there; yellow array, points the way.

PAPI — Approach Slope Indications

al_433_01Original redraw

Precision approach path indicator. A wing bar of four lights beside the runway (normally the left) near the touchdown point. Each unit shows white above a set angle and red below it, so the mix tells the pilot where the aircraft is relative to the approach slope (normally 3°): four white — too high; three white, one red — slightly high; two white, two red — on the correct slope; one white, three red — slightly low; four red — too low. The red lights are always the ones nearest the runway.

VASI — Approach Slope Indications

al_434_01Original redraw

Visual approach slope indicator. Two bars of lights beside the runway, a near (downwind) bar and a far (upwind) bar. Each shows white when seen from above its set angle and red from below. Both bars white — too high. Far bar red, near bar white — on the approach slope. Both bars red — too low. Memory aid: “red over white, you’re all right; red over red, you’re dead; white over white, you’ll fly all night.” VASI has largely been replaced by PAPI, which gives finer indications.
