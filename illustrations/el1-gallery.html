Structure of an Atom

ch01_01Original redraw

All matter is made of atoms. At the centre is the nucleus, containing positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons. Around it, in shells, move negatively charged electrons. A normal atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons and so is electrically neutral. Electrons in the outermost shell (the valence electrons) are only loosely held; in a conductor such as copper they drift freely from atom to atom, and that movement of electrons is an electric current. In an insulator they are tightly bound.

Electron Flow vs Conventional Current

ch01_02Original redraw

Two conventions for the direction of current. Electrons are negative, so in a wire they actually flow from the negative terminal, round the circuit, to the positive terminal — electron flow. Long before the electron was discovered, current was defined as flowing from positive to negative, and that conventional current is still the direction used on circuit diagrams, in the motor and generator rules and for arrow symbols on diodes and transistors. Both describe the same current; only the stated direction differs.

Voltage Drives Current — The Water Analogy

ch01_03Original redraw

Voltage is electrical pressure. A tank of water with a high level pushes water through a pipe faster than a tank with a low level; in the same way a higher voltage (potential difference) drives a larger current through the same resistance. Voltage is measured in volts, current — the rate of flow of charge — in amperes, and the opposition to flow, like the narrowness of the pipe, is resistance in ohms. Ohm’s law ties them together: V = I × R.

Resistor Symbols — Fixed & Variable

ch01_04Original redraw

Two drawing styles. A fixed resistor is drawn either as a zigzag (US and older diagrams) or as a rectangle (IEC style). A variable resistor (rheostat) has an arrow drawn diagonally through it, showing that its resistance can be adjusted. A potentiometer has three terminals: the arrow is a sliding contact — the wiper — touching the resistive track, so it can tap off any voltage between the two ends. Resistors are rated in ohms and for the power they can dissipate as heat.

Series Circuit

ch01_05Original redraw

In a series circuit there is only one path, so the same current flows through every component. The total resistance is the sum: R = 4 + 6 + 10 = 20 Ω. The current is I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 20 = 0.6 A. The supply voltage is shared in proportion to resistance: 2.4 V, 3.6 V and 6 V, adding up to 12 V. If any one component fails open-circuit, all of them stop — which is why aircraft loads are not wired in series.

Parallel Circuit

ch01_06Original redraw

In a parallel circuit every branch has the full supply voltage across it, and the currents add. Each branch current is V ÷ R: 12 ÷ 4 = 3 A, 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A, 12 ÷ 10 = 1.2 A; total 6.2 A. The total resistance is found from 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3, and is always less than the smallest branch: here about 1.94 Ω. Each branch works independently, so one failing does not affect the others — which is why aircraft loads are connected in parallel across the bus-bar.

Series-Parallel Circuit

ch01_07Original redraw

Solve it in stages. First reduce the parallel group to one equivalent resistance: 10 Ω and 6 Ω in parallel = (10 × 6) ÷ (10 + 6) = 3.75 Ω (product over sum works for two resistors). Then add the series resistor: 4 + 3.75 = 7.75 Ω. The supply current is 12 ÷ 7.75 = 1.55 A. It all flows through the 4 Ω resistor (dropping 6.2 V), leaving 5.8 V across the parallel pair, which share the current: 0.58 A through 10 Ω and 0.97 A through 6 Ω.

Kirchhoff's Current Law

ch01_08Original redraw

What flows into a junction must flow out. Charge cannot pile up at a point, so at any junction the sum of the currents entering equals the sum leaving: here I3 = I1 + I2. It is the reason the currents in parallel branches add up to the supply current, and the basis of the reverse-current and differential protection used in aircraft generator systems.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law

ch01_09Original redraw

Round any closed loop, the voltage drops add up to the supply voltage. With 2 Ω, 4 Ω and 6 Ω in series across 12 V, the current is 12 ÷ 12 = 1 A, and the drops are 2 V, 4 V and 6 V — which total 12 V. The largest resistance takes the largest share of the voltage. The law is used to find the voltage at any point in a circuit and explains why a high-resistance connection (a corroded terminal) steals voltage from the load.

Two- and Three-Position Switches

ch02_01Original redraw

Toggle switches are the commonest cockpit switch. A two-position switch is simply ON or OFF: one pole, one throw (SPST). A three-position switch has a centre OFF and two ON positions, each routing the supply to a different circuit — for example IN / OFF / OUT for a reversible actuator, or a landing light that may be TAXI / OFF / LAND. Some positions are spring-loaded to return to OFF (momentary), and important switches may be gated or guarded so they cannot be moved accidentally.

Guarded Switches & Switch-Lights

ch02_02Original redraw

Preventing the wrong selection. A guarded switch has a hinged cover that must be lifted before the switch can be moved — used for irreversible or critical selections such as fuel cut-off or fire handles; often the guard holds the switch in its normal position when closed. A switch-light (push-button annunciator) combines a push-switch with a lit legend: the upper and lower halves can show status such as ON, OFF, FAULT or OPEN. On large aircraft a flow-bar lit inside the switch shows whether a valve or circuit in a synoptic line is open.

Microswitch — Internal Construction

ch02_03Original redraw

A small, precise snap-action switch. A tiny movement of the operating plunger — often less than a millimetre — makes a spring-loaded moving contact snap over from one fixed contact to the other. The snap action gives a fast, clean make-and-break regardless of how slowly the plunger moves, which reduces arcing. Most have three terminals: common, normally open and normally closed. Microswitches are used as limit switches in actuators, landing-gear and door position sensing, and throttle and flap position switches.

Proximity Switch / Sensor

ch02_04Original redraw

Switching without contact. A proximity sensor contains a coil fed with alternating current by its electronics unit. When a ferrous target fixed to the moving part comes within a few millimetres, it changes the inductance of the coil; the electronics detect the change and switch an output to “target near” or “target far”. With no moving contacts there is nothing to wear, stick or arc, and the sensor is sealed against water, ice and dirt — so proximity sensors have largely replaced microswitches for landing-gear, door and flap position sensing on modern aircraft.

Magnetic (Variable-Reluctance) Speed Pickup

ch02_05Original redraw

Measuring speed with a passing tooth. The pickup is a permanent magnet with a pole piece and a coil, mounted facing a toothed wheel of ferrous metal. As each tooth passes, the gap — and so the magnetic reluctance — changes, the flux through the coil rises and falls, and a small voltage pulse is induced. The pulse frequency is proportional to the wheel’s speed. The device needs no power supply and no contact, and is used for engine N1/N2 speed signals and the wheel-speed transducers of anti-skid systems.

Proximity Sensors on the Landing Gear

ch02_06Original redraw

Where the sensors go. On a typical landing gear, proximity sensors and their targets detect: down-locked (on the side brace lock), up-locked (at the uplock hook in the wheel bay), and weight on wheels — the oleo compressed under load, the air/ground signal. The signals feed the gear indicating lights, the warning system and many other systems: the air/ground logic inhibits gear retraction on the ground, arms spoilers and thrust reversers, and switches pressurisation, anti-skid and other modes between air and ground.

Fuse Construction

ch03_01Original redraw

A fuse is a deliberate weak link. A fuse element of low-melting-point metal is enclosed in a glass or ceramic barrel between two metal end caps; larger fuses are packed with sand to quench the arc. If the current exceeds the rating, the element heats and melts, breaking the circuit and protecting the wiring. Fuses are rated in amperes; they protect the wiring, not the equipment, and must be replaced with one of the same rating. Aircraft usually carry spare fuses — typically 10% or at least three of each rating used.

High-Current Fuse — Current Limiter

ch03_02Original redraw

A slow-blow fuse for heavy circuits. A current limiter has a thick copper element with a narrowed section, bolted into the circuit by its two lugs. Copper has a high melting point and a large thermal mass, so it carries a short overload — such as the inrush current when a motor starts or a generator is connected — without blowing, but melts under a sustained overload or fault. It is used to protect heavy-current feeders, for example between the battery or generator and the main bus-bar, where an ordinary fuse would blow needlessly.

Circuit Breaker Construction

ch03_03Original redraw

A fuse that can be reset. In a thermal circuit breaker the current flows through a bimetallic strip of two metals that expand at different rates. A sustained overload heats the strip, it bends, releases a spring-loaded latch and the contacts snap open; the push-pull button pops out, showing a white band. It can be reset by pushing it in once it has cooled — but a breaker that trips again must not be reset repeatedly in flight. Breakers are “trip-free”: the contacts cannot be held closed against a fault. The button can also be pulled to isolate a circuit manually.

Capacitor Construction

ch03_04Original redraw

Two conductors separated by an insulator. A capacitor is two metal plates with an insulating dielectric (air, paper, mica, ceramic, plastic film) between them. Connected to a supply, electrons pile up on one plate and leave the other, storing energy in the electric field across the dielectric. Capacitance, measured in farads (practical values in µF, nF and pF), is increased by larger plates, a thinner dielectric, or a dielectric with a higher permittivity: C = εA ÷ d. A capacitor blocks steady DC but passes AC.

Capacitor Symbols

ch03_05Original redraw

Reading the symbols. A capacitor is drawn as two parallel plates. A polarised (electrolytic) capacitor has one plate marked + (or drawn curved) and must be connected the right way round — reversed, it can overheat and burst. A variable capacitor has a diagonal arrow through it (as for tuning a radio receiver); a preset (trimmer) capacitor has a short bar instead, showing it is adjusted once with a tool and then left.

Capacitors in Series and Parallel

ch03_09Original redraw

The rules are the reverse of those for resistors. Capacitors in parallel simply add, because together they present a larger plate area: C = C1 + C2. Capacitors in series combine like resistors in parallel, because the effective dielectric thickness increases: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2, so the total is always less than the smallest. Two 10 µF capacitors give 20 µF in parallel but only 5 µF in series. Series connection is used to raise the voltage rating of the combination.

Basic Cell — Electrodes & Electrolyte

ch04_01Original redraw

A cell turns chemical energy into electrical energy. Two electrodes of different materials stand in an electrolyte — a liquid or paste that conducts. Chemical reactions at the electrodes leave one with a surplus of electrons (the negative terminal) and the other with a deficit (positive), producing a potential difference. Connect a load and electrons flow through it from negative to positive while the reaction continues. The voltage depends only on the materials (about 1.5 V for a dry cell, 2 V for lead-acid, 1.2 V for nickel-cadmium); the size of the cell sets its capacity.

Dry (Primary) Cell

ch04_02Original redraw

The common torch battery. The zinc case is the negative electrode; a central carbon rod is the positive, surrounded by a depolariser of manganese dioxide; between them is an electrolyte in paste form, so it cannot spill — hence “dry”. It gives about 1.5 V. It is a primary cell: the chemical action is not reversible, so once exhausted it must be discarded and cannot be recharged. Primary cells are used in torches, emergency locator transmitters and some standby equipment.

Cells in Series vs Parallel

ch04_03Original redraw

Series raises voltage; parallel raises capacity. Cells in series (positive to negative) add their voltages but the capacity stays that of one cell: three 2 V, 10 Ah cells give 6 V, 10 Ah. Cells in parallel (positive to positive) keep the voltage of one cell but add their capacities: 2 V, 30 Ah. A 24 V aircraft lead-acid battery is 12 cells of 2 V in series; a 24 V nickel-cadmium battery is 19 or 20 cells of 1.2 V in series.

Lead-Acid Cell Construction

ch04_04Original redraw

Inside a lead-acid cell. Two groups of plates are interleaved: the positive plates carry lead peroxide (dark brown) and the negative plates spongy lead (grey). There is always one more negative plate, so every positive plate works on both faces. Porous separators stop the plates touching, and the whole stack stands in dilute sulphuric acid. Each group is joined to a terminal post. A vent cap lets hydrogen and oxygen escape while charging — they form an explosive mixture, so batteries must be well ventilated — and in aircraft is non-spill.

Lead-Acid Cell — Charge & Discharge

ch04_05Original redraw

The chemistry tells you the state of charge. Charged: positive plate lead peroxide, negative plate spongy lead, electrolyte strong sulphuric acid with a specific gravity of about 1.270–1.285. On discharge both plates turn to lead sulphate and the acid is used up, leaving more water, so the specific gravity falls to about 1.170 or less. The state of charge of a lead-acid cell can therefore be checked with a hydrometer. Each cell gives about 2 V (2.2 V at full charge). A lead-acid battery must not be left discharged or the sulphate hardens and the plates are ruined.

Secondary Batteries — Lead-Acid vs Nickel-Cadmium

ch04_09Original redraw

Two kinds of rechargeable aircraft battery. Lead-acid is cheaper and simpler; its state of charge shows in the specific gravity. Nickel-cadmium (alkaline) is lighter for its capacity, delivers very high currents for engine starting and holds its voltage almost to the end of discharge — but its electrolyte’s specific gravity does not change with charge, so a hydrometer is no use, and it is prone to thermal runaway: a rising temperature lowers its internal resistance, increasing the charging current and heating it further. Ni-Cd batteries have temperature monitoring. Never mix the two in one battery room: acid fumes destroy alkaline cells and vice versa. Spills are neutralised with sodium bicarbonate (acid) or boric acid (alkali).

Magnetic Field Patterns & Poles

ch05_01Original redraw

Lines of magnetic flux leave a magnet at its north pole and enter at its south pole, forming closed loops through the magnet. They never cross, they behave as if under tension (trying to shorten), and they push sideways against each other. Between unlike poles the lines join up and pull the poles together: attraction. Between like poles the lines are pushed apart, leaving a neutral point between them, and the poles repel. The density of the lines shows the strength of the field.

Soft Iron in a Magnetic Field

ch05_02Original redraw

Flux takes the easiest path. Soft iron has a high permeability — magnetic flux passes through it far more easily than through air — so a piece of soft iron placed in a field draws the lines of flux into itself and becomes a temporary magnet, with poles induced opposite to those of the magnets nearby. When the field is removed it loses almost all its magnetism. The same effect is used to screen sensitive instruments: a soft-iron case carries stray flux round the instrument instead of through it. It also explains why soft iron in an aircraft causes compass deviation that changes with heading.

Domain Theory — Unmagnetised, Magnetised, Saturated

ch05_03Original redraw

Magnetism comes from tiny aligned regions. A ferromagnetic material is made of microscopic domains, each a tiny magnet. In an unmagnetised bar they point at random and cancel out. Applying a magnetising field turns them into line, and the bar becomes a magnet. When every domain is aligned the material is saturated — a stronger field can add no more magnetism. Hard steel keeps its alignment (a permanent magnet); soft iron loses it when the field is removed. Heating, hammering or an alternating field will scramble the domains and demagnetise the bar.

Magnetic Field Around a Current-Carrying Conductor

ch05_04Original redraw

Every current produces a magnetic field. Around a straight wire carrying current the lines of flux form concentric circles in planes at right angles to the wire, strongest close to it and weakening with distance. The direction is given by the right-hand grip rule: grip the wire with the right hand, thumb pointing in the direction of conventional current, and the fingers curl in the direction of the field. Reverse the current and the field reverses. Increase the current and the field strengthens. This is the basis of every electromagnet, relay, motor and generator.

Current Into and Out of the Paper

ch05_05Original redraw

Drawing a wire end-on. A conductor seen in cross-section is marked with a cross (×) when conventional current flows into the paper — the tail feathers of an arrow going away — and a dot (·) when it flows out of the paper — the point of an arrow coming towards you. By the right-hand grip rule, current into the paper produces a clockwise field and current out of the paper an anticlockwise field. This shorthand is used in every motor and generator diagram.

Force Between Parallel Conductors

ch05_06Original redraw

Currents interact through their fields. Two parallel conductors carrying current in the same direction produce fields that oppose each other between the wires and combine outside them, so the wires are pushed together: they attract. Currents in opposite directions produce fields that combine between the wires, pushing them apart: they repel. The force is proportional to the product of the two currents. Under a heavy fault current the forces between bus-bars or cable cores can be large enough to cause mechanical damage.

Solenoid — The Field of a Coil

ch05_07Original redraw

Coiling the wire concentrates the field. When a conductor is wound into a coil (solenoid), the fields of the individual turns add together inside the coil and produce a field like that of a bar magnet, with a north pole at one end and a south at the other. Its strength — the magnetomotive force — depends on the ampere-turns: the current multiplied by the number of turns. A soft-iron core inside multiplies it many times. The polarity follows the right-hand grip rule applied to the coil: fingers in the direction of current round the turns, thumb points to north.

Solenoid vs Relay

ch05_08Original redraw

Both let a small current switch a large one. A small current from a cockpit switch energises a coil; the magnetism closes contacts in a separate heavy-current circuit, so the heavy cables can be kept short and away from the cockpit. A solenoid (contactor) has a moving core (plunger) drawn into the coil — strong pull, used for heavy loads such as the battery master or starter. A relay has a fixed core that attracts a hinged armature — lighter, faster, often with several sets of contacts. A spring opens the contacts when the coil is de-energised.

Motor Principle — The Catapult Field

ch05_09Original redraw

Why a motor turns. Place a conductor carrying current across the field between two magnet poles. Its own circular field adds to the main field on one side and opposes it on the other. The flux crowds onto the strong side; like stretched rubber bands the lines try to straighten, and they throw the conductor towards the weak side — the catapult field. The force is proportional to flux density × current × length (F = BIL). Its direction is given by Fleming’s left-hand rule; reversing either the field or the current reverses the force.
