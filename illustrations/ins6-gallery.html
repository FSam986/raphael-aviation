Radio Altitude on the Attitude Display

fig_20_03Original redraw

Radio altitude where the pilot is looking. Below about 2 500 ft the radio altitude appears on the attitude display as a digital readout (here 180 ft). The selected decision height (DH) is shown alongside; at DH the display flashes and an aural “minimums” sounds. On many displays a rising runway symbol climbs towards the aircraft symbol below about 200 ft, giving a pictorial sense of height and of lateral (localiser) deviation during an autoland.

FMS Control Display Unit (CDU)

fig_21_02Original redraw

The crew’s keyboard to the FMS. The CDU has a screen with six line-select keys (LSK) down each side for choosing or entering data on that line, and a scratchpad on the bottom line where typed entries appear before being line-selected into place. Function keys call up pages — INIT REF, RTE, DEP ARR, LEGS, HOLD, PROG — and the EXEC key (lit when a change is pending) activates a modified route. Two CDUs allow each pilot to work independently.

EFIS Control Panel

fig_22_02Original redraw

Choosing what the displays show. Each pilot’s EFIS control panel sets their own displays: the mode selector picks the navigation display format — APP, VOR, MAP or PLAN — and centred or expanded (arc) view; the range selector sets the map scale (e.g. 10–640 nm); map buttons add weather radar, stations, waypoints, airports and data; the minimums knob sets the radio (DH) or baro (DA) minimum; and the baro knob sets the altimeter setting.

Complete EFIS — PFD, ND & Control Panel

fig_22_05Original redraw

How the parts fit. The primary flight display (attitude, speed, altitude, heading, flight-mode annunciations) and the navigation display (map, route, weather, traffic) are drawn by a symbol generator (display computer) from the inertial or AHRS attitude, air data, FMS, radio navigation, weather radar and TCAS inputs. The EFIS control panel above selects the ND mode, range and options. If one display fails, its picture can be transferred to the other or to a centre screen.

Electronic HSI — Full Rose

fig_22_06Original redraw

The HSI on a screen. In full-rose (VOR or APP) mode the navigation display reproduces a horizontal situation indicator: a full compass rose with the aircraft heading at the top under the lubber line; the course pointer set to the selected VOR radial or ILS course; the deviation bar showing displacement from it against a dot scale (two dots each side: VOR 2° per dot, full scale 10°; localiser full scale about 2.5°); a TO/FROM indicator; the heading bug; and on an ILS, the glideslope pointer on the right.

Electronic HSI — Arc (Expanded) Mode with Weather

fig_22_07Original redraw

Expanded mode. The arc mode shows only the sector ahead (about 90°) on a larger scale, with the aircraft symbol near the bottom. Weather radar returns can be overlaid: green light precipitation, amber moderate, red heavy, and magenta turbulence (on some radars). Range rings help judge distance. Crews use it to plan deviations — commonly around red cells by at least 20 nm.

Navigation Display — Map Mode

fig_22_10Original redraw

The moving map. In MAP mode the display shows the aircraft symbol at the bottom, normally track up, and the FMS route ahead: the active leg in magenta, other legs white, waypoints as stars, airports and navaids if selected. Top left shows ground speed and TAS, below them wind direction/speed with an arrow; top right the next waypoint, its ETA and distance. A curved trend vector predicts the path over the next 30, 60 and 90 seconds. Range is set on the EFIS panel.

Flight Director System Architecture

fig_25_01Original redraw

A computed steering command. The flight director computer compares where the aircraft is with where the selected mode wants it to be. Inputs: attitude (AHRS/IRS), air data (altitude, speed), radio navigation (VOR, localiser, glideslope), FMS lateral and vertical paths, and the modes and targets set on the mode control panel. Output: pitch and roll command bars on the attitude display. The pilot flies the aircraft to centre the bars; the same commands can be fed to the autopilot.

Flight Director on the PFD

fig_25_03Original redraw

Reading the command bars. On the PFD the flight director appears either as cross-pointer bars (a horizontal pitch bar and a vertical roll bar) or as a V-bar that the aircraft symbol is flown into. Here the pitch bar is above the aircraft symbol and the roll bar to the right: the command is pitch up and roll right. When the bars are centred on the symbol the aircraft is following the selected modes, shown in the flight mode annunciator along the top.

HSI Face Explained

fig_25_04Original redraw

Every part of the HSI. The HSI combines a slaved heading indicator with a VOR/ILS course deviation indicator, so the picture is a plan view: the deviation bar shows where the course is relative to the aircraft symbol. Set the course with the course knob; the bar drifts off-centre towards the side the course lies. Red NAV and HDG flags appear when the signal or heading source is unreliable; the GS pointers hide when no glideslope is received.

Autopilot / FD Mode Control Panel (MCP)

fig_25_06Original redraw

Setting targets and modes. The MCP (or FCU) on the glareshield has a window and knob for each target — IAS/Mach, heading, vertical speed and altitude — and push-buttons for the modes: lateral (HDG SEL, LNAV, VOR LOC, APP) and vertical (LVL CHG, VNAV, V/S, ALT HOLD). Flight director switches sit at each end; autopilot CMD engage buttons and the disengage bar sit on the right. A light in a button shows the mode is selected; the FMA on the PFD confirms what is actually engaged.

FD Command Bars — Climb

fig_25_07Original redraw

Following a climb command. When a climb mode is selected the pitch bar moves up: the pilot raises the nose until the aircraft symbol sits on the bar. Once established at the commanded climb attitude the bar stays centred on the symbol. The FD commands an attitude, not a rate; the climb speed or vertical speed depends on the mode chosen (e.g. LVL CHG holds speed, V/S holds the selected rate).

FD Command Bars — Altitude Hold

fig_25_08Original redraw

Capturing and holding an altitude. As the aircraft nears the selected altitude, the FD changes to ALT ACQ / ALT* and the pitch bar moves down to command a smooth level-off. Once level, the mode becomes ALT HOLD and the bar sits centred; any drift from the altitude moves the bar to command the correction. The level-off begins earlier at higher vertical speeds to keep the manoeuvre gentle.

ILS Glideslope Geometry

fig_25_09Original redraw

The glide path in numbers. The glideslope transmitter beside the touchdown zone radiates a beam normally at 3°, which descends about 300 ft per nm (318 ft/nm). The indicator shows deviation over a total of ±0.7° (full scale, 2 dots) — each dot about 0.35°. On a 3° slope the rate of descent needed is roughly 5 × ground speed (ft/min): 600 ft/min at 120 kt. At 5 nm the aircraft should be about 1 500 ft above the threshold.

Series Autopilot Actuator

fig_26_06Original redraw

Two ways to move a control surface. A series actuator is built into the control run, extending or shortening it, so it moves the surface without moving the pilot’s controls. Its authority is deliberately limited (a few degrees of surface movement), so a runaway is easily overpowered — it suits fast, small corrections such as the yaw damper and stability augmentation. A parallel actuator drives the whole control run, moving the column or rudder pedals as well; it is used for the main autopilot axes and can be overpowered through a clutch or detent.

Autopilot Engage Interlocks

fig_26_07Original redraw

All conditions must be met. The autopilot engage circuit is a series chain of interlocks: correct power supplies, valid attitude and heading reference (gyros up to speed, no flags), valid air data, no trim fault, no excessive attitude or control force, and the engage switch selected. If any link breaks while engaged, the autopilot disconnects with a warning (light and aural) so the pilot knows to take over.

Autopilot Annunciations on the PFD

fig_26_10Original redraw

The PFD tells you what the automation is doing. Along the top of the PFD the flight mode annunciator shows, from left to right, the autothrottle mode, the roll (lateral) mode and the pitch (vertical) mode, with engaged modes in green and armed modes in white beneath. The autopilot/FD status (e.g. CMD, FD, LAND 3) is shown below. A box drawn round a newly changed mode for ten seconds draws the eye. Crews call out every FMA change.

Flight Mode Annunciator (FMA) Explained

fig_26_11Original redraw

Reading the FMA column by column. Autothrottle: N1, SPD, RETARD, THR HOLD. Roll: HDG SEL, LNAV, VOR/LOC; an armed mode (e.g. VOR/LOC) appears in white below the engaged one until captured. Pitch: ALT HOLD, V/S, VNAV, LVL CHG, G/S. AP status: CMD (autopilot), FD (flight director only), or the autoland status (LAND 3, LAND 2, NO AUTOLAND). The FMA, not the MCP lights, is the authority on what is engaged.

Dual-Channel Autopilot Architecture

fig_26_12Original redraw

Making automation fail safe. In a dual-channel system two independent autopilot computers, each with its own sensors and power, compute the same commands. A comparator monitors them: if they disagree, the system disconnects — a fail-passive system: a failure leaves the aircraft in trim with no significant deviation, and the pilot takes over (allows Cat III with a DH). With three channels the odd one out can be voted out and the approach continued — fail-operational, required for the lowest minima (no DH).

Autothrottle System Architecture

fig_28_01Original redraw

Automatic thrust control. The autothrottle computer receives the target — a speed (SPD mode) from the MCP or FMS, or a thrust limit (N1/EPR mode) from the thrust management computer — plus air data (speed, Mach) and engine data (N1 or EPR). It drives a servo motor that physically moves the thrust levers through a clutch, so the pilot sees and can override the movement. Modes include take-off/go-around thrust, climb thrust, speed hold and RETARD at flare.

Dutch Roll — Stable, Neutral & Unstable

fig_29_01Original redraw

How the oscillation develops. Dutch roll is a coupled yawing and rolling oscillation. If the aircraft’s directional (weathercock) stability is strong relative to its lateral (dihedral) stability, the oscillation dies away (stable). If they are balanced it continues at constant amplitude (neutral); if lateral stability dominates — common in swept-wing jets at high altitude — it grows (unstable). Swept-wing airliners therefore need a yaw damper.

Dutch Roll — Yaw-Roll Coupling

fig_29_02Original redraw

Why yaw causes roll. If a swept-wing aircraft yaws right, the left wing advances: it meets the airflow more nearly at right angles (less effective sweep) and moves slightly faster, so it produces more lift and the aircraft rolls right. The resulting sideslip and the fin’s weathercock stability then yaw it back to the left, which rolls it left — and the cycle repeats. The wingtip traces a figure-of-eight like a skater, giving the name.

Yaw Damper Washout Filter

fig_29_04Original redraw

Damping the oscillation without fighting turns. A rate gyro senses all yaw rate — including the steady yaw rate of a normal turn. If the yaw damper opposed that, it would apply rudder against every turn. A washout (high-pass) filter removes slowly-changing, steady signals and passes only the oscillating dutch-roll component, so the damper corrects the oscillation and ignores the steady turn.

Yaw Damper Signal Chain

fig_29_05Original redraw

From gyro to rudder. A yaw rate gyro (or the inertial reference) senses yaw rate; the washout filter removes the steady-turn component; the yaw damper computer amplifies the remaining signal (with gain scheduled for airspeed) and drives a series actuator in the rudder control run, which moves the rudder without moving the pedals. Position feedback from the actuator closes the loop. A yaw damper indicator shows the rudder movement; most jets need the yaw damper engaged for flight at high altitude.

Flight Warning Display Layout (ECAM)

fig_32_01Original redraw

Where the warnings appear. On an ECAM-type upper display — the engine/warning display — the top shows the primary engine parameters (N1, EGT, N2, fuel flow), fuel on board and flap/slat position. The lower part carries the warning and caution messages on the left, with the actions to take as a checklist, and memo or status messages on the right. The lower display (system display) automatically shows the synoptic page of the affected system.

Central Warning System Architecture

fig_32_02Original redraw

One system to alert the crew. Sensors in every aircraft system send their status to the flight warning computers (two, for redundancy). These prioritise the alerts and drive the outputs: the MASTER WARNING (red) or MASTER CAUTION (amber) lights in front of each pilot, the aural alerts (bell, chime, voice), and the messages on the warning display. Pressing a master light cancels it and the aural, ready for the next alert, but the message stays until the fault clears.

Altitude Alerting

fig_33_01Original redraw

Two alerts around the selected altitude. Approaching: when the aircraft comes within about 900 ft (system dependent, typically 900–1 000 ft) of the altitude set on the MCP, a chime sounds and the altitude box is highlighted, reminding the crew to level off. Deviating: once at the altitude, if it drifts by more than about 200–300 ft, an aural alert sounds and the alert light or box flashes. The system uses the same altitude source as the altimeter.

Altitude Alert Module

fig_33_02Original redraw

The hardware. On older or smaller aircraft a separate altitude alerter has a window showing the selected altitude, a knob to set it, and an ALERT light. It compares the selected altitude with the pressure altitude from the air data computer or encoding altimeter and drives the light and aural tone. On modern aircraft the same function is built into the MCP and PFD: the altitude window and the selected-altitude box on the tape.

Speed Tape — VMO & Buffet Limits

fig_33_04Original redraw

The protected speed range on the tape. At the top a red-and-black barber pole starts at the maximum operating speed (VMO/MMO), with an amber band below it marking the high-speed buffet or maximum manoeuvring margin. At the bottom an amber band marks the minimum manoeuvre speed (about 1.3 g buffet margin) and a red-and-black barber pole starts at the stick-shaker speed. The aircraft flies safely between them; the bands move with weight, altitude and g.

Stall Warning System Architecture

fig_33_05Original redraw

Warning before the stall. Angle-of-attack sensors on each side of the nose, with flap/slat position (which changes the stalling angle), feed the stall warning computers. When the angle of attack reaches a margin below the stall (about 5–7% above stall speed), they fire the stick shaker on the control column — a strong tactile and aural warning. Aircraft with poor natural stall behaviour (e.g. T-tails) also have a stick pusher that pushes the column forward if the crew does not respond. Light aircraft use a simple leading-edge vane switch or reed horn.

Angle-of-Attack Vane Sensor

fig_33_07Original redraw

Measuring the angle of attack. A small vane on the side of the forward fuselage is free to rotate and aligns itself with the local airflow, like a weathervane. Its angle relative to the fuselage is measured by a synchro or resolver inside and sent to the stall warning, air data and flight control computers, which correct for the local flow at the sensor’s position. The vane is electrically heated against ice; a jammed or damaged vane can give false stall warnings.

AoA Indicator & Indexer

fig_33_08Original redraw

Displaying angle of attack. An AoA indicator shows angle of attack directly (in units or as a fraction of the stall angle), with a band for the optimum approach angle and a red band near the stall. The approach indexer on the glareshield gives a heads-up cue: an upper chevron (fast: angle too low — reduce speed), the central doughnut (on speed) and a lower chevron (slow: angle too high — increase speed). Because the stalling angle is constant, AoA gives a correct margin at any weight.
