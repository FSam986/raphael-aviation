The Heart

hp-05Original redraw

The heart is two pumps side by side. The right side receives oxygen-poor blood from the body through the venae cavae into the right atrium, passes it to the right ventricle and pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs. Oxygen-rich blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, passes to the left ventricle — the thickest, strongest chamber — and is pumped through the aorta to the whole body. Valves keep the flow one-way. At rest the heart beats about 70 times a minute and pumps about 5 litres a minute. (The diagram is drawn as if facing the person, so the right side of the heart is on the left of the picture.)

Pulmonary & Systemic Circulation

hp-06Original redraw

Blood travels two circuits in series. In the pulmonary circulation the right ventricle sends oxygen-poor blood through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, where it gives up carbon dioxide and takes on oxygen, returning through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium. In the systemic circulation the left ventricle sends that oxygen-rich blood through the aorta and arteries to the body tissues, where oxygen is delivered in the capillaries; veins return the oxygen-poor blood to the right atrium. Note the exception to the usual colours: the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood and the pulmonary veins oxygen-rich.

Blood Pressure — Systolic & Diastolic

hp-07Original redraw

Blood pressure is quoted as two numbers. Systolic pressure is the peak, when the ventricles contract and push blood into the arteries; diastolic pressure is the lowest value, while the heart relaxes and refills between beats. A typical healthy adult reading is about 120/80 mmHg. Persistently raised pressure — hypertension, generally taken as 140/90 or more — increases the risk of stroke and heart attack and can cost a pilot the medical; low pressure (hypotension) reduces tolerance to G and can cause fainting. Pressure rises with age, obesity, stress, smoking and lack of exercise.

The Sense of Smell — Olfactory System

hp-08Original redraw

Smell is a chemical sense. Molecules carried in the inhaled air dissolve in the mucus high in the nasal cavity and stimulate olfactory receptor cells. Their nerve fibres pass up through the skull to the olfactory bulb, which sends the signal to the brain. The sense is very sensitive but adapts quickly: a continuous smell fades from awareness within minutes, so a pilot may stop noticing fumes, fuel or smoke — never dismiss a smell that was noticed earlier just because it seems to have gone. Smell is degraded by a cold, by hypoxia and by smoking.

The Respiratory System — Lungs & Alveoli

hp-09Original redraw

External respiration brings oxygen to the blood. Air passes down the trachea, through the bronchi and ever-smaller bronchioles to about 300 million tiny air sacs, the alveoli. Each is wrapped in capillaries with walls one cell thick: oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out, each moving from high partial pressure to low. Breathing is driven by the diaphragm and rib muscles and its rate is controlled mainly by the carbon dioxide level in the blood, not the oxygen level. At rest: 12–16 breaths a minute, about 500 ml each. As altitude increases the partial pressure of oxygen in the alveoli falls — the cause of hypoxia.

Structure of the Eye

hp-14Original redraw

The eye works like a camera. Light is bent mainly by the cornea (about 70% of the focusing) and fine-focused by the lens, whose shape is changed by the ciliary muscles — accommodation. The iris adjusts the size of the pupil to control the light entering. The image falls on the retina, which has two kinds of receptor: cones, concentrated at the fovea, give sharp colour vision in good light; rods, in the surrounding retina, work in dim light but see no colour and little detail. Where the optic nerve leaves there are no receptors — the blind spot.

Visual Acuity Falls Away from the Fovea

hp-15Original redraw

Sharp vision covers only a tiny area. Acuity is highest at the fovea, where the cones are packed most densely, but it collapses as the image moves off-centre: about half at 5°, a quarter at 10° and a tenth at 20°. So only what the pilot looks at directly, within about 1° to 2°, is seen in full detail; an aircraft a few degrees to the side may not be noticed until it moves. The blind spot lies about 15° to the nasal side. At night the cones stop working and the best sensitivity is found 10° to 15° off-centre, where the rods are most numerous — look slightly to the side of a dim object.

Lookout — The Sector Scan & Empty-Field Myopia

hp-16Original redraw

The eyes only see detail when they are still. During the jump between fixations (a saccade) vision is effectively switched off, so sweeping the eyes smoothly across the sky sees very little. The effective lookout is the sector scan: divide the sky into blocks of about 10°, fixate on each for 1 to 2 seconds, then move on, covering the area ahead and to both sides methodically. With nothing to focus on — an empty sky, haze, darkness — the eyes relax to a focus of only 1 to 2 metres (empty-field myopia), so distant aircraft go unseen: refocus regularly on a distant object such as a wingtip or cloud edge.

Long Sight & Short Sight

hp-17Original redraw

Focus errors come from the shape of the eye. In a normal eye, light from a distant object is focused exactly on the retina. In long sight (hypermetropia) the eyeball is too short, so the focus would fall behind the retina: near objects are blurred, and it is corrected with a convex (converging) lens. In short sight (myopia) the eyeball is too long and the focus falls in front of the retina: distant objects are blurred, corrected with a concave (diverging) lens. Presbyopia is the loss of accommodation with age (from about 40–50) as the lens stiffens — near vision goes first. A pilot who needs correction must carry a spare pair of spectacles.

Collision Course — Constant Bearing, Late “Blossom”

hp-38Original redraw

An aircraft on a collision course does not move across the windscreen. If the relative bearing stays constant and the range is closing, the two aircraft will collide — and with no relative movement the eye, which detects movement best, is slow to notice it. Worse, its apparent size grows very slowly at first: the image doubles each time the range halves, so it stays a tiny dot for most of the approach and then “blossoms” in the last few seconds. Recognising, deciding and manoeuvring takes about 10–12 seconds; by the time the other aircraft looks alarming it may be too late. An aircraft that appears stationary in the windscreen is the dangerous one.

False Horizon — Sloping Cloud Bank

hp-31Original redraw

The eye takes the most prominent line for the horizon. A sloping cloud bank, a tilted layer of haze, a coastline or a line of lights at night can be mistaken for the true horizon, and the pilot unconsciously rolls the aircraft to put the wings “level” with it — leaving the aircraft banked and turning while feeling level. It is most likely when the real horizon is hidden or indistinct, and at night over sparsely lit ground where stars and ground lights merge. The cure is the same as for every disorientation illusion: believe the instruments, especially the attitude indicator.

Approach Angle & the Visual Aiming Point

hp-32Original redraw

On a steady approach the aiming point does not move. The point on the ground towards which the aircraft is actually flying stays fixed in the windscreen; everything beyond it appears to move up and everything short of it to move down. If the chosen aiming point drifts up the windscreen the aircraft is undershooting (too low / too shallow); if it drifts down it is overshooting. The runway’s shape also tells the angle: on a normal 3° approach it has a familiar perspective; too high, it looks longer and narrower; too low, it looks short and flat.

Sloping-Runway & Runway-Width Illusions

hp-33Original redraw

Pilots judge height from the runway’s perspective, so an unusual runway fools them. An upsloping runway looks longer and steeper, as if seen from higher up: the pilot feels too high and flies a low, shallow approach — risk of undershoot. A downsloping runway looks short and flat: the pilot feels too low and flies high — risk of overshoot and a long landing. Width works the same way: a narrower runway than usual looks further away, so the pilot feels high and flies low, flaring late; a wider one makes the pilot feel low, fly high and flare too early. Upsloping terrain in the undershoot has the same effect as an upsloping runway.

Aiming Point vs Touchdown Point

hp-36Original redraw

The aircraft does not land where it is aimed. The pilot flies the eye towards the aiming point, but the flare reduces the rate of descent and carries the aircraft along the runway, so the touchdown point is beyond the aiming point. On a large aircraft the effect of geometry matters too: the main wheels are well below and behind the pilot’s eyes, so they cross the threshold much lower than the eye path — aiming at the very start of the runway would put the wheels in the undershoot. That is why the aiming-point markings are painted about 300 m (in the range 150–400 m) in from the threshold.

Rain on the Windscreen — Refraction Illusion

hp-39Original redraw

Water on the windscreen bends the light. A layer of rain acts as a prism and refracts light from the runway downwards, so the runway and horizon appear lower than they really are. Seeing the runway lower in the windscreen, the pilot believes the aircraft is higher than it is and descends — a low approach and undershoot. The error can be of the order of 200 ft at 1 nm from the runway. Heavy rain also reduces visibility and makes lights appear dimmer and further away, reinforcing the impression of height. Use the glide-slope, PAPI and the altimeter rather than the picture.

Structure of the Ear

hp-10Original redraw

Three parts, two jobs. The outer ear collects sound and channels it down the ear canal to the eardrum. In the air-filled middle ear three tiny bones, the ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes), pass the vibration to the fluid-filled inner ear, where the cochlea turns it into nerve signals — hearing. The inner ear also contains the vestibular apparatus (semicircular canals and otoliths) — balance. The Eustachian tube links the middle ear to the throat to equalise pressure; it opens easily in the climb but can stay shut in the descent, especially with a cold, causing pain and possible eardrum damage (otic barotrauma). Noise above about 85–90 dB damages the cochlea permanently.

The Vestibular Apparatus

hp-11Original redraw

The balance organ of the inner ear has two kinds of sensor. Three semicircular canals, set at right angles to one another, detect angular acceleration in pitch, roll and yaw. Two otolith organs — the utricle (mainly horizontal movement) and the saccule (mainly vertical) — detect linear acceleration and the direction of gravity. Both work well on the ground but were never designed for flight: the canals cannot sense a steady turn or a very slow rate of roll, and the otoliths cannot tell acceleration from tilt. Without outside visual reference they produce powerful false sensations — spatial disorientation.

How the Semicircular Canals & Otoliths Work

hp-12Original redraw

Both sensors rely on inertia. A semicircular canal is a ring of fluid (endolymph) with a jelly-like flap, the cupula, across it. When the head starts to turn, the fluid lags behind and bends the cupula, signalling rotation. In a steady turn the fluid catches up within about 20 seconds, the cupula straightens and the sensation of turning stops; when the turn ends the fluid carries on and signals a turn the other way. An otolith organ is a bed of hair cells under a gel layer weighted with chalk crystals; linear acceleration or tilt makes the heavy layer slide and bend the hairs — and the brain cannot tell the two apart.

The Leans — Semicircular-Canal Disorientation

hp-13Original redraw

The most common disorientation illusion. The semicircular canals have a threshold: a roll slower than about 2° per second is not felt. If the aircraft banks that slowly — while the pilot is distracted — the pilot still feels level. On noticing the attitude indicator, the pilot rolls back to level briskly; that movement is above the threshold and is felt as a roll the other way. The aircraft is now level, but the pilot feels banked in the opposite direction and may lean to one side, or even re-enter the original bank, to feel upright. The sensation can last for many minutes. Remedy: believe the instruments and fly the attitude indicator.

Somatogravic Illusion — Acceleration Felt as Pitch-Up

hp-37Original redraw

The otoliths cannot tell acceleration from tilt. In steady flight the only force on them is gravity, straight down. During a strong forward acceleration — a take-off or go-around, especially at night or in cloud — inertia adds a rearward force, and the resultant of the two points down and back. The brain takes that resultant for “down”, so the pilot feels tilted backwards, as if pitching up, and instinctively pushes the nose down — into the ground. Deceleration gives the opposite illusion, a false pitch-down. With no visual horizon the sensation is completely convincing: fly the attitude indicator and hold the correct climb attitude.
