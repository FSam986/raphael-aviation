Cylinder Arrangements

fig_02_01Original redraw

The four classic layouts with their characteristic bank angles (V 60°, radial ~40° between cylinders), tied together by a shared crankshaft motif.

Normal Combustion — flame spread

fig_06_01Original redraw

Normal combustion — the spark lights a kernel that spreads as one smooth flame front, so pressure rises progressively and pushes the piston evenly. A sudden multi-point explosion instead would be detonation.

Convergent–Divergent Duct (Bernoulli)

fig_01_02Original redraw

Bernoulli in a duct — squeeze the flow and it speeds up while pressure and temperature fall; let it expand and the reverse happens. This is the principle behind the venturi, the carburettor throat and compressor/diffuser stages.

TDC, BDC, Stroke & Crank Throw

fig_02_02Original redraw

Stroke & dead centres — the piston reverses at Top and Bottom Dead Centre; the distance between them is the stroke, and the crank throw (crank radius) is exactly half of it.

Air–Fuel Mixture Ratio

fig_07_01Original redraw

Mixture — a richer mixture (lower number) gives best power and cools the charge at high power to resist detonation; a leaner mixture near 15:1 gives best economy. Take-off uses full rich.

Float-Chamber Carburettor

fig_08_03Original redraw

Carburettor — air speeding through the venturi drops the pressure at the throat, drawing fuel up from the float chamber through the main jet. The float and needle valve hold a constant fuel level; the pressure-balance duct keeps the chamber referenced to intake pressure.

Propeller Blade — Anatomy

fig_12_01Original redraw

Blade anatomy — the root/shank near the hub carries the loads and the pitch-change peg; the aerofoil section runs out to the tip, with the leading edge cutting into the air and the trailing edge behind.

Four-Stroke Cycle & Valve Timing

fig_02_04Original redraw

Valve & ignition timing — over two crank revolutions. The inlet valve opens 20° before TDC and closes 71° after BDC; the exhaust valve opens 62° before BDC and closes 29° after TDC — so both valves are briefly open together around TDC (valve overlap). Ignition is timed 34° before TDC so peak cylinder pressure arrives just after it. (Typical light-aero figures.)

Boosted Induction & Constant-Speed Unit

fig_11_09Original redraw

Boosted induction — air (and fuel) is compressed by the engine-driven supercharger to raise manifold pressure; the automatic boost controller trims the throttle to hold the set boost, while the constant-speed unit varies blade pitch to hold the rpm the propeller lever selects.

Ignition Advance vs RPM

fig_06_02Original redraw

Ignition advance — the spark fires before TDC so peak pressure arrives just after it. At higher rpm the piston covers ground faster, so the spark must fire even earlier (more advance); at low rpm the advance is reduced.

Gas-Turbine Combustion Chamber

fig_16_03Original redraw

Combustion chamber — only ~⅓ of the air burns in the primary zone (the snout/swirler stabilises the flame); the rest enters through liner holes as secondary and dilution air to cool the liner and drop the gas to a temperature the turbine can take.

Direct Fuel-Injection System

fig_10_01Original redraw

Fuel injection — air is metered through the throttle and sprayed with fuel at the injector nozzle just upstream of each cylinder. Engine-driven and electric pumps keep fuel pressure up; the alternate-air source bypasses a blocked intake filter.

Detonation vs Normal Combustion

fig_06_03Original redraw

Detonation — instead of a smooth flame front, the end-gas self-ignites and explodes, giving a violent pressure spike and the characteristic knock. It overheats and can destroy the piston; caused by too-low grade fuel, too-weak mixture at high power, or excess temperature.

Magneto Ignition Circuit

fig_05_01Original redraw

Magneto — the engine-driven rotating magnet induces a current in the primary winding; when the contact breaker opens, the collapsing field induces a high voltage in the secondary, which the distributor routes to each spark plug in firing order. The ignition switch fires the engine by not earthing the primary.

Turbocharger — General Arrangement

fig_11_03Original redraw

Turbocharger — exhaust gas spins the turbine, which drives the compressor to raise manifold pressure. A wastegate dumps surplus exhaust to hold the target boost; its oil-pressure actuator and controller manage the setting. Unlike the supercharger, it is driven by otherwise-wasted exhaust energy, not the crankshaft.

Liquid Cooling System

fig_04_01Original redraw

Liquid cooling — the pump circulates coolant through jackets in the cylinder block; hot coolant rises to the header tank and passes down through the radiator, where ram air carries the heat away before it returns to the pump.

Turbojet — Axial-Flow Gas Turbine

fig_13_02Original redraw

Turbojet — air is compressed through the stages of the axial compressor (the annulus narrows as pressure rises), fuel burns at constant pressure in the combustor, and the hot gas drives the turbine — which turns the compressor on the same shaft — before accelerating out of the nozzle as a high-speed jet. Whittle's original used a centrifugal compressor; modern engines are axial, as shown.

Propeller Blade Angle & Angle of Attack

fig_12_04Original redraw

Blade angle vs angle of attack — the blade angle (θ) is fixed between the chord and the plane of rotation, but the angle of attack (α) is what the blade meets the relative airflow at, and that airflow is the resultant of rotational speed and forward TAS. As TAS rises the helix angle (φ) grows and α falls — which is why a variable-pitch/constant-speed prop re-twists the blade to hold an efficient α.

Constant-Speed Unit (Governor)

fig_12_12Original redraw

Constant-speed unit — flyweights driven at engine speed work against the speeder spring the prop lever sets. Overspeed: weights fly out, the pilot valve ports oil to coarsen pitch and pull rpm back. Underspeed: weights fall in, oil drains to fine the pitch — so rpm stays constant as power and airspeed change.

Centrifugal vs Axial Compressor

fig_15_03Original redraw

Compressor types — a centrifugal impeller flings air radially for a big pressure rise in one rugged stage; an axial compressor passes air straight through many rotor/stator stages, each adding a little, for higher efficiency and mass flow. Big engines use axial (or axial-plus-centrifugal) compressors.

Feathering a Propeller

fig_12_18Original redraw

Feathering — after an engine failure the blades are turned ~90° so the chord lines up with the airflow. The prop stops windmilling, which removes the large drag of a dead, flat-pitched propeller and reduces the yaw and rate of descent.

Propeller Reduction Gearing

fig_12_22Original redraw

Reduction gearing — a small engine gear drives a larger propeller gear, so the prop turns slower than the crankshaft. This keeps the blade tips below the speed of sound (where efficiency and noise suffer) while letting the engine run at its efficient high rpm.

Turbofan & Turboprop

fig_13_10Original redraw

Turbofan & turboprop — both extract extra turbine work to move a large mass of air gently rather than a small mass fast, which is more efficient at their speeds. The turbofan does it with a ducted bypass fan; the turboprop with a reduction-geared propeller driven by a power turbine.

Gas-Turbine Start Sequence

fig_24_02Original redraw

Starting — the starter spins the compressor to a minimum rpm, then fuel and igniters are introduced for light-up (watch for an EGT spike — a hung or hot start). Once the turbine is self-sustaining the starter and igniters cut out and the engine accelerates to idle.

Gas-Turbine Oil System

fig_26_01Original redraw

Oil system — a pressure pump feeds filtered oil to the shaft bearings; larger scavenge pumps return it (a dry-sump system scavenges more than it pressures) through a cooler to the tank. Oil both lubricates and carries heat away from the bearings.

Engine Instruments

fig_27_01Original redraw

Engine instruments — N1 (fan) and N2 (core) are the two spool speeds; EGT guards the turbine temperature limit (critical on start and at full power); EPR (exhaust/inlet pressure ratio) is the primary thrust reference on many engines; fuel flow trends consumption. Together they show the engine's health at a glance.

Gas-Turbine Working Cycle

fig_18_03Original redraw

Parameter changes — through the compressor, pressure and temperature rise while velocity falls (the diffuser trades speed for pressure). Burning adds a large temperature spike at near-constant pressure. The turbine then drops pressure and temperature to drive the compressor, and the nozzle converts the remaining energy back into high velocity — the thrust-producing jet.

Thrust Reverser

fig_20_05Original redraw

Thrust reverser — on landing, blocker doors close the gas path and cascade vanes turn the flow forward and outward, so the engine's thrust now acts to slow the aircraft. It supplements the wheel brakes, especially on wet or icy runways, and is stowed again once speed is low.

Compressor Stall

fig_15_08Original redraw

Compressor stall — each compressor blade is an aerofoil meeting the airflow at an angle of attack. If axial airflow drops or the spools get out of step, the AoA becomes too great, the flow separates and the blade stalls. Widespread stall is surge — a violent flow reversal, guarded against by bleed valves and variable stator vanes.

Compressor Bleed Air

fig_22_01Original redraw

Bleed air — hot, high-pressure air tapped from the compressor is piped to a manifold and used for cabin pressurisation and air-conditioning, airframe and engine anti-ice, and air-turbine engine starting. Taking too much bleed reduces thrust and narrows the surge margin, so it is scheduled carefully.

Bypass Ratio

fig_13_11Original redraw

Bypass ratio — the mass of cold air driven round the core by the fan versus the mass passing through the core. A high bypass ratio moves a large mass of air gently, which is quiet and fuel-efficient at airliner speeds; a low bypass ratio gives a faster jet for high-speed flight.

FADEC — Engine Control

fig_22_03Original redraw

FADEC — a Full-Authority Digital Engine Control reads the thrust lever and engine/air-data sensors and schedules fuel flow, vane and bleed positions and ignition to run the engine at the demanded thrust within its limits. "Full authority" means no manual backup — so it is dual-channel (A/B) for redundancy.

Air Intake Types

fig_19_01Original redraw

Intakes — the compressor needs subsonic, high-pressure air. A subsonic intake is a divergent duct that slows the flow and recovers ram pressure. A supersonic intake uses a shock cone or ramp to stand up shock waves that decelerate the flow below Mach 1 before it reaches the compressor.

Exhaust Nozzle Types

fig_20_01Original redraw

Nozzles — a convergent nozzle speeds the gas up to the speed of sound at its throat, which is enough for most airliner engines. A convergent-divergent nozzle adds a diverging section that expands the already-sonic gas to supersonic speed, used on afterburning and high-speed engines.

Afterburner (Reheat)

fig_20_08Original redraw

Afterburner / reheat — fuel is sprayed into the hot, oxygen-rich gas in the jet pipe downstream of the turbine and burned behind flame holders, giving a large thrust boost for take-off and combat. The nozzle must open (variable area) to pass the hotter, faster gas. It is very thirsty, so it is used only briefly.

Engine Fire Protection

fig_27_03Original redraw

Fire protection — a continuous-loop (fire-wire) detector around each designated fire zone senses overheat/fire and triggers a warning. The pilot then discharges an extinguisher bottle, flooding the zone with agent. Transport engines carry two shots and the system is tested before flight.

Twin-Spool Engine & Station Numbering

fig_13_10Original redraw

Twin-spool layout — two mechanically independent spools on concentric shafts. The N1 (low-pressure) spool links the front LP compressor to the rear LP turbine; the N2 (high-pressure) spool links the HP compressor to the HP turbine. Each spool self-selects its best speed, improving surge margin and efficiency. Station numbering (0–8) fixes a pressure (P) and temperature (T) reference at each plane along the gas path — the basis for performance and health monitoring.

The Otto Cycle (P–V Diagram)

fig_02_06Original redraw

Otto cycle — the ideal four-stroke cycle on a pressure–volume diagram. Air is drawn in (induction), compressed adiabatically (1→2), burned at almost constant volume near TDC (2→3), and the hot gas expands to do work on the piston (3→4) before blow-down and the exhaust stroke. The enclosed area is the net work per cycle.

Overhead-Valve Mechanism

fig_02_08Original redraw

Valve gear — the rotating camshaft lobe lifts the tappet, which pushes the pushrod up; the rocker arm pivots and presses the valve down off its seat to open the port. The valve spring closes it again as the lobe passes. This overhead-valve (pushrod) layout is standard on light-aircraft piston engines.

Spark Plug Construction

fig_05_04Original redraw

Spark plug — high-tension voltage reaches the terminal and runs down the centre electrode, insulated from the metal shell by the ceramic. It jumps the air gap to the earth electrode (which is screwed into the cylinder head), producing the spark. Gap size and heat range are set for the engine.

Wet Sump vs Dry Sump

fig_09_02Original redraw

Sump types — a wet sump stores the oil in a pan under the crankcase and a single pump feeds it round. A dry sump keeps the oil in a separate tank; larger scavenge pumps continuously empty the crankcase, so the engine can run inverted and stays cooler — used on aerobatic and large engines.

Carburettor Icing & Carb Heat

fig_08_06Original redraw

Carb icing — fuel vaporising and air accelerating through the venturi can drop the temperature 20–30 °C, so ice forms at the throat and on the throttle even in warm, humid air — choking the engine. Carburettor heat routes air past an exhaust muff to melt and prevent it (at the cost of some power).

Aircraft Fuel System

fig_10_03Original redraw

Fuel system — fuel gravity-feeds (high-wing) or is pumped from the wing tanks through the selector, a gascolator (strainer/water trap), and the engine-driven and electric boost pumps to the carburettor or injection. Vents keep the tanks at ambient pressure; sump drains let water be checked before flight.

Two-Stroke Cycle

fig_03_01Original redraw

Two-stroke — a power stroke every revolution. As the piston rises it compresses the charge above and draws fresh mixture into the crankcase; as it falls it pressurises the crankcase and uncovers the exhaust then transfer ports, so fresh charge sweeps in and pushes the exhaust out (scavenging). Simple and light, but less efficient and dirtier than a four-stroke.

Propeller Effects — Left-Turning Tendencies

fig_12_09Original redraw

Left-turning tendencies — a clockwise propeller (seen from the cockpit) produces four effects that all swing the nose left, strongest at high power and low airspeed (take-off, climb): torque reaction, gyroscopic precession, slipstream over the fin, and asymmetric blade (P-factor). Right rudder corrects them.

Flat-Rating & EGT Limit

fig_17_05Original redraw

Flat-rating — an engine is certified to hold a constant thrust up to a "corner" temperature by running below its full mechanical/thermal capability on cold days. Beyond the corner the EGT (turbine temperature) limit is reached, so thrust must lapse as OAT rises. This gives predictable performance across the normal temperature range.

Water-Methanol Injection (ADI)

fig_11_11Original redraw

Water-methanol (ADI) — for brief maximum power (take-off, go-around) a water-methanol mix is sprayed into the induction. It evaporates and cools the charge, raising density and suppressing detonation so more boost/fuel can be used; the methanol adds a little fuel and stops freezing. It restores power lost to hot, high-altitude conditions.

Ice Protection — Anti-ice & De-ice

fig_11_13Original redraw

Ice protection — anti-ice prevents ice forming (hot air or heated elements, run before entering ice); de-ice removes ice already formed (pneumatic boots that crack it off, or TKS fluid). Protected areas include wing and tail leading edges, propeller, windscreen, pitot/static probes and the engine intake/carburettor.

Detonation vs Pre-Ignition

fig_06_04Original redraw

Two different faults — detonation is the unburnt end-gas exploding after the plug fires, giving the knock and pressure spike; pre-ignition is the charge being lit by a glowing hot spot before the plug fires, so it burns against the rising piston. Both overheat and can wreck the engine; pre-ignition can be the more rapidly destructive.

Compression Ratio

fig_02_10Original redraw

Compression ratio — how much the charge is squeezed: the full cylinder volume (swept + clearance) divided by the clearance volume left at TDC. A higher ratio extracts more work and improves efficiency, but raises peak pressure and the risk of detonation, so it is limited by fuel grade.

Volumetric Efficiency

fig_02_12Original redraw

Volumetric efficiency — the actual mass of charge that fills the cylinder versus the theoretical full swept volume. It peaks in the mid-rpm range where valve timing and intake ram are best matched, and falls off at low rpm (weak ram) and high rpm (induction/exhaust flow losses). Higher VE means more power.

Exhaust System & Heat Muff

fig_11_12Original redraw

Exhaust — the manifold collects the hot gas from each cylinder into a silencer that quietens the pulses, then out of the tailpipe. A jacket (heat muff) around a pipe section warms air drawn through it for carburettor heat and cabin heat — which is why an exhaust leak is a carbon-monoxide hazard.

Constant-Speed Unit — Over & Underspeed

fig_12_13Original redraw

Governing — the governor holds the set rpm by sensing it with the flyweights. In overspeed the weights fly out, the pilot valve sends oil to coarsen blade pitch (more load) so rpm falls; in underspeed the weights fall in and oil fines the pitch (less load) so rpm rises. On-speed, the ports are closed and pitch is held.

Turbine Blade Cooling

fig_19_06Original redraw

Blade cooling — turbine-entry gas is hotter than the blade metal can survive, so cool air bled from the compressor is fed up through the hollow blade's internal passages (convection) and bled out through tiny holes to lay a film of cool air over the surface, shielding it from the hot stream. This lets the engine run at higher, more efficient temperatures.

Mixture Control & Idle Cut-Off

fig_08_04Original redraw

Mixture control — the mixture lever moves a needle (or back-suction valve) that meters how much fuel the main jet delivers. RICH for take-off and climb, LEAN to match the thinner air at altitude for economy, and Idle Cut-Off (ICO) fully closes the fuel to stop the engine — the correct way to shut down, clearing fuel from the cylinders.

Carburettor Accelerator Pump

fig_08_05Original redraw

Accelerator pump — opening the throttle suddenly lets a slug of air through before the slow fuel column responds, giving a momentary weak mixture and a stumble. A small pump linked to the throttle squirts extra fuel straight into the venturi on rapid opening to bridge the gap, so the engine accelerates cleanly.

Engine Mounts & Vibration

fig_02_15Original redraw

Engine mounts — the engine is bolted to a tubular mount frame through flexible rubber mounts that absorb the firing and rotational vibration before it reaches the airframe, and take the torque and thrust loads. Flexible connections (fuel, controls, exhaust) let the engine move slightly on its mounts without fatigue.

Three-Spool Turbofan

fig_13_11Original redraw

Three-spool — the LP (fan), intermediate (IP) and HP compressors each run on their own concentric shaft and turbine, so all three self-select their most efficient speed. It gives excellent efficiency and a short, stiff engine, at the cost of mechanical complexity (three sets of bearings and seals).

Propeller Blade-Angle Range

fig_12_11Original redraw

Blade angle range — a constant-speed/variable-pitch prop sweeps from fine (low angle, high rpm for take-off and climb) to coarse (high angle, efficient cruise), on to feather (edge-on, engine-out) and, on turboprops, into the beta/reverse range (flat and negative) for ground handling and braking.

Shaft Bearings & Labyrinth Seal

fig_26_02Original redraw

Bearings & seals — a ball bearing locates the shaft axially and takes thrust and radial loads; roller bearings take only radial load but let the shaft grow in length with temperature. A labyrinth seal (a comb of fins) pressurised with compressor bleed air keeps the lubricating oil in the bearing chamber and hot gas out.

Fuel Grades & Detonation Margin

fig_07_02Original redraw

Fuel grades — avgas is graded by its knock resistance (performance number, lean/rich). Higher grades resist detonation at higher pressures, so an engine designed for 100LL must not be run on a lower grade. The dye colour (red 80, blue 100LL, green 100) lets ground crew confirm the right fuel; never use a lower grade than specified.

Propeller Slip (Geometric vs Effective Pitch)

fig_12_07Original redraw

Slip — the geometric pitch is how far the blade would screw forward in one revolution through a solid; the air yields, so the effective pitch (the distance actually advanced) is less. The difference is slip — the price of producing thrust. A fixed-pitch prop is efficient only near its design speed.

Dual Ignition & the Mag Check

fig_05_03Original redraw

Dual ignition — each cylinder has two spark plugs fed by two independent magnetos, for redundancy and a faster, more complete burn. The mag check before flight selects each magneto in turn: a small rpm drop is normal, but an excessive drop (or none) signals a fault. Magnetos are engine-driven and self-powered, so the engine keeps running with the electrics off.

Supercharger Types

fig_11_10Original redraw

Supercharger types — a centrifugal blower spins an impeller to fling air outward, giving a big smooth pressure rise at high speed (the common aero type). A positive-displacement (Roots/screw) blower traps a fixed volume between rotating lobes and pushes it on, giving boost that is steady with rpm. Both are crankshaft-driven, unlike the exhaust-driven turbocharger.

Power vs Altitude & Critical Altitude

fig_11_04Original redraw

Power vs altitude — a normally aspirated engine loses power steadily as air thins with height. A turbo/supercharged engine holds its rated power by boosting the thinner air up to the critical altitude — the highest altitude at which full rated manifold pressure can still be maintained — above which its power also falls away.

Turbine Nozzle Guide Vanes

fig_17_07Original redraw

Nozzle guide vanes — the fixed vanes ahead of each turbine stage form convergent passages that accelerate the hot gas and swirl it to the correct angle so it strikes the moving rotor blades efficiently. They sit in the hottest part of the engine, so they are hollow and air-cooled, and set the first turbine's throat area.

Oil Cooler & Thermostat

fig_26_03Original redraw

Oil cooling — a thermostatic valve controls oil temperature. When the oil is cold it bypasses the cooler so the engine warms quickly; as it heats up the valve routes it through the oil cooler, where ram air carries the heat away. This holds the oil in its correct operating range in all conditions.

Starter-Generator

fig_24_04Original redraw

Starter-generator — one machine does both jobs on many turbine engines. For start it is fed electrical power and acts as a motor to spin the engine up; once running, the engine drives it and it becomes a generator supplying the aircraft. Combining the two saves weight and a set of drive pads.

Propeller Synchrophasing

fig_12_19Original redraw

Synchrophasing — on a multi-engine aircraft a synchrophaser trims the slave propeller(s) to exactly match the master's rpm and hold a fixed blade phase angle between them. Matching rpm removes the slow "beat" throb; choosing the phase angle positions the pressure pulses to minimise cabin noise and vibration.

Piston-Engine Gauges & Limits

fig_27_02Original redraw

Engine indicating — the piston-engine gauges (rpm, manifold pressure, oil temperature & pressure, cylinder-head and exhaust-gas temperature) carry colour operating arcs: green is the normal range, amber a caution band, and a red line the never-exceed limit. A glance confirms every parameter sits in the green.

Power-Enrichment (Economiser)

fig_08_08Original redraw

Economiser (power enrichment) — at cruise the main jet alone gives a lean, economical mixture. As the throttle is opened wide for high power, a linked economiser valve opens an extra fuel passage, enriching the mixture to give best power and to cool the charge against detonation. It closes again at lower power to save fuel.

Spark vs Compression Ignition

fig_02_16Original redraw

Two ways to light the charge — a spark-ignition (petrol) engine compresses a fuel–air mixture at a modest ratio and fires it with a spark plug. A compression-ignition (diesel/Jet-A piston) engine compresses air alone to a high ratio until it is hot enough that injected fuel ignites on its own — no ignition system, better fuel economy, heavier build.

Fuel Injector / Atomiser

fig_16_11Original redraw

Injector — fuel delivered under pressure lifts a spring-loaded needle off its seat and is forced through a fine orifice, breaking it into a cone of tiny droplets. Atomising the fuel gives a huge surface area so it mixes and burns rapidly and completely; the needle snaps shut cleanly when pressure drops to stop dribble.

Reduction Gear Types

fig_12_22Original redraw

Reduction gearing — a spur train uses a small crankshaft gear driving a larger prop gear, which offsets the propeller axis above the crankshaft (nose-high thrust line). A planetary (epicyclic) set — sun, planets and ring — keeps the propeller coaxial with the crankshaft and shares the load over several gears, so it is compact and strong for high-power engines.

Mixture Leaning & Peak EGT

fig_07_03Original redraw

Leaning — as the mixture is leaned from rich, EGT rises to a peak (chemically correct) then falls again. Best power is a little rich of peak; best economy is at or slightly lean of peak. Pilots lean by reference to EGT, keeping within cylinder-head temperature limits, to save fuel and run the engine cleanly at cruise.

Compressor Map & Surge Line

fig_15_04Original redraw

Compressor map — pressure ratio against mass flow, with a curve for each rotational speed. The surge line marks where the flow breaks down; the engine is run along the operating line a safe distance below it. The gap is the surge margin — eroded by acceleration, ice, damage or bleed, and protected by bleed valves and variable vanes.

Variable Stator Vanes

fig_15_05Original redraw

Variable stator vanes — the early compressor stators pivot on a unison ring driven by the fuel control. At low rpm they close down to swirl the air and cut the flow, keeping the compressor off the surge line; as rpm rises they open to full axial flow for maximum pressure rise. Together with bleed valves they give a wide, stall-free operating range.

Turbine Igniter & Exciter

fig_23_03Original redraw

Turbine ignition — a gas turbine only needs ignition for starting and relight. The exciter builds up and dumps a high-energy pulse to a surface-discharge igniter plug (not a fine-gap spark plug), giving a fat, hot spark that lights the atomised fuel reliably even in cold, wet, high-altitude air. Once self-sustaining, the igniters switch off.

Fuel Control Unit

fig_22_03Original redraw

Fuel control unit — the heart of engine control. It takes high-pressure fuel from the pump and meters exactly how much reaches the burners, scheduling it against the thrust-lever demand and engine speed, compressor delivery pressure and temperature. It limits acceleration and over-speed/over-temperature to keep the engine off surge and within its limits — the job FADEC now does electronically.

Auxiliary Power Unit (APU)

fig_24_05Original redraw

APU — a small self-contained gas turbine, usually in the tail, that runs on the ground (and sometimes in flight) to supply bleed air for air-conditioning and for starting the main engines, and electrical power through its generator — making the aircraft independent of ground equipment.

Geared Turbofan

fig_13_12Original redraw

Geared turbofan — a reduction gearbox sits between the LP turbine and the fan, so the big fan can turn slowly (quiet, efficient, high bypass) while the LP compressor and turbine spin fast at their own best speed. Decoupling the two lets each run near its optimum, cutting fuel burn and noise, at the cost of a heavily loaded gearbox.

Contra-Rotating Propellers

fig_12_17Original redraw

Contra-rotating props — two propellers mounted on concentric shafts turning in opposite directions. The second disc recovers the swirl the first leaves in the slipstream, raising efficiency, and the opposing torques cancel — so there is no torque reaction, slipstream or P-factor yaw. The penalty is weight, complexity and noise.

Compressor Bleed (Surge) Valve

fig_15_06Original redraw

Bleed (surge) valve — at low rpm and during acceleration the front stages pump more air than the back can swallow, which would stall the compressor. A handling bleed valve opens to dump the surplus air overboard, keeping the flow stable; as the engine accelerates to high rpm the valve closes so all the air goes through the combustor for full power.

Ram Air Turbine (RAT)

fig_27_03Original redraw

Ram air turbine — an emergency last-resort power source. If the engines and APU fail, a small turbine drops into the slipstream and the airflow spins it, driving a hydraulic pump and/or generator to power the essential flight controls and instruments until the crew restores normal power or lands.

Automatic Mixture / Altitude Compensation

fig_08_10Original redraw

Altitude compensation — as the aircraft climbs the air thins but the fuel jet keeps flowing the same, so the mixture would go over-rich. An aneroid capsule senses the falling air density and progressively bleeds air into the metering circuit (or trims the needle), automatically leaning the mixture to hold the correct fuel-air ratio with height.

Thrust Reverser — Cascade vs Clamshell

fig_26_02Original redraw

Thrust reversers turn the exhaust forward to brake on the runway. The cascade type blocks the fan duct and routes the cold bypass air out through fixed turning vanes; the clamshell (bucket) type swings two doors into the full jet pipe and deflects the whole stream forward. Both are inhibited in flight and below a minimum speed.

Two-Stroke Porting

fig_05_06Original redraw

Two-stroke porting — no valves; the piston itself opens and closes ports in the cylinder wall. As it rises it draws mixture into the crankcase through the inlet port; as it descends after firing it uncovers the exhaust port to release the burnt gas, then the transfer port, letting crankcase-compressed mixture sweep up and scavenge the cylinder. One power stroke every revolution.

Hot-Section Components

fig_20_04Original redraw

Hot section — the components bathed in the highest gas temperatures. The combustor liner contains the flame and is cooled by a film of air through its perforations; the nozzle guide vanes accelerate and turn the gas onto the turbine at the correct angle (and take the hottest gas of all); the turbine blades extract the work. These parts are life-limited and the focus of hot-section inspections.

Magnetic Chip Detector

fig_18_07Original redraw

Magnetic chip detector — a magnetic plug sits in the oil system to catch ferrous wear particles. A healthy engine sheds only fine "fuzz"; a failing bearing or gear throws larger chips. When enough metal bridges the two insulated poles it completes a circuit and lights a cockpit warning, giving early notice of internal mechanical failure before it becomes catastrophic.

Vapour Lock

fig_07_09Original redraw

Vapour lock — if fuel in the line gets hot (a soaked-back hot engine, climbing to low ambient pressure, or a volatile fuel) it boils and forms a vapour bubble. A pump can move liquid but not compressible vapour, so the bubble blocks the flow and starves the engine — causing rough running or stoppage. A boost pump pressurising the line raises the boiling point and clears it.

Acceleration / Deceleration Fuel Schedule

fig_22_06Original redraw

Fuel schedule — the fuel control must thread fuel flow between two limits as speed changes. Add fuel too fast and the extra pressure pushes the compressor into surge; cut it too fast and the flame goes out (weak extinction). On a slam acceleration the unit follows a scheduled line just under the surge limit, giving the quickest spool-up the engine can take without stalling.

Combustion Chamber Types

fig_19_03Original redraw

Combustion chamber types — the multiple-can layout has separate flame tubes around the shaft: simple and testable one at a time, but heavy and draggy. The cannular puts individual flame tubes inside one common air casing — a compromise. The annular is a single continuous ring: lightest, shortest and most efficient with the best outlet-temperature distribution, which is why modern engines use it.

Free-Turbine Turboprop

fig_13_15Original redraw

Free-turbine turboprop — the propeller is driven by a separate power turbine with no mechanical connection to the gas-generator spool; the two are linked only by the gas stream. The gas generator runs at its own best speed while the free turbine drives the prop through a reduction gearbox. This eases starting (less to turn), allows feathering without braking the core, and decouples prop and compressor speeds.

Magneto Impulse Coupling

fig_17_11Original redraw

Impulse coupling — at low cranking speed a magneto turns too slowly to make a strong spark, and the spark would come too early. A spring-loaded coupling lets a pawl catch and hold the magnet while the engine turns and the spring winds up; near top-dead-centre the pawl trips and the spring snaps the magnet round fast, giving a strong, automatically retarded spark for easy starting. Above idle it disengages and the mag runs normally.

EGT Thermocouple

fig_21_07Original redraw

EGT thermocouple — two dissimilar metals joined at a hot junction in the exhaust generate a small voltage proportional to the temperature difference between that junction and the cool gauge end (Seebeck effect). The gauge reads the voltage directly as exhaust gas temperature — the key limit watched on start and at high power, with several probes averaged around the duct.

Flame Tube — Airflow Zones

fig_19_05Original redraw

Flame-tube airflow — only about a quarter of the compressor air burns. In the primary zone a swirler mixes a near-stoichiometric charge around the nozzle for a stable flame; more air enters the secondary zone to burn the rest completely; and cool dilution air is added last to drop the gas to a temperature the turbine can accept, with an even outlet distribution. A film of air also shields the tube walls.

Duplex Fuel Spray Nozzle

fig_22_09Original redraw

Duplex nozzle — a single nozzle must atomise fuel well across a huge flow range. At low flow only the fine primary passage sprays, giving a good cone for lighting and idle; as pressure rises a pressurising valve opens the larger main passage so the full flow still atomises finely at high power. This avoids the dribbling, poorly-atomised spray a single fixed orifice would give at idle.

Fuel Heater (Ice Prevention)

fig_22_11Original redraw

Fuel heater — cold fuel can hold water that freezes into ice crystals and blocks the fine filter. A fuel–oil heat exchanger passes hot engine oil around the fuel, warming it above the icing point before the filter (and cooling the oil usefully at the same time). It runs automatically or on a timer, clearing or preventing the ice that would otherwise starve the engine.

Fuel Filter & Bypass

fig_22_12Original redraw

Fuel filter & bypass — the filter traps dirt and water before the fuel control and nozzles. If the element clogs, the pressure drop across it rises; a spring-loaded bypass valve then opens so the engine still gets fuel (dirty is better than none), usually after lighting an impending-bypass warning. A clean element keeps all flow through the mesh and the bypass shut.

Oil Filter & Bypass Valve

fig_18_09Original redraw

Oil filter & bypass — a full-flow filter cleans all the oil on its way to the bearings. On a cold start thick oil, or a clogged element, raises the pressure drop across it; a bypass valve then opens so the bearings keep getting oil — unfiltered oil beats oil starvation. In normal running the bypass stays shut and every drop passes through the element.

Engine Priming System

fig_07_12Original redraw

Priming — a cold engine's carburettor cannot vaporise enough fuel to start. The pilot pumps the primer, a small plunger that draws fuel from the strainer and injects a measured shot of raw fuel straight into the intake ports (or induction manifold) to give a rich, ignitable charge for the first firing. Over-priming floods the cylinders; the primer is locked after start.

Pressure Carburettor

fig_08_14Original redraw

Pressure carburettor — instead of a float and suction, it meters fuel with a diaphragm chamber fed by two pressures: ram/air pressure on one side and the low venturi-throat pressure on the other. Their difference (proportional to airflow) sets the metering pressure, and fuel is injected under pump pressure at the throat. Having no float and no suction discharge, it is far less prone to icing and works through any attitude or g.

Turbine Blade Fir-Tree Root

fig_20_11Original redraw

Fir-tree root — turbine blades see enormous centrifugal load and heat. Each blade's root is cut in a serrated fir-tree shape that slides into a matching broached slot in the disc rim, spreading the pull over several lands. The slight looseness lets the blade expand and seat under rotation, and the shape allows blades to be replaced individually without scrapping the disc.

Noise-Suppressing Lobed Mixer

fig_26_06Original redraw

Lobed mixer — jet noise comes from the violent shear between a fast hot core jet and the still air around it. A corrugated lobed mixer at the nozzle folds the cool bypass air and hot core flow into interleaved fingers, so they blend quickly and the exhaust leaves more uniform and slower at its edge. The gentler shear cuts the low-frequency roar, and mixing also recovers a little thrust.

Turboprop Torquemeter

fig_21_09Original redraw

Torquemeter — a turboprop's thrust comes from the propeller, so power is read as torque, not just rpm. A torquemeter measures the twist (or the reaction load in the gearbox casing) of the shaft driving the reduction gear: the greater the torque, the greater the twist. It is shown as a torque gauge (in psi, %, or lb·ft) and is the primary power-setting instrument for the turboprop pilot.

Beta Range & Ground Fine

fig_11_14Original redraw

Beta range — on the ground the turboprop pilot controls blade angle directly (beta), not rpm: from reverse through ground fine (a flat, low-drag taxi pitch) up to flight idle. Above that lies the governing (alpha) range, where the constant-speed governor takes over and holds rpm while it varies pitch. The changeover protects against fine pitch being selected in flight.

Autofeather & Negative-Torque Sensing

fig_11_16Original redraw

Autofeather / NTS — a windmilling dead engine's propeller creates huge drag. When an engine fails the prop starts to drive the engine instead of the reverse — a negative torque. A negative-torque sensing unit detects this and automatically commands the pitch-change mechanism to drive the blades toward feather, removing the drag before the crew can react, which is vital for climb performance after an engine failure.

Propeller Electric De-Ice

fig_28_04Original redraw

Propeller de-ice — ice on the blades unbalances the prop and spoils its efficiency. Electric heating boots bonded to the blade roots and cuffs are fed through slip rings and brushes on the rotating hub. A timer cycles the heat between blades (or sides) a few seconds each, so ice is loosened and flung off by centrifugal force without overloading the electrical system or shedding ice all at once.

Engine Energy Balance

fig_03_11Original redraw

Energy balance — only about a third of the fuel's heat energy becomes useful shaft work. Roughly another third leaves as hot exhaust, about a quarter is carried away by the cooling system, and the rest is lost to friction and pumping. This is thermal efficiency: the Otto cycle's modest figure is set by its compression ratio and the unavoidable rejection of heat, which is why engines run warm and loud.

Power & Torque vs RPM

fig_04_12Original redraw

Power & torque — torque (turning effort) is set by how well the cylinder breathes, so it peaks at the mid-range rpm of best volumetric efficiency, then falls as breathing and friction worsen. Power is torque × rpm, so it keeps climbing past peak torque and peaks at a higher rpm, only dropping when torque falls faster than rpm rises. Max power and max torque occur at different speeds.

Specific Fuel Consumption

fig_03_14Original redraw

Specific fuel consumption — the fuel burned per unit of power (per hp or per lb thrust·hour) measures how economically the engine works. Plotted against power it is U-shaped: wasteful at low load (friction and enrichment dominate) and again at high load (power enrichment), with a minimum at a cruise power where combustion is most efficient. Pilots lean and set power to sit near that minimum.

Jet Thrust — Speed & Altitude

fig_25_09Original redraw

Thrust with speed & altitude — unlike a propeller, a pure jet's thrust stays roughly constant with airspeed: as speed rises the mass flow grows even though the velocity increment shrinks, and ram recovery props it up at high speed. With altitude, though, thrust falls steadily because the thinner air gives less mass flow — which is why climb performance decays with height even at constant rpm/EPR.

Pressure & Temperature Through the Engine

fig_14_08Original redraw

P & T through the engine — pressure climbs steadily through the compressor, stays near-constant across the combustor (heat is added at roughly constant pressure), then drops through the turbine and nozzle as energy is extracted and the gas accelerates. Temperature rises a little with compression, spikes in the combustor to the turbine-entry limit, then falls as the turbine takes work and the nozzle expands the gas.

Engine Pressure Ratio (EPR)

fig_21_12Original redraw

EPR — on many jets thrust is set by engine pressure ratio: the ratio of turbine-exit (or jet-pipe) total pressure to engine-inlet total pressure. Pressure probes at both stations feed a transmitter that shows the ratio directly. Because it reflects the actual pressure rise the engine is producing, EPR is a truer thrust indication than rpm alone, and the primary thrust-setting gauge on those engines.

Fuel Tank Venting & Drains

fig_07_15Original redraw

Venting & drains — as fuel is used or warms, the tank must breathe: a vent keeps it at ambient pressure so the feed doesn't stall under vacuum or burst under pressure. Water, denser than fuel, settles at the tank's low point, where a sump drain lets the pilot draw off and check a sample before flight. Vents are placed and sometimes heated so they can't be blocked by ice or attitude.

Low-Tension vs High-Tension Magneto

fig_17_14Original redraw

LT vs HT magneto — a high-tension magneto makes the high voltage in one coil and distributes it through a rotor and long HT leads to each plug: simple, but at altitude the high voltage can leak (flashover) across the distributor in thin, damp air. A low-tension magneto distributes only low voltage and steps it up in a small coil right at each plug, so only short HT runs exist — far less prone to high-altitude flashover, at the cost of extra coils.
