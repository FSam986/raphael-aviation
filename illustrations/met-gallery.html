The Atmosphere — Composition & Vertical Structure

atmosphere.jpgOriginal redraw

Composition — dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and 1% argon, with a trace of carbon dioxide (about 0.04% today; older texts quote 0.03%). Water vapour varies from 0 to 4% and is what makes weather. Structure — the troposphere holds nearly all the weather and water vapour; temperature falls with height at about 1.98°C per 1 000 ft. Its top, the tropopause, is low and relatively warm over the poles (about 8 km, −45°C) and high and cold over the equator (16–18 km, −75°C). ISA uses 11 km (36 090 ft) and −56.5°C. Above it, in the stratosphere, temperature is constant and then rises.

Temperature — Diurnal Variation, Lapse Rate & Inversions

temperature.jpgOriginal redraw

Diurnal variation — the surface is heated by insolation by day and cools by terrestrial radiation at night. The maximum comes about two hours after local noon (around 1500) and the minimum about half an hour after sunrise. Cloud cover and wind reduce the daily range; clear, calm conditions give the largest range. With height — temperature normally falls (a lapse rate; ISA 1.98°C per 1 000 ft). Where it rises with height the layer is an inversion, and where it stays constant it is isothermal. Both are very stable and trap haze, fog and low cloud beneath them.

Humidity, Saturation & Dew Point

humidity.jpgOriginal redraw

Warm air can hold more water vapour than cold air — the curve shows the maximum (saturated) amount at each temperature. The example air at 20°C holds 9.4 g/m³ but could hold 17.3 g/m³, so its relative humidity is 54%. Cool it with no change in moisture and it moves left until it meets the curve at 10°C: that temperature is the dew point, where RH is 100%. Cool it further and the excess condenses as cloud, fog or dew. A small spread between temperature and dew point means high RH and a risk of fog or low cloud.

Air Density — What Changes It

air-density.jpgOriginal redraw

Density is mass per unit volume (ISA sea level 1.225 kg/m³). It increases with pressure — the air is squeezed into less space — and decreases with temperature, because warm air expands. It also decreases as humidity rises: a water-vapour molecule is lighter than the nitrogen or oxygen molecule it replaces, so moist air is less dense than dry air. And it decreases with altitude as pressure falls. Low density (“hot, high and humid”) means less lift and less engine power: longer take-off runs and poorer climb.

Pressure Altitude — Worked Example

pressure-altitude.jpgOriginal redraw

Pressure altitude is the height above the 1013.25 hPa level — what the altimeter reads with 1013 set. Here the airfield is 4 000 ft above mean sea level and the QNH is 1030 hPa. Pressure falls with height, so the 1013 level lies above sea level: 17 hPa × 30 ft = 510 ft above it. The airfield is therefore only 3 490 ft above the 1013 level: pressure altitude 3 490 ft. Rule: QNH higher than 1013 gives a pressure altitude lower than elevation; QNH lower than 1013 gives a higher one. (30 ft per hPa is the exam approximation; 27 ft is closer at sea level.)

Altimeter Settings — QFE, QNH & Standard

altimetry.jpgOriginal redraw

The altimeter measures height above whatever pressure is set on its subscale. Set QFE (the pressure at the airfield) and it reads height above the field — zero on landing. Set QNH (airfield pressure reduced to sea level using ISA) and it reads altitude above mean sea level — field elevation on landing. Set 1013.25 hPa and it reads pressure altitude, used as flight levels above the transition altitude; QNE is that reading at touchdown. QFF is station pressure reduced to sea level using the actual temperature; it is used to draw isobars on surface charts, not to set altimeters.

Stability — ELR against the Adiabatic Lapse Rates

stability.jpgOriginal redraw

Stability depends on how the environment (ELR) compares with a rising parcel. Unsaturated air cools at the DALR, 3°C per 1 000 ft; saturated air cools more slowly, at the SALR, about 1.5°C per 1 000 ft, because condensation releases latent heat. If the environment cools faster than the DALR, a lifted parcel is always warmer than its surroundings and keeps rising: absolute instability. If the ELR is less than the SALR the parcel is always colder and sinks back: absolute stability. In between, the air is stable if dry but unstable once saturated: conditional instability. Unstable air gives cumuliform cloud, showers and turbulence; stable air gives layer cloud, drizzle, fog and smooth flight.

Cloud Types by Height & How Cloud Forms

clouds-precip.jpgOriginal redraw

Ten cloud types in three height bands. High (cirro-): Ci, Cc, Cs — ice crystals, no significant icing or turbulence. Medium (alto-): Ac, As. Low: St, Sc. Three have great vertical extent: Ns (thick rain-bearing layer), Cu and Cb (base low, top up to the tropopause, with an anvil). “Nimbo/nimbus” means rain-bearing; “strato” means layered (stable air); “cumulo” means heaped (unstable air). Cloud forms when air is lifted and cooled to its dew point — by convection, by terrain (orographic), by convergence, or at a front. Band heights are for temperate latitudes.

Thunderstorms — Ingredients & Life Cycle

thunderstorms.jpgOriginal redraw

A thunderstorm needs three things: a deep layer of instability, plenty of moisture, and a trigger to start the lift (convection, a front, convergence or high ground). Each cell passes through three stages. Cumulus stage: updraughts throughout, the cloud builds rapidly, no rain yet. Mature stage: precipitation begins and drags air down, so strong up- and downdraughts exist side by side — the most dangerous stage, with heavy rain, hail, lightning, severe turbulence, icing and a gust front at the surface. Dissipating stage: downdraughts spread through the cell, cutting off the supply of warm air; the anvil remains.

Thunderstorm Hazards

thunderstorm-hazards.jpgOriginal redraw

Every hazard in the book lives in a cumulonimbus. Severe turbulence and vertical draughts can exceed the aircraft’s structural limits. Hail can be thrown out of the anvil into clear air. Icing is severe where supercooled water droplets exist, worst between 0°C and about −20°C. Lightning can blind the crew, damage the airframe and upset the magnetic compass; static causes radio noise. Near the ground the outflow produces windshear, gust fronts and microbursts. Pressure changes under the storm can also cause altimeter errors. Avoid thunderstorms visually or by weather radar — never fly under or through one.

Turbulence — The Four Main Causes

turbulence.jpgOriginal redraw

Mechanical turbulence comes from wind blowing over rough ground, buildings and trees; it grows with wind speed and usually reaches about 2 000 ft above the surface. Convective (thermal) turbulence comes from uneven heating: rising thermals with sinking air between, worst on hot afternoons and smooth above the cumulus tops. Wake turbulence is the pair of counter-rotating vortices shed from the wingtips of every aircraft; they are strongest behind an aircraft that is heavy, slow and clean, and they sink and drift with the wind. Clear-air turbulence (CAT) is found in strong windshear near jet streams, typically on the cold (polar) side, with no cloud to warn of it.

Mountain Waves & Windshear on the Approach

mountain-waves-windshear.jpgOriginal redraw

Mountain (standing) waves form when a strong wind — about 15 kt or more at the summit, increasing with height — blows within about 30° of perpendicular to a ridge, with a stable layer near the summit. The air oscillates downwind for many miles. Lenticular clouds sit in the wave crests, a cap cloud covers the summit, and under the first crest a rotor produces severe turbulence. Expect strong downdraughts in the lee. Windshear is a change of wind speed or direction over a short distance. On the approach, a headwind that dies away near the ground makes airspeed and lift fall suddenly: the aircraft sinks below the glide path unless power is added at once.

Microburst on the Approach

microburst-surface-turbulence.jpgOriginal redraw

A microburst is a concentrated downdraught from a convective cloud that hits the ground and spreads out in all directions. It is small — less than 4 km across — and short-lived, typically 1 to 5 minutes, with winds that can exceed 60 kt. An aircraft flying through it meets three things in quick succession: an increasing headwind (airspeed rises, the aircraft balloons above the path — and the pilot is tempted to reduce power), then the downdraught, then an increasing tailwind that destroys airspeed and lift close to the ground. Avoid: delay the approach or take-off when microbursts are reported or heavy rain shafts or virga are seen beneath a storm. (Mechanical surface turbulence is shown in the Turbulence figure.)

Visibility & the Formation of Fog

visibility-fogs.jpgOriginal redraw

Fog is visibility below 1 000 m caused by water droplets; mist is 1 000 to 5 000 m with relative humidity of 95% or more; haze is reduced visibility from dry particles such as dust and smoke. Runway visual range (RVR) is reported when visibility falls below 1 500 m. Radiation fog forms over land on clear nights with moist air and a light wind of 2–8 kt, and often pools in valleys. Advection fog forms when warm moist air moves over a colder surface, day or night, in winds up to about 15 kt. Upslope fog forms when moist air is forced up rising ground and cools adiabatically. Steam fog forms when very cold air moves over much warmer water.

Frontal Fog & Tropical-Air Fog

fog-frontal-tropical.jpgOriginal redraw

Frontal fog forms in the cold air ahead of a warm front (or occlusion). Continuous rain falls from the warm air aloft into the cold air beneath; it evaporates, raises the moisture content of the cold air and saturates it. It can cover a wide belt and lasts until the front has passed. Tropical-air fog is an advection fog: warm, moist tropical maritime air moving towards the pole passes over a steadily colder surface, is cooled from below to its dew point, and forms widespread fog and low stratus over sea and coast.

Warm Front & Cold Front — Cross-Sections

fronts.jpgOriginal redraw

Warm front — warm air rides up over a retreating wedge of cold air on a very shallow slope, about 1:150. The cloud is layered and arrives in sequence over hundreds of miles: Ci, Cs, As, Ns, with continuous rain from the As/Ns ahead of the surface front. Cold front — advancing cold air undercuts the warm air on a much steeper slope, about 1:50 to 1:80, lifting it sharply: a narrow band of Cu and Cb with heavy showers, squalls and thunderstorms, followed by rapid clearance. A cold front moves faster than the warm front ahead of it and eventually overtakes it, forming an occlusion. On charts a warm front carries semicircles, a cold front triangles, pointing the way the front is moving.

Weather Changes as a Front Passes

front-passage.jpgOriginal redraw

Recognising a front from the changes it brings. At a warm front the changes are gradual: pressure falls steadily and then stops falling, temperature and dew point rise, and long-lasting rain gives way to drizzle or dry, often misty, conditions in the warm sector. At a cold front they are abrupt: a line of Cb with heavy showers, a sudden drop in temperature and dew point, a sharp rise in pressure, and then very good visibility in the cold, clear air behind. The wind veers at both fronts in the Northern Hemisphere and backs in the Southern Hemisphere.

Occluded & Stationary Fronts

occluded-front.jpgOriginal redraw

An occlusion forms when a cold front catches up with the warm front ahead and lifts the warm-sector air clear of the surface. What happens next depends on which cold air mass is colder. In a cold occlusion the air behind is colder than the air ahead, so it undercuts it — the weather resembles a cold front. In a warm occlusion the air behind is less cold, so it rides up over the colder air ahead — more like a warm front. Either way the lifted warm air gives thick cloud and prolonged rain, and Cb can be embedded in the layer cloud, hidden from view. A stationary front shows little or no movement; its symbols point in opposite directions.
