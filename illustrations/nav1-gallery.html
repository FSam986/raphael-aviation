Latitude — Angle from the Equator

nav_016_figure_02Original redraw

Latitude is the angular distance of a place north or south of the equator, measured at the centre of the Earth along the meridian through the place, from 0° at the equator to 90° at the poles. All points with the same latitude lie on a parallel of latitude, a small circle parallel to the equator (the equator itself is the only parallel that is a great circle). One degree of latitude is 60 nautical miles everywhere, and one minute is one nautical mile — which is why distance is always measured on the latitude scale at the side of a chart.

Map Projection — The Light-Source Principle

nav_290_figure_01Original redraw

A chart is a projection of the curved Earth onto a flat surface. Imagine a transparent model of the Earth — the reduced Earth — with a light inside it. Features on the globe cast shadows onto a sheet of paper, and where they fall becomes the chart. The paper may be flat (azimuthal, as here), rolled into a cylinder (Mercator) or a cone (Lambert). No projection can be perfect: a sphere cannot be flattened without distortion, so each chart preserves some properties — shape (conformality), bearing, scale, area — at the expense of others. Navigation charts are chosen to be conformal: angles on the chart equal angles on the Earth.

Lambert Conformal Chart — Standard Parallels

nav_357_figure_01Original redraw

The Lambert chart is a cone that cuts the Earth along two parallels. Those two are the standard parallels, where the scale is exactly correct. Between them the scale is slightly too small (contracted), least of all at the parallel of origin midway between; outside them it is slightly too large (expanded). The rule of thumb is to place the standard parallels at one-sixth and five-sixths of the chart’s latitude spread, which keeps the scale error under about 1%. Meridians are straight lines converging on the pole; parallels are arcs of concentric circles.

Relative Motion — Crossing a Current

nav_132_figure_01Original redraw

The classic relative-velocity problem. A boat must cross a river 10 nm wide from A to B, directly opposite, and the water is flowing sideways at 5 kt. If the boat simply points at B, the moving water carries it downstream and it lands somewhere else. The boat’s motion through the water and the water’s motion over the ground add together as vectors — exactly as an aircraft’s heading and true airspeed combine with the wind to give its track and ground speed. To arrive at B the boat must point upstream, into the current, just as an aircraft heads into wind to hold a track.

The Navigation Computer — Circular Slide Rule

nav_085_figure_03Original redraw

The calculator side of the navigation computer. It is a circular slide rule: a fixed outer scale and a rotating inner scale, both logarithmic, so setting one number against another performs multiplication or division. By convention distance or fuel goes on the outer scale and time on the inner. The black triangular 60 index on the inner scale is the “per hour” mark: put it against the speed on the outer scale and every time on the inner scale lies opposite the distance flown. The scales carry no decimal point — the user supplies it — so always make a rough mental estimate first. Extra windows convert units and solve true airspeed and density altitude.

The 1-in-60 Rule

nav_198_figure_01Original redraw

A quick way to turn distance off track into an angle. In a long, thin triangle, 1 nm off track after 60 nm is 1° of track error (because the tangent of a small angle is close to the angle divided by 60). So: track error (°) = distance off track × 60 ÷ distance gone. In the figure, 8 nm off track after 60 nm is 8°; 4 nm off after 30 nm is also 8°. The rule is accurate enough for practical navigation up to about 20°. It is used for track corrections, for judging VOR and NDB off-track distances, and for descent gradients.

Direction on the Earth

nav_010_figure_01Original redraw

Direction is defined by the Earth’s rotation. The Earth spins about an axis through the geographic (true) poles. The direction in which it turns is east; the opposite is west; towards the North Pole along a meridian is true north. Direction is measured clockwise from north through 360° and always given as three figures: east is 090°, south 180°, west 270°, north 360° (or 000°). The four cardinal points are N, E, S and W; the intercardinal (quadrantal) points are NE 045°, SE 135°, SW 225° and NW 315°.

The Graticule — Parallels & Meridians

nav_015_figure_01Original redraw

The grid that fixes position. Meridians are semi-great circles joining the poles; they run true north–south and are used to measure longitude. A meridian and its anti-meridian on the far side together make a complete great circle. Parallels of latitude are circles parallel to the equator; they run true east–west and are used to measure latitude. Parallels are small circles, except the equator, which is a great circle. Meridians and parallels cross at right angles everywhere. Together the network is the graticule, and a position is given as latitude first, then longitude.

Geocentric vs Geodetic Latitude

nav_017_figure_01Original redraw

The Earth is slightly flattened — an oblate spheroid, with the polar diameter about 43 km (23 nm) shorter than the equatorial, a compression of about 1/298. This gives two ways to define latitude. Geocentric latitude is the angle at the centre of the Earth between the equatorial plane and the line to the point. Geodetic (geographic) latitude is the angle between the equatorial plane and the normal — the line perpendicular to the surface at the point — and is the one used on charts. They agree at the equator and the poles and differ most, by about 11.6 minutes, at 45° latitude. For most navigation the Earth is treated as a perfect sphere.

Longitude — East & West of Greenwich

nav_018_figure_02Original redraw

Longitude is the angular distance east or west of the Prime (Greenwich) Meridian, measured along the equator — or, equivalently, as the angle at the pole between the Greenwich meridian and the meridian through the place — from 0° to 180° east or west. The 180° meridian is the anti-meridian of Greenwich. Unlike latitude, a degree of longitude is not a constant distance: it is 60 nm at the equator but shrinks towards the poles, in proportion to the cosine of the latitude. That shrinking distance is the departure: departure (nm) = change of longitude (minutes) × cos latitude.

Great Circle — The Shortest Distance

nav_013_figure_01Original redraw

A great circle is a circle on the surface whose plane passes through the centre of the Earth. It is the largest circle that can be drawn on a sphere, and it divides it into two equal halves. Only one great circle can be drawn through any two points (unless they are exactly opposite each other), and the shorter arc of it is the shortest distance between them. The equator and all meridians are great circles. A small circle is any circle whose plane does not pass through the centre — every parallel of latitude except the equator. A great circle generally crosses successive meridians at a changing angle, so its track direction changes along the route.

Great-Circle Vertex

nav_022_figure_01Original redraw

The vertex is the highest latitude a great circle reaches. Every great circle (other than the equator and the meridians) has a northern vertex and a southern vertex, at equal and opposite latitudes and exactly 180° of longitude apart. At the vertex the great circle is travelling due east or west (090° or 270°): it has stopped climbing in latitude and is about to descend. It crosses the equator at two points 90° of longitude from the vertices, and the angle at which it crosses the equator equals the latitude of the vertex. Here the vertices are at 63°N and 63°S, so the track cuts the equator at 63°.

Great Circle vs Rhumb Line — Polar View

nav_030_figure_01Original redraw

Two ways to fly between the same two places. A rhumb line crosses every meridian at the same angle, so it has a constant true direction — easy to fly, but it is not the shortest route. Between two places on the same parallel it simply follows the parallel. The great circle is the shortest route, but its direction changes continuously, and it always lies on the polar side of the rhumb line. The difference in distance is small at low latitudes and over short legs, and largest at high latitudes over long east–west legs — which is why long-haul routes between northern cities arc up over the Arctic.

The Great Circle Lies on the Polar Side of the Rhumb Line

nav_034_figure_01Original redraw

On a chart where the rhumb line is straight, the great circle curves towards the nearer pole. In the Northern Hemisphere the great circle bows north of the rhumb line; flying east along it the track starts less than 090° (about 060° here), reaches 090° at the mid-point and ends more than 090° — the track increases eastbound. In the Southern Hemisphere the great circle bows south, and eastbound the track decreases. The initial and final great-circle tracks differ from the rhumb line by the conversion angle. Memory aid: the great circle is always nearer the pole; the rhumb line nearer the equator.

Meridians Converge Towards the Pole

nav_246_figure_01Original redraw

Two meridians are parallel at the equator and meet at the pole. The angle between them — their inclination to one another — is the convergency. It is zero at the equator, where the meridians are parallel, and reaches its maximum, equal to the change of longitude, at the poles. In between it grows with the sine of the latitude: convergency = change of longitude × sin(latitude). Convergency is why a great circle’s direction changes along its length — it crosses each successive meridian at a different angle — and why straight lines on most charts are not lines of constant direction.

Convergency Between Two Positions

nav_248_figure_01Original redraw

Calculating convergency. For two positions, convergency (°) = change of longitude (°) × sin(mean latitude). In the example the meridians are 40° of longitude apart and both positions are at 30°N: convergency = 40 × sin 30° = 40 × 0.5 = 20°. That is the amount by which the great-circle track between the two positions changes direction from one end to the other. If the two positions are at different latitudes, use the mean (middle) latitude. Convergency is always measured as an angle, never as a distance.

Convergency & the Change of Great-Circle Direction

nav_249_figure_02Original redraw

The change in great-circle track equals the convergency. A great circle is “straight” on the globe, but it is measured against meridians that are converging. So the track angle a measured at the first meridian differs from the angle b at the second, and b − a = convergency between the two meridians. In the Northern Hemisphere the track increases when flying east and decreases when flying west; in the Southern Hemisphere it is the opposite. Knowing this, the initial great-circle track can be found from the final, or either from the rhumb-line track using the conversion angle.

Conversion Angle

nav_257_figure_01Original redraw

The conversion angle is the angle between the great circle and the rhumb line at either end. It is exactly half the convergency: conversion angle = ½ × change of longitude × sin(mean latitude). In the example A is at 30°N 020°W and B at 30°N 020°E: change of longitude 40°, so convergency = 40 × sin 30° = 20° and the conversion angle is 10°. The rhumb-line track A to B is 090°, so the great-circle track is 080° leaving A and 100° arriving at B. It is used to convert a radio (great-circle) bearing into a rhumb-line bearing for plotting on a Mercator chart.

The Earth's Magnetic Field

nav_051_figure_01Original redraw

The Earth behaves as if it had a bar magnet inside it, tilted about 11° from the spin axis, so the magnetic poles are not at the geographic poles — and they wander slowly. Lines of force leave near the south magnetic pole and re-enter near the north. A compass needle aligns with the local field: its horizontal component (H) gives direction and is strongest at the magnetic equator; its vertical component (Z) pulls the needle downwards — the dip — and is greatest at the magnetic poles, where H falls to zero and the compass is useless. Lines joining places of equal dip are isoclinals; the line of zero dip is the aclinic line (magnetic equator).

Variation & the Agonic Line

nav_052_figure_02Original redraw

Variation is the angle between true north and magnetic north at a given place. If magnetic north lies to the east of true north the variation is easterly (positive); if to the west, westerly (negative). To convert: “variation west, magnetic best; variation east, magnetic least” — with westerly variation the magnetic heading is greater than the true. Lines on a chart joining places of equal variation are isogonals; the line of zero variation, where true and magnetic north are in line, is the agonic line. Variation changes slowly over the years as the magnetic poles move, so charts carry the year and the annual change.

Variation Near the Poles — Up to 180°

nav_053_figure_01Original redraw

Variation can reach 180°. On the line joining the true and magnetic poles, at any point between the two poles, true north lies in one direction and magnetic north in exactly the opposite direction: the variation is 180°. On the extensions of that line beyond either pole, both norths lie the same way and the variation is zero. So close to the poles variation changes very rapidly over short distances, the horizontal field strength is too weak to drive a compass reliably, and magnetic references are abandoned in favour of true or grid navigation with gyro or inertial heading.

True, Magnetic & Compass Heading

nav_066_figure_02Original redraw

Three norths, three headings. True heading is measured from true north. Apply variation to get magnetic heading, measured from magnetic north. Apply deviation — the error caused by the aircraft’s own magnetism, read from the deviation card — to get compass heading, what the compass actually shows. In the example: true 100°, variation 25°W gives magnetic 125°; deviation 10°W gives compass 135°. Working from true to compass, add westerly and subtract easterly; from compass to true, do the reverse. Memory aid: “Cadbury’s Dairy Milk Very Tasty” — Compass, Deviation, Magnetic, Variation, True.

True Heading, Relative Bearing & True Bearing

nav_487_figure_01Original redraw

Relative bearing is measured from the nose; true bearing from true north. A relative bearing is the angle, measured clockwise, between the aircraft’s fore-and-aft axis and the line to the object — it is what a fixed-card ADF shows. Add it to the heading to get the bearing from north: true bearing = true heading + relative bearing (subtract 360° if the answer exceeds 360°). In the example: heading 030°(T), relative bearing 060°, so the true bearing to the station is 090°, and the bearing from the station to plot is the reciprocal, 270°. Use magnetic heading and the answer is a magnetic bearing (QDM).

The Circular Slide-Rule Principle

nav_080_figure_01Original redraw

A slide rule multiplies by adding lengths. Both rings are marked logarithmically, so the distance round the ring to a number is proportional to its logarithm; adding two such distances multiplies the numbers. To work out 2 × 3: turn the inner ring until its 1 (the index, marked 10) lies against 2 on the outer ring. Now every number on the inner ring lies opposite twice its value on the outer: opposite 3 read 6. Division is the same setting read the other way. The whole ring is now a ratio — which is why one setting solves speed, distance and time for any time.

Sidereal Day vs Apparent Solar Day

nav_390_figure_01Original redraw

Two ways to time one rotation. A sidereal day is one complete 360° rotation measured against the distant stars: 23 h 56 min 4 s. But during that day the Earth has also moved about 1° along its orbit, so it must turn a little further — nearly 361° — before the Sun is back on the same meridian. That interval, from one transit of the Sun to the next, is the apparent solar day, about 4 minutes longer. Because the Earth’s orbital speed varies (Kepler’s second law) and its axis is tilted, the apparent solar day is not constant in length through the year — hence the invention of the mean solar day of exactly 24 hours.

The Equation of Time

nav_391_figure_01Original redraw

The real Sun is an irregular clock. Because the Earth’s orbit is an ellipse and its axis is tilted, the apparent Sun runs fast at some times of year and slow at others. Clocks therefore follow an imaginary mean Sun moving at a constant rate, giving the 24-hour mean solar day. The difference between apparent solar time and mean solar time is the equation of time. It is zero four times a year (mid-April, mid-June, early September and late December) and reaches about +16 minutes in early November (Sun ahead of the clock) and about −14 minutes in mid-February (Sun behind).
