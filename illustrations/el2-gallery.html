Electromagnetic Induction Needs Relative Motion

ch06_01Original redraw

Faraday’s discovery. Moving a magnet into a coil makes the connected meter deflect; holding it still gives no reading; pulling it out deflects the meter the other way. An EMF is induced only while the magnetic flux linking the coil is changing — it does not matter whether the magnet moves, the coil moves, or the current in a nearby coil changes. This is electromagnetic induction, the principle of every generator, alternator and transformer.

Direction of the Induced Current Follows the Motion

ch06_03Original redraw

Lenz’s law. The induced current always flows in the direction that opposes the change producing it. Push the north pole towards the coil and the induced current makes the near end of the coil a north pole, repelling the magnet; pull it away and the near end becomes a south pole, attracting it. So reversing the motion — or the pole — reverses the induced current and the meter swings the other way. Because the induced field always opposes the motion, mechanical work must be done to generate electricity.

Fleming's Right-Hand (Generator) Rule

ch06_04Original redraw

For generators use the right hand. Hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of the right hand at right angles to each other. The thuMb points in the direction of Motion of the conductor, the First finger along the Field (north to south), and the seCond finger then gives the direction of the induced Current (conventional). Memory aid: right for generating, left for motoring — “Generators drive on the right”.

Three Ways to Increase the Induced EMF

ch06_05Original redraw

The induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of flux linkage (Faraday’s law), so it can be increased in three ways: 1 move the conductor or magnet faster; 2 use a stronger magnetic field; 3 use more turns on the coil. In a generator these correspond to the speed of rotation, the field current (which sets the field strength) and the number of turns built into the armature. Since engine speed varies, aircraft generators control their output voltage by adjusting the field current.

Split-Ring Commutator — Making DC

ch06_08Original redraw

How a DC generator gets direct current. A loop rotating in a magnetic field generates an EMF that reverses every half-turn. The loop’s ends are connected to the two halves of a split ring — the commutator — and fixed carbon brushes press on it. Every half-turn, just as the EMF in the loop reverses, the commutator also swaps the connections to the brushes, so the output at the brushes is always in the same direction: pulsating DC. The commutator and brushes wear and spark, which limits DC generators’ speed and output.

Single-Loop DC Output Waveform

ch06_09Original redraw

Pulsating DC. Without a commutator one loop gives a sine wave that goes positive for half a turn and negative for the next. The commutator reverses the connections each half-turn, flipping the negative halves positive, so the output is a series of humps — always in one direction, but rising to a peak at 90° and 270° (when the loop cuts the flux at right angles) and falling to zero at 0°, 180° and 360° (when it moves along the flux). Usable DC needs many coils, as the next figure shows.

Series-Wound DC Generator

ch06_10Original redraw

Field in series with the load. All the load current flows through the series field winding, which has a few turns of heavy wire. With no load there is almost no field and almost no output; as the load increases, the field strengthens and the voltage rises with load (until the iron saturates). That makes the output voltage depend on the load, so a series generator is unsuitable for supplying an aircraft bus-bar. Series windings are used, however, in compound generators and in motors.

Single vs Multiple Coil — Smoothing the DC

ch06_11Original redraw

More coils, smoother DC. A single coil gives humps that fall to zero twice a revolution. A practical armature has many coils spaced round it, each connected to its own pair of commutator segments, so the brushes always pick up the coil that is near its peak. The overlapping humps combine into a nearly steady output with only a small ripple. The more coils and segments, the smaller the ripple.

Compound-Wound DC Generator

ch06_13Original redraw

Shunt and series fields together. A shunt field (many turns of fine wire, connected across the output) provides the main field and keeps the voltage up at light load. A small series field carries the load current and adds flux as the load rises, compensating for the voltage drop inside the armature. Proportioned correctly (“level compounded”) the output voltage stays nearly constant from no load to full load. Aircraft DC generators are usually shunt or compound wound, with a voltage regulator controlling the shunt field current.

DC Generator vs Alternator

ch06_14Original redraw

Swap what rotates. In a DC generator the field is stationary and the armature rotates, so the full load current has to pass through the commutator and brushes — heavy, sparking and limiting at high speed or altitude. An alternator turns this round: the field rotates (it needs only a small current, fed through slip rings) and the output is taken from a stationary armature (stator) by fixed connections. Its AC output is rectified by diodes to DC for a light-aircraft bus. Alternators are lighter, give useful output at low engine speed and need far less maintenance — but, unlike a DC generator, cannot self-excite from a flat battery.

Carbon-Pile Voltage Regulator

ch06_15Original redraw

Controlling the field current to hold the voltage. A stack of carbon discs is in series with the generator’s shunt field. A spring squeezes the pile: tightly compressed, its resistance is low, field current high. An electromagnet connected across the generator output pulls against the spring. If the output voltage rises — say with engine speed — the electromagnet pulls harder, the pile loosens, its resistance rises, the field current falls and the voltage drops back. If the voltage falls, the opposite happens. The action is smooth and continuous, without contacts opening and closing.

Vibrating-Contact Voltage & Current Regulator

ch06_16Original redraw

The older light-aircraft regulator. The generator field is fed through two sets of vibrating contacts in series. The voltage regulator coil is across the output: when the voltage reaches the set value it pulls its contacts open, inserting a resistor into the field circuit, the field weakens and the voltage falls; the contacts close again and the cycle repeats many times a second, holding a steady average voltage. The current limiter coil carries the load current and opens its contacts in the same way if the generator is overloaded. Spring tension sets each operating point.

Generator, Bus-Bar & Battery

ch06_17Original redraw

The basic DC power system. The generator supplies the bus-bar, from which every load is fed in parallel through its own circuit breaker. The battery is also connected to the bus. With the engine running the generator voltage (about 28 V on a 24 V system) is slightly higher than the battery’s, so the generator powers the loads and charges the battery. If the generator fails or the engine is stopped, the battery takes over automatically and supplies the bus for a limited time — so the pilot must reduce the load. The ammeter shows whether the battery is charging or discharging.

Paralleled DC Generators with Load Sharing

ch06_18Original redraw

Sharing the load evenly. When two or more DC generators feed the same bus, small differences in their voltages would make one take most of the load. An equalising (load-sharing) circuit links the regulators: a small resistor (or the series-field interpole winding) in each generator’s negative lead produces a voltage proportional to its load, and an equalising coil in each regulator compares them. The more heavily loaded generator has its field reduced and the lighter one has its field increased until the loads are equal. Each generator is connected to the bus through its own reverse-current cut-out.

Fleming's Left-Hand (Motor) Rule

ch07_01Original redraw

For motors use the left hand. Hold the thumb, first and second fingers of the left hand at right angles. The First finger points along the Field (north to south), the seCond finger along the Current (conventional), and the thuMb then shows the direction of Motion — the force on the conductor. Reverse either the field or the current and the motion reverses; reverse both and it does not change, which is why a series-wound DC motor also runs on AC.

DC Motor — Loop & Multi-Coil Armature

ch07_02Original redraw

How the armature turns. Current flows in along one side of the loop and out along the other, so by the left-hand rule one side is pushed up and the other down: a turning couple. The commutator reverses the current in the loop each half-turn, so the torque always acts in the same rotational direction. With one loop the torque falls to zero twice per revolution; a real armature has many coils in slots round an iron core, so the torque is smooth and the motor will start from any position.

DC Motor Construction

ch07_03Original redraw

Inside a DC machine (a motor and a generator are built the same way). The outer yoke carries the pole pieces wound with field coils. The armature — a laminated iron core with coils in its slots — turns on a shaft in ball and roller bearings. One end of the armature carries the commutator, a ring of copper segments insulated from each other, on which spring-loaded carbon brushes in brush holders bear. A fan on the shaft draws cooling air through. The laminations reduce eddy-current losses in the iron.

Starter Motor with Slow-Start Resistor

ch07_04Original redraw

Limiting the starting surge. A motor standing still has no back-EMF, so at switch-on its current is limited only by the low resistance of its windings — a huge inrush that would pull the bus voltage down and strain the battery, gears and engine. A slow-start resistor in series limits the current for the first few seconds while the engine is turning over; then a time switch or centrifugal switch shorts the resistor out and the motor receives full voltage. As the motor speeds up its back-EMF rises and the current falls naturally.

Series-Wound DC Motor

ch07_05Original redraw

Huge starting torque. The field is in series with the armature, so at start the large current flows through both and the torque is very high (roughly proportional to the current squared). As speed rises the back-EMF reduces the current, the field weakens, and the motor speeds up further — so the speed varies widely with load, and with no load it can run away to destructive speed. Series motors are therefore used where they are always loaded and high starting torque is needed: engine starters, landing-gear and flap actuators, and they are never run off-load.

Shunt-Wound DC Motor

ch07_06Original redraw

Steady speed. The field is connected in parallel (shunt) with the armature, so it has the full supply voltage across it and a constant field strength regardless of load. The motor therefore runs at a nearly constant speed from no load to full load, but its starting torque is modest. Shunt motors are used for loads that need a steady speed — fans, pumps and some instrument drives. A compound motor combines series and shunt fields for good starting torque with limited speed variation.

Starter-Generator

ch07_07Original redraw

One machine, two jobs. Many turboprop and small turbine engines use a starter-generator mounted on the accessory gearbox. For starting, it is connected as a compound or series motor to give high torque and turns the engine; once the engine is self-sustaining, the starter circuit drops out and the machine is switched to run as a shunt-wound generator supplying the bus under the control of its voltage regulator. It saves the weight of a separate starter motor and generator, at the cost of brush wear and a more complex control system.

Reversible Split-Field Actuator Motor

ch07_08Original redraw

Running an actuator both ways. A split-field series motor has two field windings wound in opposite senses. Selecting OPEN energises one field and the motor turns one way; selecting SHUT energises the other and it turns the other way. A limit switch in each field circuit opens when the actuator reaches the end of its travel, stopping the motor and preparing the opposite circuit. An electromagnetic brake, released while the motor is powered, locks the output the moment power is removed so the actuator does not overrun or creep.

Rotary Electric Actuator

ch07_09Original redraw

Turning a valve or flap. A small high-speed reversible motor drives through a reduction gearbox to an output shaft that turns through a limited angle — for example to open or shut a fuel or bleed valve. A clutch (often a friction or magnetic clutch) protects the motor if the output jams. Limit switches, operated by cams on the output shaft, cut the motor at each end of travel, and an electromagnetic brake holds the shaft when the motor stops. A position switch drives a cockpit indicator such as a doll’s eye or flow-bar.

Linear (Screw-Jack) Actuator

ch07_10Original redraw

Turning rotation into a push or pull. A reversible motor drives, through reduction gears, a threaded screw shaft. A nut on the screw is prevented from turning, so as the screw rotates the nut — and the ram fixed to it — moves along: in or out, depending on the direction of rotation. Linear actuators move trim tabs, flaps on light aircraft, and cowl flaps. Limit switches stop the motor at full extension and retraction, a brake prevents creep, and the screw is often self-locking so loads cannot back-drive it.

Magnetic Position Indicators — Doll's Eye & Flow-Bar

ch07_11Original redraw

Simple electrical position repeaters. A doll’s eye (magnetic) indicator has a small rotating disc marked with a bar, turned by an electromagnet: when a valve is open the bar lines up with the flow line printed on the panel; when shut it lies across it; in transit, or with no power, it shows a third position (often cross-hatched). A magnetic indicator (“barber’s pole” or prism type) shows a striped or coloured face when a component is in transit or a system is unpowered. Because they need power to move, they show the last commanded state, not a measured position — check that power is available.

Generator → Bus-Bar → Parallel Loads (Two-Wire)

ch08_01Original redraw

Every load in parallel across the bus. The generator feeds a bus-bar — a heavy conductor that distributes power — and each load is connected between the bus and the return, in parallel, so every load gets the full system voltage and each can be switched or fail without affecting the others. In this two-wire (dipole) system each load has its own return wire back to the negative side. It is used where the structure is non-conducting (composite airframes) or where currents must be kept separate to avoid interference, but it doubles the amount of cable.

Earth-Return (Single-Pole) Distribution

ch08_02Original redraw

The airframe carries the return current. In an earth-return (single-pole) system each load is fed by a single wire from the bus, and its other side is connected — bonded — to the metal airframe, which serves as the return conductor back to the generator and battery negative. This roughly halves the weight of cable, which is why it is standard in metal aircraft. Good bonding of every part is essential, both for the return path and to prevent static build-up, sparks and radio interference. Composite aircraft must provide a return network instead.

Reverse-Current Cut-Out

ch08_03Original redraw

Stopping the battery motoring the generator. When generator voltage is below battery voltage — at low engine speed or on shutdown — current would flow backwards from the battery into the generator, discharging the battery and trying to drive the generator as a motor. The reverse-current cut-out prevents this. A voltage (shunt) coil closes the contacts once the generator voltage exceeds the battery’s; the load current then flows through a current (series) coil that helps hold them closed. If the current reverses, the series coil’s field opposes the shunt coil’s, the contacts spring open, and the generator is disconnected from the bus.

Moving-Coil Meter Movement

ch08_04Original redraw

The basis of most analogue electrical meters. A light coil on a pivot sits in the gap between the curved pole pieces of a permanent magnet, around a fixed soft-iron core that makes the field radial and uniform. Current through the coil produces a torque (motor principle) that turns it against two hairsprings, which also carry the current in and out. The deflection is proportional to the current, so the scale is linear. It responds only to DC; with a series resistor (multiplier) it becomes a voltmeter, and with a shunt it measures large currents.

Left-Zero vs Centre-Zero Ammeter

ch08_05Original redraw

Two ways of showing current. A left-zero ammeter (loadmeter) reads from zero upwards and is fitted in the generator lead to show how much current — or what fraction of rated load — the generator is supplying. A centre-zero ammeter is fitted in the battery lead: a needle to the right (+) shows the battery is charging; to the left (−) shows it is discharging, meaning the generator is not carrying the load. A steady discharge reading in flight is a generator failure warning.

Aircraft Battery Installation

ch08_07Original redraw

Containing the hazards. The aircraft battery sits in a box or compartment that is vented: air enters through one tube and leaves overboard through another, carrying away the hydrogen given off during charging (explosive in air) and any acid or alkali fumes. On lead-acid installations the outlet often passes through a sump jar of neutralising agent. A quick-disconnect plug allows fast removal without loose terminals. The battery must be secured against crash loads, and on Ni-Cd installations a temperature sensor warns of thermal runaway.

Light-Aircraft DC System

ch08_08Original redraw

Putting it together. The battery is connected to the bus through the battery master contactor, a solenoid operated by the master switch. The starter is fed straight from the battery side through its own heavy contactor, operated by the ignition key. The alternator charges the bus through its output circuit breaker; its field is supplied, via the alternator side of the master switch, through the voltage regulator, with an over-voltage relay to trip the field if the regulator fails. Loads are fed from the bus through circuit breakers, and the ammeter shows battery charge/discharge.

Single-Alternator System — Split Master Switch

ch08_09Original redraw

Why the master switch is split. On most light aircraft the master is a split rocker: the BAT half operates the battery contactor; the ALT half supplies the alternator field through the voltage regulator. ALT can be switched off on its own — to isolate a faulty alternator or a runaway over-voltage — but it cannot be switched on without BAT, because an alternator needs battery power to excite its field. An over-voltage protector opens the field circuit automatically if the bus voltage rises too high, and a low-voltage light warns the pilot.
